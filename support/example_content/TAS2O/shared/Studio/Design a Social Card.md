Compress one finished story into a single shareable card: one image,
six words, and alt text that carries the meaning for readers who
never see the picture. This card is the only part of your story most
people will meet — the purest test of [[Audiences and Platforms]]:
what stops the scroll without lying about what waits behind the tap.

> [!warning] Care and consent notes
> **A card travels further than a story** — it gets shared, cropped,
> and seen out of context, so confirm the person in your image gave
> [[Releases and Consent|consent that covers social use]]. **The
> image keeps its credit**, on the card or in the post text —
> sharing does not launder ownership. **The six words must be true**
> — a card that promises more than the story delivers is clickbait,
> and clickbait spends the newsroom's trust to buy one tap. **Alt
> text ships with the card**, every time — access is part of
> publishing, not a favour.

## What you need

- [ ] One finished story from the class site, read all the way through
- [ ] Its strongest image, with credit and consent confirmed
- [ ] Your image editing software, canvas set square before you start
- [ ] Your notebook, for drafting the six words

## The work

1. **Find the story's one claim.** Not its topic — its claim. "The
   team practises at 6 a.m." is a card; "sports update" is a shrug.
2. **Choose the image for small.** One face beats a crowd, one clear
   shape beats a busy scene — whichever candidate survives a squint
   at thumbnail size wins.
3. **Write the six words.** Draft ten versions in your notebook.
   Verbs beat adjectives, specifics beat hype, and the
   [[Headline Help Desk]] tests all apply at this length — sixfold.
4. **Set the type to survive the platform.** Big enough to read at
   thumbnail size, placed where the image is quiet, with contrast
   that holds when the platform dims it.
5. **Write the alt text.** Describe what the image shows and why it
   matters — "runners at dawn practice, frost on the track" gives a
   screen reader user what everyone else gets. No "image of" filler.
6. **Run the honesty check.** Would a reader who tapped through feel
   informed — or baited? [[Misinformation and Verification]] starts
   at home.

## What can go wrong

- **Six words that need the image, an image that needs the words.**
  Cover each half and check the other still means something — cards
  get seen broken, cropped, and text-free.
- **Beautiful at full size, mud at thumbnail.** You designed at the
  wrong zoom. Judge the card at the size it will actually be met.
- **The card oversells.** "You won't believe" energy crept in during
  draft seven. Cut back to the version that is merely true.

## Level up

Make a second card for the same story aimed at a different audience —
parents instead of students — and list what changed: the image, the
six words, or both. That gap is the audience made visible.
