Every task page carries its criteria, published before the work starts.
Most people read them once, at launch, and then hand in a job that
misses two of them they could have fixed in one period. This page is
that period.

We do the first one together, on my work rather than yours, so that the
move is not new to you the day it is your own bed, your own frame, or
your own tray being judged.

## The routine

1. Put the task page beside the actual thing — the object, the bench,
   the file, the plan. Not beside your memory of it.
2. Go down the criteria one at a time. For each, write **yes**, **not
   yet**, or **partly**, and one sentence of evidence: the measurement,
   the photograph, the entry in your log, the thing an inspector would
   see. "Yes" with nothing behind it is a hope.
3. Find your weakest one. Not the easiest to fix — the weakest.
4. Write one sentence saying what you will change, and in which period.
5. Then change it. This is why a self-check is never scheduled on a hand-in
   day: a judgement you have no time to act on is just a worry.

Steps 2 and 3 are the whole exercise. Naming the weakest row honestly is
harder than any of the fixing, because it means writing down the thing
you were hoping nobody would pick up.

> [!example]- One criterion, judged honestly
> From [[The Shop Build]]: *"Squareness and fit — it is square, and the
> joints close."*
>
> **Partly.** Three corners are square. The fourth is out by about four
> millimetres, which I found when the diagonals did not match and wrote
> in my log as "check later". Weakest one.
> **Change:** back off the two screws on that corner, pull it square
> with a clamp and a diagonal measurement, and re-fasten, next period —
> before the finish goes on, because after that it is permanent.
>
> That entry is worth more than "looks good" against all five criteria,
> and it took less time to write.

## Doing it out loud

Read your judgement to the person working next to you, or hand them the
criteria and the job and let them read yours back. On a practical task a
second pair of eyes finds the thing you have stopped seeing after four
periods — the sight line you got used to, the plant you have watered the
same way since Day 1.

What they say is feedback, not a mark. Nothing a classmate writes about
your work, and nothing you write about your own, becomes part of your
percentage — see [[How Marks Work]]. That cuts both ways: an honest
"not yet" cannot cost you anything, which is exactly what makes it
worth writing.

## Where this fits

What it does change is the work, and it is the cheapest mark improvement
available in this course. By the end of the semester you should be
running this without being asked — standing back from a job, comparing
it against what was specified, and fixing the gap before anybody else
sees it. That is what a tradesperson does all day, and it is the actual
point.

Keep your checks in [[The Evidence File]]. Read together at the end of
the year they show change over the semester better than the finished
jobs do, which is why [[The Evidence Portfolio]] asks for them.
