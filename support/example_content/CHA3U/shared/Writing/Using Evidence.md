Evidence is not decoration you add once the paragraph is written. It is the
thing the paragraph exists to weigh. The curriculum asks you to "interpret
and analyse evidence and information … using various tools, strategies, and
approaches appropriate for historical inquiry," and then to "evaluate and
synthesize" it. Interpretation comes before synthesis; quotation is neither.

## What each kind of source can carry

| Source | Strong evidence for | Weak evidence for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Statute or executive order | What was authorised, and in what words | Whether it was enforced, or how it felt |
| Census or statistical series | Scale, distribution, change over time | Motive, experience, or anything uncounted |
| Newspaper | What a public was told, and by whom | What happened on the day it went to press |
| Photograph | Conditions, appearances, presence | Cause, sequence, or intent |
| Testimony | Experience, meaning, what documents hide | Precise dates and totals |
| Later account or textbook | The moment that produced it | The event it describes, on its own authority |

Almost every weak paragraph in this course comes from using a source in the
right-hand column as if it were the left.

## Spending a source inside a paragraph

Four moves, in this order. **Introduce** it: who made it, when, for whom, in
half a sentence. **Quote** the minimum that does the work — a phrase is
usually better than a sentence. **Interpret** it: say what it establishes,
in your words, narrowly. **Weigh** it: say what it does not settle, and what
would settle it. The fourth move is the one that reads as a historian.

## Corroboration and the appearance of it

Two sources that agree are only independent if they were made independently.
A newspaper that reprinted a government statement and the statement itself
are one source with two datelines. Check where each account came from before
you claim they confirm each other.

When sources conflict, do not average them. Ask which was in a position to
know, which had a reason to shade, and whether they are answering the same
question at all. A stated conflict, explained, is stronger writing than a
smooth paragraph that hid it.

## Quantities, quoted honestly

Give the figure, its year, its unit and its source in the same sentence.
Prefer a rate when populations differ. If two published figures disagree,
say so and explain why — [[Statistics and the Census]] sets out the usual
reasons — rather than choosing the one that suits your case.

Then check that every claim is attached to something a reader could find,
which is what [[Citing Historical Sources]] is for, and that the whole thing
is still an argument rather than a survey, which is what
[[Building an Argument]] is for.
