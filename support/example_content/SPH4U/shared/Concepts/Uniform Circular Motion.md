Moving in a circle at constant speed is accelerated motion, because
velocity is a vector and its DIRECTION is changing constantly.

## The acceleration points to the centre

$$a_c = \frac{v^2}{r}$$

Always toward the centre, always perpendicular to the velocity. Which is
why the speed does not change: a force perpendicular to motion changes
direction and nothing else.

## In terms of period and frequency

Since one revolution covers $2\pi r$ in a period $T$ (with frequency $f = 1/T$):

$$v = \frac{2\pi r}{T} = 2\pi r f \qquad \text{so} \qquad a_c = \frac{4\pi^2 r}{T^2} = 4\pi^2 r f^2$$

Which form to use is a question about what you measured. A stopwatch gives
you period $T$; a photogate or tachometer gives you frequency $f$ or speed $v$.
[[The Flying Pig]] uses both, and compares.

## Deriving it

The derivation from similar triangles — the velocity vector difference triangle
is similar to the position displacement triangle — is worth doing once, in
class, because it is where the $v^2$ comes from and why the result contains no
mass. In an inertial frame of reference, this centripetal acceleration requires
a net inward component of force; in a rotating non-inertial frame, the apparent
outward tendency is an artifact of inertia.
