"Women got the vote in 1918" is the version most people carry out of school.
It is wrong in three directions at once: about which women, about which
governments, and about why it happened when it did. Sorting that out is
better history than memorizing a date.

## The wartime acts came first, and they were not about equality

On 20 September 1917 Parliament adopted two statutes together. The *Military
Voters Act* enfranchised serving military personnel, including nurses. The
*Wartime Elections Act* gave the federal vote to the wives, mothers, sisters
and daughters of servicemen — and took the vote away from people born in
enemy countries who had been naturalized after 31 March 1902, and from
conscientious objectors.

Read the two together and the motive is not hard to see. The government was
about to fight an election on conscription. It enfranchised the women most
likely to support it and disenfranchised voters likely to oppose it. Some
suffrage campaigners welcomed this as a wedge. Others were disgusted that the
first federal votes for women were handed out as a tactic, at the expense of
other women. The broader statute followed: the *Act to Confer the Electoral
Franchise upon Women* received royal assent on 24 May 1918, still subject to
the same property qualifications men faced.

The provinces moved on their own schedules, starting on the Prairies in 1916
and ending, decades later, with Quebec in 1940. Holding office was a separate
fight: the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council in London had to rule, on
18 October 1929, that women were "persons" eligible for the Senate.

> [!success]- Who still could not vote federally after 1918 (click to expand)
> Elections Canada's own history is blunt. Racial disqualifications stayed in
> the federal franchise until 1948, when Parliament deleted them — which is
> when Chinese, South Asian and Japanese Canadians gained the federal vote.
> Inuit had the vote restored without qualification in 1950. First Nations
> people could vote only by surrendering their status until 10 March 1960,
> when the House of Commons voted to remove that condition. A First Nations
> woman in 1918 gained nothing at all from a law usually described as giving
> women the vote.

## What to take from it

The suffrage movement was real, organized and effective — and its leading
figures were not uniformly admirable. Several prominent campaigners argued
for votes for women on the grounds that respectable British-descended women
would outvote immigrants, and some actively supported eugenics. Both things
are true, and a page reporting only one of them is doing publicity rather
than history.
