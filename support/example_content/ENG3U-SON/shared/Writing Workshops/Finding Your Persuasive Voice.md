Voice is the thing that makes a piece of writing sound like one specific
person made every choice in it, on purpose. It's not decoration added
after the ideas are settled — it's part of how the ideas get to be
persuasive in the first place.

## What voice sounds like when it's working

Read a paragraph from [[Funny, You Don't Look Like One]] and notice how
much of its persuasive force comes from tone, not just argument. Drew
Hayden Taylor makes serious points about identity and stereotype, and he
routinely makes them through humour and a direct, first-person address to
the reader rather than through a formal, distanced argument. The humour
isn't a break from the persuasion — it's the delivery mechanism. A reader
who's laughing is a reader who's still listening.

## Neutral versus voiced

> [!fail] Neutral
> Many people have incorrect assumptions about Indigenous people based on
> stereotypes. These assumptions are often wrong and can be harmful.

> [!success] Voiced
> Ask most people to picture an Indigenous person and you'll get the same
> handful of images, none of which have met an actual Indigenous person
> in decades. I'd find it funnier if I didn't have to keep explaining
> that I don't own a horse.

The second version makes essentially the same point as the first, but it
commits to a specific speaker with a specific way of talking — direct
address ("you'll get"), a concrete image, a wry aside. That commitment is
what makes it persuasive rather than just informative.

## Building your own

Three moves, in order:

1. **Choose your stance toward the reader.** Are you inviting them in as
   an ally, needling them, or speaking as if they already agree with you
   and you're just naming what they know? Taylor mostly does the third —
   assumes a shared understanding, then lets the gap between that
   assumption and reality do the persuading.
2. **Let your sentence rhythm carry attitude.** A short, blunt sentence
   after a longer one can land as dry humour or as a punch — the timing is
   part of the voice, not separate from it.
3. **Check what your own choices reveal.** Read back what you've written
   and ask what it shows about what you actually believe, not just what
   you stated — a sentence can claim neutrality and still reveal a bias in
   which examples it reaches for first, or which ones it leaves out.

## Voice still needs precision

A distinctive voice is not permission to be vague — the descriptive,
evocative word choice that makes writing vivid still has to be the *most
accurate* word available, not just the most colourful one. Persuasive
writing that sounds lively but says nothing specific persuades nobody.

This is the craft study behind [[Your Canada, Your Story]] — the essay
half of that task asks you to argue a position in a voice that's
recognizably yours, not a voice borrowed from a formal template.
