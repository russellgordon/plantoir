A history essay is not a report on a topic. It is a claim that somebody
could reasonably deny, defended with evidence, in an order a reader can
follow. The curriculum asks you to "evaluate and synthesize" your findings
in order to "formulate conclusions and/or make informed judgements or
predictions." Evaluate, then synthesise — two jobs, and most weak work has
skipped the first.

## A thesis is a claim, not a subject

Test yours by writing its opposite. If the opposite is absurd, you have a
topic sentence pretending to be a thesis.

> The American Revolution was a controversial event that changed the
> colonies in many ways.
>
> The measures Parliament passed between 1764 and 1774 did not create
> colonial opposition so much as convert an existing constitutional
> objection into an organised one, which is why the Revolution should be
> explained by the sequence of responses rather than by the grievance
> itself.

The first cannot be denied by anyone. The second can — and a reader who
disagrees now knows exactly where to attack, which is the courtesy an
argument owes.

## Say your criteria before you judge

Almost every question in this course is a judgement: most significant,
justified, a turning point, inevitable, successful. A judgement without
stated criteria is a preference. Put the criteria in the introduction: *I am
treating significance as depth of impact, durability, and reach beyond the
group directly involved.* Now the reader can dispute your standard rather
than your taste.

## Structure that survives contact with evidence

Open with the claim and the criteria. Give each body paragraph one reason,
not one source — sources are what you spend inside a paragraph, not what
organises the essay. Deal with the strongest evidence against you in the
body, where it can be answered, never in a final apologetic sentence. Close
by saying what your conclusion is bounded by; a limit stated is a strength,
and it is what separates a judgement from an opinion.

## Then choose the form deliberately

The curriculum expects you to communicate "using various formats and styles,
as appropriate for the audience and purpose" — its own examples for this
course include a seminar on the labour movement, an essay on westward
expansion, and a debate on whether the United States has lived up to the
ideas expressed in the Declaration of Independence. The argument does not
change between those forms; the ordering, the tone, and the amount of
context do. Decide who is receiving this, and what they already know, before
you decide how to open.

Get the evidence right in [[Using Evidence]], the vocabulary right in
[[The Words Historians Use]], and the acknowledgements right in
[[Citing Historical Sources]]. Then write one for real in
[[The Source Study]].
