> [!abstract] At a glance
> **Format:** a working motor plus a five-page report. **Built in twos;
> the report is yours, and so is the question you answer at the
> presentation.** **This is the culminating task for the course**, and
> with the [[Final Examination]] it makes up the final thirty per cent of
> your mark — see [[How Marks Work]]. Due in the last week.
> **Assessed on:** all four categories.

Build the motor from [[Building a Simple Motor]], then take it seriously:
measure it, improve it, and explain it — including the moment when you run
it backwards and it becomes a generator.

## Part 1 — The build, measured

- Your motor, working, with a diagram showing current, field, and force.
- Its speed in revolutions per second, measured three ways or three times.
- **The energy it uses.** Measure the potential difference across it and
  the current through it while it runs, and time one run. Potential
  difference times current is the power; power times time is the energy
  it consumed. All three are measured at the bench — none of them can be
  reconstructed afterwards.
- **Where that energy went.** Your motor lifts nothing, so none of it
  leaves as useful work. Account for it anyway: name where it ended up
  and rank your candidates by size, with a reason for the ranking. A
  motor whose entire output is heat, noise and moved air is not a broken
  motor — saying so, with a number for the energy involved, is the
  physics.

## Part 2 — One improvement, tested

Change one variable — turns in the coil, magnet strength, current — and
measure the effect on speed. Predict first. Report the prediction whether
or not it survived.

## Part 3 — The reverse

Disconnect the battery, connect a sensitive meter, and spin the coil by
hand. Record what you see. Explain it with
[[Electromagnetic Induction]], and connect it to how the electricity in
this room was generated — see [[Where Our Electricity Comes From]].

## Part 4 — The argument

Two pages: where this physics shows up in Ontario's grid, what it costs,
and what you think should change. Use numbers from the course. An opinion
without a number in it is not a physics argument.

One of those numbers is a transformer. Your coil makes electricity at a
few volts; the line on the road outside carries it at several thousand,
and something brings it back down again before it reaches this building.
Work out the turns ratio each of those two transformers needs, say which
is the step-up and which the step-down, and explain in three sentences
what the grid buys by doing it — in terms of the current in the wire,
not in terms of the word "efficiency".

## Who does what

Two people build one motor, because a coil that has to be held while it
is soldered needs two pairs of hands, and because the arguing is where
the physics happens. The measurements from Parts 1 to 3 are shared.

The report is not. You write your own, you hand in your own, and at the
presentation you each answer one question of mine on your own. Part 4 in
particular has to be yours: two identical arguments about Ontario's grid
are two people who have each done half the reading.

## How it is marked

| Category | What I am looking for |
| --- | --- |
| Knowledge | The diagram shows current, field and force with their directions consistent, and the right-hand rule you used is named |
| Knowledge | The two turns ratios are calculated, the step-up and the step-down are named the right way round, and the reason the grid transforms at all is given in terms of the current in the wire |
| Thinking | The energy your motor consumed is computed from a measured potential difference, a measured current and a timed run — and the account of where it went RANKS its candidates, with a reason, instead of listing them |
| Thinking | The improvement reports a prediction made before the measurement, and the result whether or not it survived |
| Application | Part 3 explains YOUR generator, not generators in general, and connects it to how the electricity in the room was made |
| Communication | Part 4's argument carries numbers from this course, cited, and a reader could check any of them |
| Communication | Your answer at the presentation explains one part of your own device to somebody who has not seen it, without reading from the report |

> [!note] For teachers: why there is no efficiency figure here
> The coil-in-cradles motor from [[Building a Simple Motor]] is unloaded
> and has almost no torque, so there is no mechanical work to measure and
> an "efficiency" would be a number divided by nothing. Part 1 therefore
> asks for the energy IN and an argument about where it goes, which the
> apparatus can actually support. If your motors are strong enough to
> wind a thread and lift a few grams through a measured height, add that
> timed run and you get a real joule out — but check it lifts before you
> promise it.

> [!note] Why this is the culminating task
> It is the only piece of work all year that asks you to build a device,
> measure it, improve it on evidence, and then argue about what the same
> physics does at the scale of a province — four periods, after two spent
> getting the motor to turn at all — forces on a current-carrying
> wire, energy conversion and efficiency, the field concept, and a
> societal argument that only works if the numbers are right. If you can
> do this, you can do the course.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 5, Day 10, the first half of the working period, before
the rehearsal starts
  Watch for: whether the ORIGINAL configuration goes back on the bench
  and gets measured again after the change has been made. It is the only
  way to know that a faster motor is the extra turns rather than a
  battery that has warmed up or a bearing that has worn in — and Part 2
  reports a prediction and a result whichever way it was done, so the
  report cannot tell you. This is the whole of controlling a variable, in
  a device that changes under you while you work.
  Going well: the coil is rewound, measured, then unwound back to what it
  was and measured a second time, and the two baseline numbers get
  compared out loud.
  Stuck: one reading before, one reading after, a battery that has been
  in the circuit for forty minutes, and a confident percentage.
  Record: one column on the class list — did the baseline get taken
  again, yes or no.

TALK — Unit 5, Day 9, at the conference already on that agenda
  Ask, before they touch it: "Spin your coil the other way. Which way
  will the needle go, and what tells you?" Then let them try it.
  Then: "When you spin the coil by hand the meter moves. Where did that
  energy come from, and what did it cost you?"
  The first question is the whole of F2.7 — the DIRECTION of the induced
  current, worked out from Lenz's law and the right-hand rule rather than
  read off afterwards — and a report shows you the direction they ended
  up with, never whether they could have said it first. The second is
  F2.7's other named tool, conservation of energy: a strong answer puts
  the hand in the account and notices the resistance, so spinning gets
  harder and the electricity is bought from the person turning it. That
  is felt rather than recited, and a written report can reproduce the
  law's words without the student ever having noticed it in their
  fingers.
  Record: two ticks per student on the conference sheet, and a note of
  anyone whose prediction was right for the wrong reason.

The product evidence is the motor and the report, presented and handed in
on Day 11.
