A test mark tells you where you stood on one morning. Your journal shows
what changed across a semester, and you are the only person who can
build it.

## What it is for

This course asks you to design investigations and then to defend the
numbers they produce. Most of the interesting work happens **before** you
touch any equipment — choosing what to measure, deciding what to hold
constant, committing to a prediction — and most of the rest happens in
the twenty minutes afterwards, when you are deciding what your figures
are allowed to mean.

None of that survives in a data table. It survives here or nowhere.

## What goes in

| Goes in | Does not go in |
| --- | --- |
| Predictions, written before you measured, with a reason | Marks and grades |
| The raw readings, with their units | Only the final calculated answer |
| Which instrument gave each reading, and how finely it reads | Copies of the concept pages |
| Reasoning you later found out was wrong | A summary of what we covered |
| A number that surprised you, and what you did next | Everything you handed in |
| Questions you still cannot answer | Anything you wrote to impress me |

The left column is what a reader learns something from. The right column
already exists somewhere else.

## Raw readings, not just answers

This is the entry habit that separates a useful journal from a decorative
one, and it takes ten extra seconds.

Write down the numbers the instruments actually gave you, before any
arithmetic. If your entry says only *"$n = 0.0421$ mol"*, and you later
discover you used the wrong molar mass, the entry is dead — there is
nothing left to recompute from. If it says *"2.46 g on the four-figure
balance in the fume hood; molar mass taken as 58.44 g/mol"*, you can fix
the error in one line, three weeks later, and the record is still honest
about what you did at the time.

The same applies to a value you looked up. Write down where you got it
and under what conditions it applies, the way
[[Reading a Data Table]] describes. A number with no provenance cannot
be checked, which means it cannot be defended.

## The habit that makes it work

Write the entry the day of the work. The details you will want at the
end of the course — what you tried first, which reading you doubted at
the time, the moment your group changed the plan — are gone within a
week and no amount of effort in the final month brings them back. Ten
minutes on the day beats an hour in the last week of the semester.

## Rhythm

- After every investigation, one entry.
- After every discussion, two or three sentences on what moved.
- Twice, once in each of Units 3 and 4, a [[Showing Growth]] comparison
  written in class.
- At the end, [[Final Reflection]].

> [!important] Predictions get written down before the measurement
> Not after. A prediction you record afterwards is not a prediction, and
> your memory of what you "basically expected" is generous to you in a
> way you will not notice.
>
> Grade 11 adds one requirement to that. Where you can, make the
> prediction a **number with a direction**: not "some product will be
> lost", but "we will recover less than the calculated mass, because
> some precipitate will stay on the filter paper". A prediction shaped
> like that can be checked against your own data, and being wrong about
> it teaches you something specific.
