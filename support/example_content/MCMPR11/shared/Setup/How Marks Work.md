Marks in this course worry people for one wrong reason: the belief that some people are 
"programmers" and the rest are here to watch. No such split exists. Reading code, writing 
code, and building something a person can use are learned skills. You are marked on process, 
growth, and what your work does — all of it inside your control.

## Where the evidence comes from

Evidence of what you can do is gathered three ways, and all three count.

**Product — what you hand in.** [[Task 1 - Pacific Trail Route Planner]], 
[[Task 2 - Salish Sea Marine Sensor Tracker]], 
[[Task 3 - Indigenous Language Lexicon Engine]], 
[[Task 4 - Wildfire Early Warning Dashboard]] and the 
[[Final Evaluation - Software Portfolio and Technical Challenge]] are the programs and 
writing you submit. This is the evidence everyone expects, and the easiest to see.

**Observation — what you are seen doing while you build.** Testing a branch the moment you 
write it, tracing a loop on paper instead of guessing, handing the keyboard over when the 
pair timer goes, opening the documentation before asking. None of that survives into the 
finished file, so it is watched for while it is happening — in work periods, milestone 
checks, and labs.

**Conversation — what you can explain out loud.** At a milestone check, a peer code review, 
or a one-on-one you will be asked why your program does what it does. A program you can 
explain is worth more than a program that merely runs, because the explaining is the part 
that carries to the next problem.

> [!important] The most recent evidence is the evidence that counts
> Where the three sources disagree, the most recent and most consistent picture wins. A 
> shaky first attempt that you later fix, explain and defend is not averaged against the 
> finished version — it is replaced by it.

## What is actually being judged

British Columbia's curriculum describes what you should be able to **do**, and calls those 
the Curricular Competencies. In the words you would actually use, they come to this:

| What you are getting better at | What it looks like in this class |
| --- | --- |
| Working out what the problem really is, and who it is for | You can say who your program is for and what would count as solving their problem, before you write a line of it. |
| Designing before typing | Flowcharts, function names and contracts, a sketch of the data — a plan somebody else could read. |
| Writing Python that runs | Loops, conditions, dictionaries and functions that do what you meant, and an error message read rather than groaned at. |
| Testing on purpose | Test cases you chose deliberately, including the awkward ones — empty input, a negative number, the boundary exactly on the line. |
| Acting on feedback | You asked for a specific kind of feedback, got it, and changed something because of it. |
| Handing it over | Code and documentation another person can run and maintain without you sitting beside them. |
| Judging what the technology does to people | You can name who your software could harm, and what you did about it. |

Every task page turns these into success criteria for that particular project, in the same 
plain words, before the work starts.

**Check-ins and code tracing** are short, diagnostic, and low-stakes. They exist to find out 
what needs work before a project deadline, not to close the book on it.

**Reflection is yours, and it is reported rather than scored.** Your [[Learning Journey Log]] 
is where a broken build turns into an account of what you now understand, and your 
self-assessment against [[The Core Competencies]] at the end of each unit is written in your 
own words. Neither is a mark your teacher borrows from you; both are part of what gets 
reported about your learning, authored by you.

> [!important] Small and used beats ambitious and broken
> On every task, and most of all on the culminating project, a modest program that works 
> scores higher than a grand design that does not run. This is not a consolation prize for 
> low ambition — judging scope accurately is one of the hardest skills in software.

> [!important] Broken programs are never penalised as broken
> Version one of everything crashes. The mark follows where your work ends up and how 
> visibly you got there — your journal and your comments are how "visibly" happens.

Each task page lists its success criteria before you start. If a mark ever surprises you, 
ask: those criteria are the whole story.
