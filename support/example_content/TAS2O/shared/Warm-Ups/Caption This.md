One photograph on the screen, sixty seconds on the clock, and everyone
writes the two-line caption: who, what, when. A caption is the
smallest complete piece of journalism there is — everything a reader
needs, in the space a reader will actually give you.

## How to run it

1. Show the photo plus honest facts — names, score, date. Never guess.
2. One quiet minute. Everyone writes; nobody drafts out loud.
3. Hear four or five. Ask what a reader now knows that the photo did not.
4. Crown the sharpest caption and name exactly why it won.

> [!example]- Three captions, flat to publishable (click to expand)
> The photo: a player mid-air at the net, arms up, gym behind her.
> 1. *A student plays volleyball.* — True, and useless: the photo
>    already says this.
> 2. *A senior blocks at Tuesday's home game.* — What and when; no who.
> 3. *Grade 11 middle Sam Okafor stuffs match point in Tuesday's
>    semifinal win — the school's first finals berth in six
>    years.* — Who, what, when, and why a reader cares.

## One variation

Run it in reverse: a strong caption, no photo, and the room describes
the photograph that must sit above it — obvious, once the picture is gone.

> [!tip] Facts first, flourish never
> If the fact sheet beside the photo does not say it, your caption
> cannot say it either — the reflex that [[Caption Practice]] builds
> and [[Making Photographs]] depends on at publication speed.
