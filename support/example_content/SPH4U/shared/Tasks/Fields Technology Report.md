> [!abstract] At a glance
> **Format:** four pages. **Individually.** Due at the end of Unit 3.
> **Assessed on:** Knowledge, Application, Communication.

Take one technology that works because of a field, and explain it to the
standard of someone who could rebuild it.

## Choose one

Mass spectrometer · MRI scanner · particle accelerator · electrostatic
precipitator · maglev train · GPS constellation · Van de Graaff generator ·
electron microscope · ion thruster.

## What to produce

1. **The physics**, with the field equation that governs the device and a
   diagram showing the field and the force.
2. **A calculation** with real numbers you cite — the radius of a proton's
   path in an MRI's field, the orbital period of a GPS satellite, the
   voltage needed to deflect an ink droplet.
3. **The engineering trade-off**: what gets harder as you scale the device
   up, and why.
4. **The societal question**: who benefits, who pays, and what the
   environmental cost is.

Part 4 is not decoration; it is a third of the mark, and it is the
Ministry's reason this strand exists.

## How this is assessed

This one is yours from end to end — the research, the calculation and the
writing. Read it against these rows before you hand it in, and use
[[Judging Your Own Work]] on the weakest one.

| Quality | What it looks like in your report |
| --- | --- |
| The right field equation, and it is used | The governing equation stated with every symbol identified, and then applied to your device rather than left sitting beside it |
| A diagram a reader can follow | The field drawn, the force on the charge or the mass marked, and the geometry that makes the device work visible in the picture |
| Real numbers, from somewhere you name | Values taken from a source you cite, carried through with units, and an answer whose size you say something about |
| The trade-off is specific | One thing that gets harder as the device is scaled up, tied to a quantity that appears in your own equation |
| The societal question is answered, not gestured at | Who benefits, who pays, and what the environmental cost is — each with evidence behind it rather than an impression |
| Sources somebody could check | An accepted form of documentation, and at least one source that is not a summary site |

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

This is an individual research task, so most of its working periods are
quiet desk work and no use to you for watching. The two slots worth
using are the discussion, where the research meets other people, and the
conference, where you can ask about a device rather than read about it.

OBSERVE — Unit 3, Day 14, during the discussion on that agenda
  Watch for: what a student brings to the table. A figure with a source
  behind it, or a position. Then watch the harder thing — whether
  anybody's position moves when somebody else's figure lands on it. A
  four-page report presents a settled view and cannot show you whether
  it was ever unsettled.
  Going well: "I thought that too, until I found…", said out loud, in
  front of people.
  Stuck: research produced only where it agrees with the opinion the
  student walked in with.
  Record: two ticks against the class list — brought a figure, changed a
  position. The second tick is rare and it is worth writing the sentence
  down when it happens.

TALK — Unit 3, Day 13, at the conference already on that agenda
  The agenda's own question about sources is already printed, so it will
  be answered before you sit down. Go to the argument instead.
  Ask: "Your fourth section says who pays for this. Take the other side
  for thirty seconds — what is the strongest case that the benefit is
  worth that cost?"
  Then: "Your calculation gave you a number. If it came out ten times
  bigger, what would have to be different about the machine?"
  A strong first answer can build the opposing case out of something
  real, and usually improves the section it was arguing against; a weak
  one restates its own position more firmly, which tells you the section
  was written to a conclusion. That is D1.2 heard — impact assessed
  rather than asserted, which is what "assess" asks for. A strong second
  answer connects a magnitude to an engineering consequence: a bigger
  radius wants a bigger magnet, a bigger chamber, a bigger room, a bigger
  bill. That is D1.1, the operation of the system analysed — and a report
  can describe an operation perfectly while its author has only
  reproduced somebody else's description of it.
  Record: one line each on the conference list, and a mark against
  anyone who could not build the other case. Those reports are summaries.

The product evidence is the four pages, in on Day 15.
