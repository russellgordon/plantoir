## What you are making

A rollercoaster's height profile, seen from the side, is a curve with
a job. Yours is a polynomial $h(x)$ — height in metres above the
ground, $x$ metres along the track — and it must meet four
constraints:

- the ride begins and ends at platform height, $10$ m up;
- it dips below ground into a tunnel exactly once, so the profile
  crosses ground level exactly twice;
- each ground crossing is *gentle* — the profile visibly flattens as
  it crosses, which is something multiplicity can buy you;
- the highest point clears $25$ m.

You finish with the **equation in factored form**, a labelled graph,
a paragraph per constraint pointing at the exact algebra that
enforces it, and two solved questions about your own design: *where
exactly is the ride $15$ m high?* — a polynomial equation — and
*over which stretch is it underground?* — a polynomial inequality.

## Milestones

- [ ] Profile sketched by hand with every constraint marked, before
      any equation exists
- [ ] Zeros chosen, with multiplicities that make the tunnel
      crossings gentle — [[Zeros and Multiplicity]] is the lever
- [ ] Leading coefficient tuned in [[Using Desmos]] until the peak
      clears $25$ m and both ends sit at platform height
- [ ] The $15$ m question solved algebraically — [[The Factor Theorem]]
      earns its keep here — then confirmed on the graph
- [ ] The underground stretch stated as an interval and defended with
      a sign argument, using test values on either side of each crossing

## How it is assessed

You work in a pair and you are marked as an individual. Put your
initials beside each constraint paragraph you wrote, write your own
milestone journal entry on the due date, and answer for your own work
at the boards — those three are what carries your mark.

Per [[How Marks Work]], the reasoning is the product: a profile that
misses one constraint, with the miss measured and the fix argued,
outranks a perfect curve that cannot say why it works. On the due
date your pair defends the design at the boards. Ten minutes of that
class are set aside for your milestone journal entry on the constraint
that fought back hardest — written there, in the room, and belonging to
this task rather than to your [[Math Journal]].

Before any of that, run [[Judging Your Own Work]] against the table
below, in the ten minutes set aside on day 5 — while there is still
time in that period to act on what it finds.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like in your work |
| --- | --- |
| Picture first | A hand sketch with constraints marked, before algebra |
| Algebra that enforces | Each constraint traced to a factor or coefficient |
| Gentle by design | Multiplicity chosen on purpose, and explained |
| Exact where it counts | The $15$ m crossings solved, not read off |
| An honest interval | The tunnel stretch defended with a sign argument and test values |
| A miss you measured | Any constraint not perfectly met, measured rather than glossed, with the fix argued |
| An entry that names the fight | The milestone entry names the constraint that resisted, what you tried, and the algebra that finally enforced it |

> [!success]- If the ends will not both sit at $10$ m
> Symmetry is free money: place your tunnel crossings symmetrically
> about the middle of the track and build from factors that mirror
> each other — whatever the left end does, the right end must copy.
> Then remember that your zeros define a whole *family* of coasters;
> one known point pins down the member, and the leading coefficient
> falls out.

Triangulation — the evidence that never arrives on its own.

Pairs, so ask one student at a time and let the other keep working. Day 13
already has a conference on it; the questions below are deliberately not the
one printed on that agenda.

OBSERVE — Unit 1, Day 14, the working period, once the modelled self-check
is over and the pairs are building

  Watch for HOW a pair tests. A profile has four constraints, and there are
  two ways to check them: take one constraint at a time, evaluate or sketch
  for that constraint specifically, record a verdict, move on — or build the
  whole function, look at the graph, and decide it looks about right.

  The hand-in cannot tell you which happened. The criteria row "Algebra that
  enforces" asks for each constraint traced to a factor or coefficient, and a
  pair who fiddled the leading coefficient until the picture behaved can
  write that mapping afterwards just as convincingly as a pair who derived
  it. Watching is what separates the two, and it corroborates a row the
  product only half-reports.

  Going well: one partner reading a constraint aloud while the other tests
  that constraint alone, and a tick going onto a list of four.

  Stuck: both partners at one screen, dragging the leading coefficient and
  rereading the whole list of constraints after every drag.

  Record: three columns on your day plan — pair, a tick for "tests one at a
  time", and which constraint they were on at half-time. The pairs with no
  tick by half-time are the ones to reach first.

TALK — Unit 1, Day 13, at the conference already on that agenda

  Ask: "Suppose I raise the finishing platform to twelve metres and change
  nothing else about what the ride has to do. What in your equation moves,
  and what happens to your tunnel?"

  Then: "Two other pairs have exactly your four constraints and none of their
  rides look like yours. What is it about the constraints that leaves that
  much room — and what would I have to add to the list to force everyone's
  ride to be the same?"

  On the first, a strong answer separates the two repairs and says they are
  not the same ride. Adding two metres is a vertical shift: every ground
  crossing moves inward, the tunnel gets shorter and shallower and can vanish
  altogether, and the peak clears twenty-seven metres instead of
  twenty-five. Moving the zeros instead keeps the tunnel and rebuilds the
  ends. A student who says only "add two" has not noticed that the zeros are
  where the curve meets ground level, not platform level.

  On the second, the strong answer is that the constraints fix very little:
  they demand two ground crossings but not where; they force odd multiplicity
  of at least three at each crossing, because a squared factor would bounce
  off the ground rather than pass through it — a tunnel entrance you could
  not drive into; and they leave the leading coefficient free until some
  point is pinned down. One extra condition — "the ride is exactly eighteen
  metres high forty metres along" — collapses the family to one member. That
  is C1.7 and C1.8 heard rather than read: the page shows one member, and only
  the conversation says whether the student knows it was chosen out of a
  family.

  Record: one line per student on your class list. The pair's name is no use
  to you here.

The product evidence is the profile defended on Day 16 and the milestone
entry written in class that day.
