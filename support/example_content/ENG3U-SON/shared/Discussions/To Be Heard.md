Today the text stays closed for the first part of class. Before you look at
a single printed word of Hamlet's first soliloquy — "O, that this too too
solid flesh would melt" — you're going to listen to it performed aloud,
more than once, and talk about what you heard before you talk about what
you'd have read.

## What close listening asks of the room

This isn't a reading circle. Nobody is scanning ahead, and nobody is
formulating their comment while someone else is still talking — the room
stays quiet during each round, the way it would for a piece of music. You
get three passes: once with your eyes closed, once following along on the
page, and once more, listening specifically for whatever the group decides
is still unsettled after the first two.

Before each round, listen for:

- [ ] Where the reader speeds up, and where they slow down
- [ ] A pause that runs longer than you expected
- [ ] A word the reader leans on harder than the words around it
- [ ] Where the voice seems to change what it's feeling

## The questions

1. On the first listen, before you'd seen a single word, what feeling did
   you catch first — before you could have named a single line it came
   from?
2. Hamlet's voice moves between exhaustion, disgust, and something close to
   tears within these thirty-odd lines. Where did you hear the voice
   change, and how did the reader signal it — speed, pitch, volume,
   silence?
3. This performer stretched the pause after "an unweeded garden." What do
   you think that pause was doing? Would you have put a pause somewhere
   else instead?
4. What did hearing this soliloquy aloud give you that reading it silently,
   first, would not have?

A strong answer to question 2 names an actual line where the shift happens —
not "he gets sadder" in general, but the specific moment the performer's
pace or volume changes and what that change makes you hear in the line that
follows it. [[Soliloquy and Dramatic Irony]] gave you the vocabulary for
what a soliloquy is doing structurally; this is where you practise hearing
it do that work.
