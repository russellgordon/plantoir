In [[Factors That Change a Rate]] your team took one variable and the
class pooled the rest. That was a good afternoon and it was not an
investigation — you ran somebody else's design and reported a direction.

This time the whole thing is yours: the reaction, the variable, the
range, the number of trials, the way you extract a rate from what you
measured, and the number at the end. And the number at the end is not
"it went faster". It is a **ratio, with an uncertainty**.

## The situation

Somewhere, right now, somebody is being paid to make a reaction go
faster — or slower. A process engineer deciding whether a catalyst is
worth its cost. A food scientist working out what temperature stops
something spoiling. A water treatment operator sizing a contact tank so
that the reaction finishes before the water leaves it. A conservator
working out what will slow the corrosion of an object that has to last.

In every case the useful answer is quantitative. *Warmer is faster* buys
nothing. *Ten degrees roughly doubles it, so the tank can be half the
size if we heat the inlet* is a decision.

You are going to produce one answer of that kind, at bench scale, and
then find out what the same question looks like where the arithmetic has
consequences.

## Who does what

You design and run this in **pairs**, because four values of a variable
in triplicate is more than one pair of hands can time well, and because
an argument about what to hold constant is worth having out loud.

**The report is yours alone from the analysis onward.** One design
contract and one raw data table come from the bench and may be
identical; the graph you chose, the result you extracted, the
uncertainty you ranked, the conclusion you justified and the whole of
Part 2 are written by you and marked as yours.

The design is still marked individually, and the conference in the
working period is where that happens: I ask each of you separately why
that range, why that many trials, and how you are getting an initial
rate rather than an average. Signing a contract your partner wrote is
audible in about fifteen seconds, so it is worth building it together
rather than dividing it up.

## Part 1 — Design and run the investigation

**Choose your reaction** from those we have used, or propose another and
bring it early:

| Reaction | How rate is measured | Suits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Magnesium + dilute hydrochloric acid | Time to consume the ribbon, or gas volume against time | Concentration, temperature |
| Marble chips + dilute hydrochloric acid | Mass loss against time | Surface area, concentration |
| Hydrogen peroxide decomposition | Oxygen volume against time | Catalyst, temperature, concentration |

**Choose one independent variable** and take it seriously. At least
**four** values, spread widely enough that the effect is larger than
your scatter — and you should be able to say, before you start, roughly
how large you expect the effect to be, which is what tells you how wide
the spread needs to be.

### The design contract, submitted and approved before you run anything

- [ ] **The balanced equation, with states.**
- [ ] **The independent variable and its four or more values**, with the
      reason for that range.
- [ ] **The dependent variable, as a quantity with a unit.** "How fast
      it went" is not a variable.
- [ ] **How you will obtain an *initial* rate** rather than an average
      over the whole run. This is the design decision that most separates
      a level 4 from a level 3, and there is more than one defensible
      way to do it.
- [ ] **Every controlled variable, with two things beside each**: how
      you will hold it constant, and **which way** a failure would push
      your result. There are more than you think — volume, mass,
      container shape, swirling, room temperature, the age of the acid,
      the surface condition of the metal.
- [ ] **Number of trials at each value**, and the agreement you will
      accept before moving on.
- [ ] **Your predicted relationship**, sketched as a graph with labelled
      axes, **and a predicted ratio with a number in it.**
- [ ] **What you will do about the reaction heating itself**, since both
      acid reactions are exothermic.
- [ ] **The hazards, and your response to each one.**

I will send some of these back for a redesign. That is the system
working rather than a judgement of you.

> [!danger] Hydrogen, hot water, peroxide, and the reaction left off the list
> - **Magnesium in acid produces hydrogen, which is flammable and forms
>   an explosive mixture with air.** No flames anywhere in the room, and
>   nothing that sparks. The gas is invisible and it collects under
>   anything you put over the flask.
> - **Nothing that makes a gas is ever sealed.** Not a stoppered flask,
>   not a syringe with the plunger held, not a bottle with a cap resting
>   on it. Both acid reactions here make gas quickly, and a sealed rigid
>   container is a pressure vessel you built by accident.
> - **If your design needs a lower concentration, add acid to water,
>   never water to acid.** Dilution releases heat; with the water
>   already there it spreads through a large volume, and the other way
>   round it concentrates in a few drops that can boil and spit acid
>   back at you. Dilutions are done at the bench with the container on
>   the bench, before you set up.
> - **Temperature work uses water baths, never flames.** Water at 60 °C
>   scalds, and **hot glassware looks exactly like cold glassware** —
>   assume anything near a bath is hot until you have watched it cool.
> - **Hydrogen peroxide bleaches skin, hair and clothing** and is an
>   oxidiser. Keep it away from anything combustible, including paper
>   towel, and rinse contact immediately and at length.
> - **Manganese(IV) oxide is a fine powder and is harmful if inhaled.**
>   Add it with a spatula, do not tip it from a height, close the
>   container, wash your hands.
> - **Eye protection from the first move to the last of the cleanup.**
>   Hair back, sleeves secured, closed-toe shoes.
> - **Waft, never sniff. Nothing is tasted. Nothing goes back into a
>   stock bottle.** Check the pH of your waste and follow the disposal
>   route I give you.
> - **School reagents only, at the concentrations supplied**, and
>   **combine only what your approved procedure lists.** Substances that
>   are harmless apart can produce a toxic gas together and there is no
>   way to tell by looking.
> - **The thiosulfate "disappearing cross" clock is not available**, and
>   the reason is worth knowing: it releases **sulfur dioxide**, a
>   respiratory irritant that is genuinely dangerous for anyone with
>   asthma, in a room where I cannot know everyone's history. Good
>   experiment; belongs in a fume hood, not on twelve open benches.
> - **Know where the eyewash, shower, extinguisher, blanket, and spill
>   kit are before you start**, and **report every incident
>   immediately**, however small.

## Part 2 — Where the rate has consequences

Research **one** process where the conditions are chosen deliberately to
control a rate, and where that choice matters environmentally. Not a
survey — one process, in depth.

Directions that work:

- **An industrial synthesis** where temperature, pressure, and a
  catalyst are all traded off against each other. The synthesis of
  ammonia is the classic, and it is classic because the trade-off is
  genuinely awkward: the conditions that make the reaction fast are not
  the conditions that make the yield high, and the operating point is a
  compromise nobody is entirely happy with.
- **A catalytic converter or another emission control**, where a
  catalyst makes a reaction fast enough to finish inside a pipe.
- **An enzyme-driven process** in wastewater treatment, brewing, or
  food production, where the rate is controlled by temperature and pH
  rather than by pressure.
- **Preservation and spoilage**, where the whole technology — chilling,
  freezing, drying, packaging — exists to make reactions slower.
- **Corrosion control**, where the goal is to slow a reaction that is
  thermodynamically certain to happen eventually.

**Three questions your section must answer:**

1. **What conditions are used, and why those?** Name the temperature,
   pressure, catalyst, or concentration regime, with a source, and
   explain each choice in terms of rate.
2. **What is being traded off?** Almost every real process is a
   compromise. Name the two things pulling in opposite directions.
3. **How does improved efficiency connect to environmental
   sustainability?** Specifically: less energy per tonne, less waste to
   dispose of, a lower operating temperature, fewer emissions, a
   catalyst that replaces a reagent that would otherwise be consumed.
   Name the mechanism, not the sentiment.

**Sources**: at least two, of appropriate kinds — a government or
regulatory agency, a professional body, a company's published technical
documentation, a peer-reviewed article. Every figure traceable to
something a reader can open, cited in the format in
[[Writing About Chemistry]]. If you cannot find a reliable figure,
**write that you could not**. See [[What Counts as Evidence]].

## What to hand in

**1. A full lab report**, in the sections set out in
[[Writing a Lab Report]], including:

- Your design contract, with its justifications, **dated before the
  lab**.
- Your predicted relationship and predicted ratio, **dated before the
  data**.
- A complete data table: every trial, every controlled quantity
  recorded rather than assumed, every measurement with the resolution of
  the instrument that produced it.
- **A graph with labelled axes and units.** If you timed a reaction to
  completion, think about whether you should be plotting $1/t$, and say
  why you chose what you chose.
- **Your quantitative result**, as a ratio or a relationship, with an
  uncertainty derived from your own repeats.
- **A conclusion that is justified with chemistry**, not just with your
  graph. What does collision theory say should happen, does your result
  agree, and where does the model stop being able to account for what
  you saw?
- **A ranked analysis of your uncertainty**, each mechanism with a
  **direction** and, where you can, a size.

**2. Part 2**, about a page, connected explicitly to your bench result.
The connection is the marks: your reaction is a small version of a
problem somebody solves at scale, and saying exactly how it is and is
not similar is the work.

Before you hand in, check every one of these:

- [ ] The equation is balanced and carries states
- [ ] The prediction is dated before the data and has not been edited
- [ ] Every controlled variable was **recorded**, not just intended
- [ ] Rate is reported with units, and it is clear what it is a rate of
- [ ] The initial-rate method is stated and justified
- [ ] At least four values of the independent variable
- [ ] Enough trials to show spread, and the spread is reported
- [ ] The graph has labelled axes with units
- [ ] The quantitative claim is a **number with an uncertainty**
- [ ] Every uncertainty mechanism has a **direction**
- [ ] The conclusion cites chemistry, not only the graph
- [ ] Part 2 names a trade-off and a specific sustainability mechanism
- [ ] Every researched figure is traceable

## How it will be judged

| What I am looking for | Level 3 sounds like | Level 4 sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Design | A workable procedure with variables named | Range, replication, and initial-rate method each chosen with a stated reason |
| Controlled variables | Listed | Listed, **recorded**, and each with the direction a failure would push |
| Prediction | An expected direction | A predicted **ratio**, with a mechanism, before the data |
| Data | Complete, with units | Every measurement carries its instrument's resolution, and the spread is shown |
| Analysis | A correct graph | The right quantity plotted, with the choice argued |
| The result | "Rate increased with concentration" | "Doubling the concentration multiplied the rate by 1.9 ± 0.2" |
| Conclusion | Consistent with the data | Justified with collision theory, **and** the model's limit named |
| Uncertainty | Sources listed | Ranked, each with a direction, and the dominant one identified |
| Part 2 | A real process, correctly described | The trade-off named, and the sustainability mechanism specific |
| Sources | Two appropriate sources, cited | Matched to the claims they support, with their limits noted |

## What sinks an otherwise good investigation

- **A result with no number.** "The rate increased" was the answer in
  Grade 10. If your report does not contain a ratio, it has not answered
  the question this task asked.
- **An average rate reported as an initial rate.** Timing a reaction to
  completion gives you an average over a period in which the
  concentration was falling. It is a legitimate measurement and it is
  not the same quantity, and calling it the wrong thing costs more than
  measuring it the harder way would have.
- **"Sources of error: human error."** A limitation is something about
  the method that survives doing everything perfectly. Misreading a
  balance is a blunder — repeat the trial.
- **A list of errors with no directions.** Half push the rate up and
  half push it down, and a list that does not say which is not an
  analysis.
- **Two trials called a spread.** They tell you that you can repeat
  yourself once. They do not give you an uncertainty, and quoting a mean
  of two values to three figures is a claim your data cannot support.
- **Ignoring self-heating.** Both acid reactions warm their own mixture,
  so the later part of each run is faster than your stated temperature
  would give — and the effect is larger for your more concentrated
  runs, which distorts the series in a specific direction. Naming that
  is worth more than any amount of tidy graphing.
- **Part 2 written as an essay about how important catalysts are.** Name
  the process, name the conditions, name what is traded against what,
  and name who or what benefits from the efficiency.
- **Concluding that a catalyst increased the yield.** It did not. Faster
  is not further, and this is the sentence I will be looking for
  specifically.
