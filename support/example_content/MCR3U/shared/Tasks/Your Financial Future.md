## What you are making

This one is about your actual life. You will build a **personal
savings-and-borrowing plan** with three parts, each powered by the
mathematics of this unit:

1. **A goal.** Something real — a car, a first apartment, a semester
   of tuition. Using compound interest, $A = P(1 + i)^n$, work out
   what a single deposit today would grow to, and how long your goal
   takes at a rate you can actually find advertised.
2. **A habit.** Nobody saves in one deposit. Design a regular-payment
   plan — an annuity — and show what it is worth at the end. Say in
   your own words what an annuity is, and take it apart: match each of
   your payments to its term in the geometric series it belongs to, so
   that the first deposit is the one compounding longest and the last
   one is not compounding at all. Then change one condition at a time
   (payment size, frequency, rate) and report which change matters
   most.
3. **The mirror.** Borrowing is the same mathematics pointed at you.
   Choose a realistic debt — a financed phone, a used car loan — and
   compute the total interest paid over its life. State, in one plain
   sentence, what the loan really costs beyond its sticker price.

The comparison table is the heart of the plan: scenarios side by side,
so the reader can watch one condition change while everything else
holds still — [[Money Over Time]] shows the pattern.

## Milestones

- [ ] Goal chosen; a real advertised interest rate found and cited
- [ ] Part 1 computed two ways — formula and spreadsheet — and the two
      agree
- [ ] Part 2 annuity designed, each payment matched to its term in the
      series; scenarios tabled and the most powerful condition named
- [ ] Part 3 loan analysed; total interest stated next to the principal
- [ ] One page of plain-language advice written to your future self

## How it is assessed

Per [[How Marks Work]]: reasoning, honesty, and communication — not
the size of the imaginary fortune. A plan that concludes *this loan is
a bad idea* is a strong plan. This one is yours alone from first page
to last, so the mark is yours alone too.

Your [[Math Journal]] entry on what surprised you most about the mirror
is written in class, in the ten minutes set aside on the day the plan
comes in, and it belongs to this task. With two working periods still
to run, [[Judging Your Own Work]] turns the table below into a fix while
there is time to make one; [[Checking Your Own Work]] is the last step
before handing it in.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like in your plan |
| --- | --- |
| Real numbers | Rates are current, cited, and plausible |
| Two-way checks | Formula and spreadsheet agree, or the gap is explained |
| An annuity, said plainly | What an annuity is, in your own words, before any formula arrives |
| An annuity taken apart | Each payment matched to its term in the series — the first compounding longest, the last not at all |
| Fair comparisons | One condition changes at a time in the table |
| Honest debt | Total interest is stated plainly, next to the principal |
| Advice that lands | Your future self could act on the final page |
| The entry that goes with it | Your in-class journal entry names what surprised you most about the mirror |

> [!warning]- A word about the mirror
> Advertising shows the payment, never the total. If your loan's total
> interest surprises you, you have done the task correctly — write the
> surprise down. This is the one page from this course most worth
> keeping.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 14, the working period where the rate is hunted down

  Going well: a student reading past the headline number, then writing the

  divided by twelve because that felt like the sort of thing you do.

  Record: on your class list, the rate, and a P beside anyone you heard find

TALK — Unit 4, Day 15, at the conference already on that agenda

  Ask: "Your part 2 changes one condition at a time. Which of those
  conditions could you actually change in real life, and which ones are

  Then: "If every payment in your part 2 moved from the end of the period to
  the beginning, what happens to the total?"

  On the first, listen for the split: the payment is theirs and the frequency
