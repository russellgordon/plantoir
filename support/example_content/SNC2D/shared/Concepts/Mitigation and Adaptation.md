When people talk about responding to climate change, almost every disagreement
comes down to confusing two different projects.

**Mitigation** means addressing the cause: reducing greenhouse gas emissions
or enhancing sinks so that the warming slows down and eventually stops.
**Adaptation** means addressing the consequence: changing how we build, farm,
protect communities, and live so that we can handle the climate change that is
already happening or committed.

A serious plan never chooses between them. You cannot adapt your way out of
unlimited warming, and you cannot mitigate fast enough to avoid the warming
already locked into the system.

## Mitigation: reducing the drivers

Mitigation is physics and chemistry: stopping the accumulation of greenhouse
gases in the atmosphere. Every action here either prevents an emission or
captures carbon before it warms the planet.

- **Decarbonising electricity generation.** Shifting grids from fossil fuels
  like coal and natural gas to hydro, nuclear, wind, and solar removes the
  single largest point-source emissions.
- **Electrifying heating and transport.** Replacing fossil-fuel combustion in
  vehicles and building heating with heat pumps and electric vehicles shifts
  energy demand onto the decarbonised electricity grid.
- **Industrial and agricultural practices.** Improving industrial efficiency,
  refrigerant management, reducing methane from livestock and waste, and
  improving nitrogen fertiliser application.
- **Protecting and restoring natural sinks.** Halting deforestation, restoring
  wetlands and peatlands, and managing soils so that biological systems take up
  more carbon than they release.

Because greenhouse gases mix globally, a tonne of $\ce{CO2}$ prevented
anywhere helps the entire planet equally. But mitigation takes time to alter
temperatures because of the thermal inertia of the oceans and the long
atmospheric lifetime of carbon dioxide.

## Adaptation: preparing for the consequences

Because past emissions have already committed the planet to additional warming,
adaptation is not an alternative to mitigation — it is an immediate necessity.
Unlike mitigation, adaptation is local: the risks in a coastal town are
different from the risks in a boreal community or a dense urban centre.

- **Built infrastructure and drainage.** Designing storm-water systems, culverts,
  and permeable pavements to handle high-intensity precipitation events without
  catastrophic flooding.
- **Urban heat and cooling.** Expanding tree canopies, green roofs, and
  reflective surfaces, and establishing municipal cooling centres for heatwaves.
- **Coastal and northern resilience.** Building sea walls and living shorelines
  against storm surges, and modifying foundations and winter transport routes
  to cope with thawing permafrost.
- **Agriculture and emergency response.** Developing drought-tolerant crop
  varieties, shifting planting schedules, and expanding wildfire response
  and evacuation planning.

> [!important] Why both are mandatory
> If we only adapt, emissions continue to rise, positive feedback loops
> accelerate, and the costs and physical limits of adaptation eventually
> overwhelm human systems. If we only mitigate, communities will suffer
> preventable disasters from the changes already in motion before global
> temperatures stabilise.

## Evaluating initiatives and local action

When evaluating a climate policy or proposing an initiative for
[[The Climate Brief]], test it with two questions:

1. **Which ledger does it address?** Is it preventing future warming
   (mitigation) or protecting people from current/committed impacts
   (adaptation)? Some measures — like restoring an urban wetland — do both.
2. **Who carries the upfront cost versus who receives the benefit?** Does it
   protect one neighbourhood while shifting flood water onto another?

Explore the ethical and economic trade-offs in [[Whose Climate, Whose Cost]],
and ground your claims in the data from [[Natural and Human Influences on Climate]].
