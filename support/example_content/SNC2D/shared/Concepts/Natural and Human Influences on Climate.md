[[Modelling the Greenhouse Effect]] showed you one variable acting alone. The
real atmosphere never does that. Climate has been changing for as long as
there has been a planet, driven by things nobody put there, and the honest
question is not "is the climate changing" — it always is — but **what is
driving the change we are measuring now, and how do we tell the causes
apart.**

That is a question about timescales and mechanisms, and it is answerable.

## Natural influences, and the timescales they work on

Each of these is real, and each one leaves a signature you can look for.

- **Orbital cycles.** Earth's orbit and the tilt of its axis vary slowly,
  changing how sunlight is distributed across the seasons and latitudes. This
  is the pacemaker of the ice ages, and it operates over **tens of thousands
  of years**. Over the span of a century it moves essentially nothing.
- **Solar output.** The Sun brightens and dims slightly on a cycle of about
  eleven years, with longer, weaker variations on top. The effect on global
  temperature is small, and — importantly — it is measured directly by
  satellites, so it is not a matter of speculation.
- **Volcanic eruptions.** A large eruption injects sulfate particles into the
  stratosphere, which reflect sunlight and **cool** the surface noticeably for
  two or three years before settling out. A volcano is a sharp, temporary
  downward spike in the record, not a trend.
- **Plate tectonics, uplift, and weathering.** Continents move, mountains
  rise, and the rate at which rock weathers and locks away carbon changes with
  them. This shapes climate over **millions of years**.
- **Internal variability.** The El Niño and La Niña cycle in the Pacific moves
  heat between the ocean and the atmosphere on a scale of a few years. It
  makes individual years warmer or cooler without adding any energy to the
  system — it redistributes what is already there.

Line those up and a pattern appears. The natural drivers that are strong are
slow, and the natural drivers that are fast are small or temporary. None of
them produces a steady rise sustained over a century, and the ones that could
be checked directly — solar output, volcanic activity — have been checked and
do not account for it.

## Human influences, including the one that cools

The additions are straightforward to list and hard to argue with, because in
most cases we know how much was burned.

**Burning fossil fuels** is the largest, releasing carbon that had been out of
circulation for hundreds of millions of years. **Deforestation and land-use
change** remove a sink and release stored carbon at the same time. **Cement
production** drives carbon dioxide out of limestone chemically, before any
fuel is burned. **Agriculture** contributes methane from livestock and rice
paddies and nitrous oxide from fertilised soils. **Industrial halocarbons**
were manufactured for refrigeration and propellants and exist for no other
reason.

One human influence works the other way, and leaving it out would be
dishonest. **Aerosols** — sulfate and soot particles from burning coal and
from industry — reflect sunlight and have masked part of the warming.
They also cause serious air-quality harm and wash out of the atmosphere within
about a week, so cleaning up the air removes a cooling influence that had been
hiding some of the effect. That is an uncomfortable fact and it is still a
fact.

**Canada's part** in this has a particular shape. Emissions per person are
among the highest in the world, and the reasons are structural rather than
moral: a cold climate with a long heating season, enormous distances between
places, an economy that includes large-scale oil and gas extraction, and a
grid whose carbon intensity varies dramatically from province to province.
Canada also holds a vast area of boreal forest and peatland, which stores an
immense amount of carbon and releases it when it burns or thaws — a sink and a
potential source in the same landscape. Environment and Climate Change Canada
publishes the national inventory of emissions each year; if you need numbers
for [[The Climate Brief]], take them from there rather than from an article
that does not say where its figures came from.

## Not everything in the sky is climate change

Three atmospheric problems get tangled together constantly, in newspapers and
in exam answers. They have different causes, different chemistry, and
different solutions.

> [!warning] Ozone depletion is not global warming
> **Stratospheric ozone depletion** is a chemical problem. Chlorofluorocarbons
> released at the surface drift up into the stratosphere, where ultraviolet
> light breaks them apart and frees chlorine atoms. Each chlorine atom
> destroys ozone molecules catalytically — it is not consumed, so one atom
> destroys many. Less ozone means more ultraviolet radiation reaching the
> surface, which raises rates of skin cancer, cataracts, and damage to crops
> and plankton. It does **not** mean more heat gets in. Sunlight was never
> being blocked by ozone in any quantity that matters for temperature, and
> "the hole lets the heat in" is simply wrong. The Montreal Protocol phased
> out the responsible chemicals and the ozone layer is slowly recovering,
> which makes this the clearest case we have of an international agreement
> fixing an atmospheric problem. The one genuine overlap is that CFCs are also
> potent greenhouse gases, so banning them helped on both counts — that is a
> coincidence of chemistry, not a shared cause.

**Ground-level ozone** is the same molecule in the wrong place. It is not
emitted directly; it forms when nitrogen oxides and volatile organic compounds
from vehicles and industry react in sunlight, which is why smog is worst on
hot, still, sunny days in cities. At ground level ozone irritates airways,
worsens asthma, and damages crops. Ozone high up protects us and ozone down
here harms us, and both statements are true at once.

**Smog** is the visible mixture that results: ground-level ozone together with
fine particles. It is a local and regional air-quality problem operating over
days, while the enhanced greenhouse effect is a global problem operating over
decades. Cleaning up smog is worth doing on its own terms; it is not the same
project as reducing greenhouse gas emissions, though several actions serve
both.

## Which gases matter, and how we know the climate is changing

Not all greenhouse gases are equivalent, and two properties decide how much
each one matters: **how strongly a single molecule absorbs infrared**, and
**how long it stays in the atmosphere**.

Carbon dioxide is the weak, long-lived, high-volume case, and it is used as
the reference point. Methane absorbs far more strongly per molecule — its
warming effect over a hundred years is roughly thirty times that of the same
mass of carbon dioxide, as reported in IPCC assessments — but it breaks down
within about a decade, so cutting methane produces results quickly. Nitrous
oxide is stronger still per unit mass, roughly two hundred and seventy times,
and lasts about a century. Manufactured halocarbons are extraordinarily potent
per molecule and are present in tiny amounts.

Those two properties pull in opposite directions when deciding what to cut
first. Reducing methane buys time; reducing carbon dioxide is the only thing
that addresses the long run. Both are true.

The **indicators** are what turn all of this from argument into measurement.
Look for the ones that are independent of each other, because agreement
between unrelated measurements is what makes a conclusion hard to dismiss:
retreating mountain glaciers, shrinking ice sheets, declining Arctic sea ice
extent at the end of summer, rising sea level from both meltwater and the
thermal expansion of warming seawater, rising ocean heat content, thawing
permafrost, earlier lake ice break-up and later freeze-up, shifting growing
seasons and species ranges, and changes in precipitation and wind patterns. In
Canada the northern indicators are the loudest — permafrost thaw damaging
buildings and roads, and shorter winter ice-road seasons for communities that
depend on them.

Read those records carefully rather than credulously, using
[[Reading a Graph Honestly]] and [[Climate Data Practice]]. Then look at why
the system does not respond in a simple straight line:
[[Feedback Loops in Climate]].
