In this course the investigation still comes **first**. You go to the
bench before anybody explains what you are about to see, because a
question you have felt is worth ten questions you have been handed. The
concept page arrives afterwards, and its only job is to account for what
your class actually measured.

What changes this year is what counts as an account. Last year a good
explanation described what happened. This year it has to say **why that
much and no further** — why the boiling point is *that* high, why the
reaction stops where it stops, why one downhill reaction takes a second
and another takes a decade.

Four of the eight are written up in full, in class, and marked — the
first, third, fifth and seventh in that list. Which four, on which days,
and against what criteria: [[The Lab Reports]]. The other four are read
back in the consolidation and live in your [[Chemistry Journal]].

Every page has the same seven parts: what you are trying to find out,
what you have to work with, safety for **that** procedure, the
prediction you write down first, what to collect, what to bring to the
consolidation, and what you should not claim. Read the whole page before
the class it belongs to — especially the safety section, which is not
optional reading and is not the same list twice.

## What is different from Grade 11

You already know that the answer is a number, that uncertainty is
handled numerically, that you design rather than follow, and that an
error gets a **direction** and not just a mention. All of that still
holds. Three things are added.

- **You choose a model and say where it stops being true.** VSEPR,
  collision theory, and Le Châtelier's principle are each good enough to
  predict most of what you will see and each has an edge past which it
  quietly fails. Finding that edge is not a criticism of the model. It
  is the most useful thing you can know about it.
- **Energy is a driver, not a side effect.** When something happens, you
  are expected to separate two questions that Grade 11 let you blur:
  *which way does it go* and *how fast does it get there*. Downhill is
  not the same as quick, and the labs in Unit 3 exist to make that
  distinction impossible to ignore.
- **Structure is the explanation, not the label.** "It has hydrogen
  bonding" is a name. "The molecule is bent, so the two polar bonds do
  not cancel, so neighbouring molecules attract each other strongly
  enough that this takes an extra forty kilojoules per mole to
  vaporise" is an explanation. Aim at the second one.

> [!danger] Standing rules — every lab day, no exceptions
> These are not repeated on every page. They are assumed on every page,
> and the page's own safety section is what gets **added** to them.
>
> - **Eye protection on** from the first move to the last of the
>   cleanup, including while somebody else is still working. Long hair
>   tied back, loose sleeves secured, closed-toe shoes.
> - **Know where the eyewash, the safety shower, the fire extinguisher,
>   the fire blanket, and the spill kit are before you start.** Not
>   roughly. Point at each one.
> - **Add acid to water, never water to acid.** Dilution releases heat.
>   With the water already there, the heat spreads through a large
>   volume; the other way round it concentrates in a few drops that can
>   boil and spit acid back out at you.
> - **Hot glassware and hot ceramic look exactly like cold glassware and
>   cold ceramic.** There is no visual difference. Assume anything near
>   a heat source is hot until you have watched it cool.
> - **Never pipette by mouth.** Bulb or pump, every time, for every
>   liquid, including water.
> - **Waft, do not sniff.** Cup a hand and draw the air toward you.
>   Never put your face over a container and inhale.
> - **Never return unused chemical to the stock bottle.** Take what you
>   need; what is left goes to the labelled waste container, not back
>   into stock and not down the sink unless the page says so.
> - **Nothing is tasted. Nothing is food.**
> - **School reagents only, at the concentrations supplied**, and
>   combine only what your approved procedure lists. Substances that are
>   harmless apart can make a toxic gas together and there is no way to
>   tell by looking, which is why this rule is absolute rather than a
>   matter of judgement.
> - **Nothing that makes a gas is ever sealed in a rigid container**, and
>   nothing sealed is ever heated.
> - **Report every incident the moment it happens, however small.** A
>   splash you rinsed off yourself is still something I need to know.
>
> Any procedure **you** designed is checked by me before you run it,
> including what could go wrong and what you would do about it. Missing
> eye protection or an unchecked procedure means the written alternative
> instead, for everyone's sake.

## Four hazards this course adds

Grade 12 chemistry puts things on the bench that Grade 11 did not, and
the four below are the ones that hurt people in school laboratories.
Each one appears again, in detail, on the page where it applies.

**Flammable organic solvents.** Ethanol, propanol, hexane, acetone and
their relatives are used in Units 1 and 2. **The vapour is the hazard,
not the liquid** — it is heavier than air, it travels along a bench, and
it finds an ignition source you did not think was nearby. So: no open
flame anywhere in the room, no hot plate under an open container, work
in the fume hood, keep bottles closed between uses, and heat only in a
water bath.

**Sodium hydroxide, which does not warn you.** Dilute acid stings, so
you notice a splash and wash it off. Sodium hydroxide feels soapy or
slippery instead — that sensation is the solution attacking the fats in
your skin — and it goes on working while it does not hurt. A base burn
of the same strength penetrates **deeper** than an acid burn for exactly
that reason. Rinse any suspected contact for at least 15 minutes under
running water, whether or not it hurts, and tell me. In an eye it is an
emergency: eyewash immediately, hold the lid open, and somebody else
comes for me while you stay at the eyewash.

**Gases from electrolysis.** Passing current through an aqueous solution
makes gas at both electrodes, and one of them is usually **hydrogen**.
No flames in the room, and the cell is never sealed — a sealed cell is a
pressure vessel you built by accident.

**Combustion in a calorimeter is a demonstration.** Burning a fuel under
a can of water involves an open flame, a hot metal surface, and a
flammable liquid within a few centimetres of each other. I run that one.
Student-hands calorimetry in this course is done in a polystyrene cup,
with solutions, at room temperature, and the pages say so explicitly.

## Toxic salts and heavy metals go to waste containers

Several of these investigations use solutions of copper, iron, and other
metal salts, and one demonstration uses a thiocyanate salt. **None of it
goes down the sink.** Every page tells you which labelled container its
waste belongs in. Wash your hands before you leave the room, every time,
including the days when you were sure you touched nothing.
