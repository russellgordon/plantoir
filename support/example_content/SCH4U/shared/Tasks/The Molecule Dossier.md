Pick up almost anything in your house and an organic chemist could tell
you what it is made of, why it works, and what happens to it afterwards.
The bottle under the sink. The film on the sandwich. The tablet in the
cupboard. The bag the shopping came in.

You are going to become that chemist for exactly **one** compound. Not
a survey, not a category — one molecule, followed all the way from its
structural formula to the people it affects.

## The situation

Somebody is deciding whether to keep using something. Not a chemist — a
person with a normal amount of scepticism, a limited amount of time, and
a genuine question, which is usually some version of *should I be
worried about this, and if so, about what exactly?*

The two easy answers are both useless. "It's a chemical, so avoid it"
tells them nothing, since everything is. "It's approved, so it's fine"
tells them nothing either, since approval is a decision made under
conditions that may not be theirs.

What is useful is the middle: **what the substance is, what it does,
what is known about its effects, who says so, and where the knowledge
runs out.** That is the dossier.

## Who it is for

A general reader who is not a chemist and who will notice if you are
being vague. They will accept a structural formula on the page if you
tell them what to look at in it. They will not accept "studies show".

Write for that person throughout, including in the technical sections.
If you find yourself writing a sentence you could not defend to somebody
who asked "how do you know that?", cut it or cite it.

## Choosing your compound

It must be an organic compound that people actually encounter, and you
must be able to draw its structure and name its functional groups. Some
that work well:

| Category | Examples that work |
| --- | --- |
| Solvents and household | Ethanol, propanone, ethyl ethanoate, propane-1,2,3-triol |
| Food-related | Ethanoic acid, citric acid, ascorbic acid, a named preservative |
| Pharmaceutical | Aspirin, paracetamol, ibuprofen, caffeine |
| Polymer | Polyethene, polystyrene, poly(ethylene terephthalate), PVC |
| Agricultural | A named herbicide or insecticide |

You may propose something else. Bring it early, and bring it with a
structural formula, because a compound whose structure you cannot find
is a compound you cannot write this dossier about.

**One person per compound in each class.** First come, first served, on
the sign-up sheet.

## What the dossier contains

**1. Identity — and this section is marked on precision.**

- **The IUPAC name**, correctly formed. If the compound is normally
  known by a common name or a trade name, give those too and say which
  is which.
- **The molecular formula.**
- **The structural formula, drawn by hand.** Not copied and pasted.
  Drawing it is how you find out whether you understand it, and a
  drawing with the wrong number of bonds on a carbon is a very visible
  thing.
- **Every functional group present, circled on your drawing and named.**
- **The class or classes** the compound belongs to.
- **If it is a polymer**: the monomer, its structure, the type of
  polymerisation, and the repeating unit drawn with the bonds that
  continue shown as continuing.

**2. Properties, explained from the structure.** At least three physical
properties — melting or boiling point, solubility in water, solubility
in fats or non-polar solvents, volatility — each with a value from a
source **and** an explanation that points at a specific part of your
drawing.

This is the section where the whole of Unit 1 pays off. "It is soluble
in water" is a fact. "The $-\ce{OH}$ group can hydrogen bond to water,
and the carbon chain is short enough that the rest of the molecule does
not outvote it" is chemistry.

**3. What it is used for, and why this structure suits the job.** The
connection between the two is the point. A solvent that evaporates fast
has a structure that makes it evaporate fast. A polymer that is flexible
at room temperature is flexible for a structural reason.

**4. The impact — health, society, and environment.** All three, and
each one specific:

- **Health.** What are the recognised hazards, and at what exposures? A
  safety data sheet is the standard starting point and it is written
  for people who handle the substance in quantity, which is worth
  saying to your reader.
- **Society.** What does it make possible? What would be different
  without it? This section is not a formality — a compound with real
  hazards may still be worth using, and a dossier that lists only harms
  has not assessed anything.
- **Environment.** What happens to it after use. Where it ends up, how
  long it persists, what breaks it down and into what.

**Report what your sources say and attribute every claim to the source
that made it.** Where sources disagree — and on this kind of question
they often do — say so, name both, and say what kind of body each one
is. **Do not resolve a disagreement you are not equipped to resolve.**
"A regulatory agency and a campaigning organisation reach different
conclusions here, and the difference appears to turn on which exposure
level is being considered" is a strong, honest sentence.

**5. A personal course of action.** One realistic change a person could
make, stated concretely, together with an honest account of what it
would and would not achieve. The curriculum's own examples are of this
shape: weeding a lawn by hand rather than using a herbicide, using cloth
bags instead of plastic ones, choosing a more fuel-efficient vehicle.

The marks here are entirely in the honesty. **A proposal that says what
it does not solve is worth more than one that claims to solve
everything.** If your compound is used mainly in industrial processes,
say that an individual's choices barely touch the total and name what
would. If the substitute has its own costs, name them.

> [!warning] Nothing comes in from home, and nothing is opened here
> This is the rule this particular task exists to break, so it is
> written out in full.
>
> **Do not bring the product to school.** Not the bottle, not a sample,
> not "just a little in a jar". Bring a **photograph of the label**,
> which is all the dossier needs and which contains the ingredient list,
> the hazard symbols, and the manufacturer's name.
>
> Household products are mixtures of unknown composition at unknown
> concentrations. They are not school reagents, they are not covered by
> our safety agreement, and two of them together can make a toxic gas
> without any warning — which is exactly why the standing rule is
> **combine only what an approved procedure lists**, and why that rule
> is absolute rather than a matter of judgement.
>
> Nothing is opened, nothing is smelled, nothing is tasted, and nothing
> is tested. This dossier is written from sources and from your own
> chemistry, and it does not need a bench.

## Documentation

A1.7 asks for **an accepted form of academic documentation**, used
consistently. Pick one style, use it for everything, and do not mix.

At least **four sources**, of at least **three different kinds**:

| Kind | What it is good for | What to watch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Safety data sheet | Hazards, handling, physical data | Written for bulk handling, not for consumer exposure |
| Government or regulatory agency | Approvals, limits, monitoring data | States conditions; read what the limit applies to |
| Peer-reviewed article | Specific findings, with methods | One study is one study |
| Professional or scientific body | Consensus positions | Check the date |
| Manufacturer documentation | Composition, technical properties | Has an interest, and should be labelled as such |

**Every number in the dossier is traceable to something a reader can
open.** If you cannot find a reliable figure for something, **write that
you could not**. That sentence has never cost anybody a mark in this
course and inventing a plausible number has cost several. See
[[What Counts as Evidence]].

## What to hand in

**1. The dossier**, about three pages plus the drawing, with the five
sections above clearly separated.

**2. The structural formula, drawn by hand**, with the functional groups
circled and labelled.

**3. A reference list**, complete, in one consistent style.

Before you hand in, check every one of these:

- [ ] The IUPAC name is correctly formed, with locants where they are
      needed
- [ ] The molecular formula matches the drawing — count the atoms
- [ ] Every carbon in the drawing has exactly four bonds
- [ ] Every functional group is circled **and** named
- [ ] Each property has a sourced value **and** a structural explanation
- [ ] Health, society, and environment all appear, all specifically
- [ ] Every claim about effects is attributed to a named source
- [ ] Disagreements between sources are reported rather than resolved
- [ ] The personal course of action says what it does **not** achieve
- [ ] One documentation style throughout
- [ ] No number appears that a reader cannot trace

## How it will be judged

| What I am looking for | Level 3 sounds like | Level 4 sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Identity | Correct IUPAC name and formula | Correct, with the naming choices explained where they were not obvious |
| Structural drawing | Accurate, groups labelled | Accurate, and used as evidence later in the dossier |
| Properties from structure | Properties listed with values | Each one derived from a named feature of the drawing |
| Use and structure | The use is described | The use is **explained** by the structure |
| Health, society, environment | All three covered | All three specific, with exposures and conditions attached |
| Handling sources | Sources cited | Sources weighed — kind, interest, and date all considered |
| Disagreement | Noted | Characterised: what the disagreement is actually about |
| Personal action | A real, relevant change | Realistic, with its limits and its costs named |
| Documentation | Consistent and complete | Consistent, complete, and matched to the claims it supports |
| Writing for the reader | Clear | A non-chemist finishes it better informed and appropriately uncertain |

## What sinks an otherwise good dossier

- **"Studies show."** Which studies? Who ran them, on what, at what
  exposure? An unnamed study is a rumour with a lab coat on.
- **A structure copied from a search result and not understood.** It
  shows the moment the dossier has to explain a property, because the
  explanation points at the wrong part of the molecule or at no part of
  it.
- **A carbon with three bonds or five.** Count them. This is the
  cheapest mark on the page and it is lost more often than any other.
- **Toxicity treated as a yes-or-no property.** Everything is toxic at
  some dose and harmless at some lower one. A dossier that says "it is
  toxic" without a quantity has not said anything a reader can use.
- **An impact section that is only harms, or only benefits.** Either one
  alone is advocacy. The task is assessment.
- **A personal course of action with no limits.** "Everyone should stop
  using it" is not a proposal; it is a wish. What would actually
  change, by how much, and what would it cost the person doing it?
- **Mixed citation styles**, which reads as a dossier assembled rather
  than written.
