> [!abstract] At a glance
> Individual · three hours in the examination period · closed book ·
> together with [[The Independent Study]], this is the final 30%

## What is on it

| Part | Roughly | What it asks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A. Sight passage — prose | 25% | An unseen passage: inference, technique, and one analytical paragraph |
| B. Sight passage — poem | 20% | An unseen poem, read closely |
| C. Short answers on the studied texts | 20% | Precise interpretation across the play, the novel, and the short texts |
| D. Comparative essay | 35% | One argument needing two studied texts, planned and written in the room |

## What it rewards

- **Passages known cold**: four per major text, chosen now.
- **A thesis with a mechanism**, produced under time. That is a habit,
  and habits are built by writing three practice openings, not by
  reading notes.
- **Reading the question's verb** — *analyse*, *evaluate*, *compare*.
- **Analysis longer than quotation**, under pressure.

## How it is marked

The same four categories as everything else, and the criteria are the
ones you have been marked against since September. Nothing on this paper
asks for a skill that has not already been practised in class.

| Category | What I am looking for |
| --- | --- |
| Knowledge | The studied texts known precisely: a passage placed, quoted accurately, and used for what it actually says |
| Thinking | The unseen passages read rather than summarised; inferences carried by the lines in front of you |
| Communication | Each answer shaped for the question asked and for what it is worth; the essay holds together from first sentence to last |
| Application | The method works on a text you are meeting for the first time, under time and without help |

## How to prepare

1. Four passages per text, annotated, in your notes.
2. Rewrite three of your own paragraphs from the autumn, from the
   passages rather than from the old paragraph.
3. Two timed sight passages, one prose and one poetry, before the
   examination.
4. Three practice comparative openings, fifteen minutes each.
5. Bring your questions to the four review classes.

> [!tip] In the room
> Budget by the weightings and wear a watch. Plan the essay for five
> minutes; the strongest examination essays every year have a visible
> plan in the margin. Answer everything — partial answers earn partial
> marks and a blank earns none.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

This is the one task where there is genuinely nothing to watch and
nobody to ask. The paper is sealed, the room may not be yours, and it is
marked after the course has ended, so anything you noticed would arrive
too late to be used by you or by them. Saying otherwise here would be
the easiest sentence on this page to write and the least true one.

So both slots sit in the three preparation classes — Days 13 to 15 —
where the same expectations are in play, the same paper is the reason,
and there are still four classes left in which to act on what you find.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 15, the three comparative openings.
  Fifteen minutes each, three prompts, one period: the only time this
  term anybody writes the same kind of thing three times in a row. What
  you can see, and cannot see from any one of them, is whether opening
  two is built differently from opening one.
  Watch for: the shape changing. A student who writes all three the same
  way has one essay plan and will use it whatever Part D asks; a student
  who reorders after the first attempt is choosing a pattern rather than
  reaching for one.
  Going well: a different first move on the second prompt; a plan in the
  margin that is crossed out and redrawn; somebody who abandons an
  opening at minute four rather than finishing a bad one.
  Stuck: three openings that begin with the same clause, and a student
  who is finished at minute nine each time.
  Record: a class list with three narrow columns and a shape letter in
  each — same, changed, abandoned. It is your list of who to work with
  on Days 16 to 18.
  Evidence for C1.4: choosing the organizational pattern best suited to
  the content and the purpose. Three prompts in one period is the only
  place the CHOOSING is separable from the writing.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 13, while the four passages per text are being chosen
in class.
  Ask: "Say out loud, in one sentence, what this passage is doing — the
  sentence you would actually write under time." Then: "If somebody else
  in this room chose this same passage for the opposite argument, what
  would they be pointing at?"
  A strong first sentence names the thing rather than admiring it — a
  hendiadys, a shift from verse to prose, a monosyllable dropped into a
  line of Latinate diction — and says what it does to a reader. That is
  B2.3, spoken. It is also the exact sentence they will have to produce
  in the examination room with a clock running, so hearing it now is
  worth more than reading it afterwards.
  A strong second answer holds two readings of the same lines at once
  and can support the one it does not hold, from detail the passage
  implies rather than states. That is B1.4, and it is the best predictor
  you will get of what happens to that student in Part D.
  Record: the class list you already have for passage selection. A star
  beside anyone who named a stylistic element unprompted; a circle
  beside anyone whose four passages are all the famous ones.

The product arrives on its own, and later than anything else in the
course.
