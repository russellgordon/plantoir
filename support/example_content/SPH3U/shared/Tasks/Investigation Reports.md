> [!abstract] At a glance
> **Format:** five write-ups, one per unit. **The bench work is done in
> your lab group. The write-up is yours alone.** **Assessed on:**
> Thinking and Investigation, and Communication.

We run twelve investigations this semester. Five of them are written up
and marked, and you know from today which five they are. The other seven
are for finding things out, and nothing you write about them counts
against you.

## The five, and when they are due

| Unit | Investigation | What is written | The physics it has to get right | Handed in |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | [[Measuring g]] | Analysis section | A velocity–time graph built from an object in free fall, and what its slope is | Unit 1, Day 10 |
| 2 | [[Newton's Second Law]] | Full report | Forces on one object in one dimension, argued from a free-body diagram, not from the equation | Unit 2, Day 11 |
| 3 | [[Specific Heat of a Metal]] | Analysis section | $Q = mc\Delta T$ solved for the quantity you did not measure, with the hazards of the method named | Unit 3, Day 12 |
| 4 | [[Measuring the Speed of Sound]] | Analysis section, both methods compared | A measured wave speed set against the theoretical value, with the discrepancy accounted for | Unit 4, Day 7 |
| 5 | [[Ohm's Law and Resistance]] | Analysis section | A circuit read from its diagram using $V = IR$, in the right words: ohmic, resistance, potential difference | Unit 5, Day 4 |

Each one gets a class period of analysis time with me in the room, and
that period is the check-in: put whatever you have in front of me during
it and I will name the row that is weakest, while the rest of the period
is still there to deal with it. Nothing here is handed in without that
chance having existed. [[Writing a Lab Report]] is the shape a full
report takes; an analysis section is that document's longest part, on its
own.

Two of the five handed you a real choice of method — [[Measuring g]] runs
video against a pendulum, and [[Measuring the Speed of Sound]] runs echo
timing against resonance in a tube. Those two write-ups end with a
recommendation: which method would you hand to somebody who had to
measure this tomorrow, with what equipment, and why that one. The other
three prescribe their apparatus, so instead you name the instrument that
put the ceiling on your result and say what you would use in its place.

## Why the group builds it and you write it

Two or three people at one bench get better data than one person at
three benches, and arguing about a reading is half of learning to take
one. So the measurements are shared: everyone at the bench may use the
same table, and you should say in one line whose hands did what.

The analysis is not shared. You choose what to graph, you decide what
your uncertainty allows you to claim, and you write the conclusion. Two
people from the same bench handing in the same sentences is two people
who have each done half of the thinking.

## The criteria, before you start

| What is judged | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| The data is honest | Every trial is there, including the one you did not like; the spread is reported, not just the mean |
| The instrument was chosen, not just used | Where there was a choice of method you recommend one and say what it cost you in precision, range or time; where there was not, you name the instrument that limited your result and what you would use instead |
| The graph earns its axes | You say why THAT quantity is on the vertical axis, and what its slope is physically |
| The analysis goes past the number | Per cent difference from the accepted or expected value, where there is one; the largest source of error named, with the direction it pushes your result; and one change that would reduce it |
| The conclusion is justified, not just stated | It claims what your uncertainty allows and no more — "consistent with 9.8 within our uncertainty" and "we measured 9.8" are different sentences — and it says WHY the number landed where it did, in the physics the investigation was testing |
| The hazards are named | One or two lines saying what in this method could have hurt somebody, and what you did about it — the way a real method section does it |
| Somebody could repeat it | Enough method that a reader could get your numbers, in the past tense, without "human error" appearing anywhere |
| How you work at the bench | Equipment used the way it is meant to be used; one variable changed at a time; a doubtful reading taken again rather than kept. This is the row I judge by watching |

"Somebody could repeat it" is the row that decides most write-ups, and
it asks one thing: could a person who was not there follow you? Read
[[Uncertainty and Error]] before you write the fourth row, and
[[Graphing Data]] before you draw anything.

**The last row is the odd one, because it is not in your write-up at
all.** Expectation A1.5 asks you to conduct an inquiry "safely,
accurately, and effectively", so how you work at the bench is part of
what is judged here, and the bench periods are the only place it can be
seen. It is the one working habit in this course that reaches your mark,
and it does so because the curriculum put it there — not because I like
tidy benches. ([[Safety in the Lab]] is a separate matter, and not
negotiable in any case.)

## What a strong write-up does that a weak one does not

A weak write-up explains the difference between its value and the
accepted one by listing everything that could ever go wrong in a lab. A
strong one names the ONE thing that mattered most in this room, on this
day, says which way it pushed the number, and says what it would cost to
remove it. That is a shorter paragraph and a much harder one.

> [!tip] Write the conclusion first, in rough
> If you cannot write one honest sentence answering the question the
> investigation asked, the analysis is not finished — and you have found
> that out before spending two hours on formatting.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 2, Day 3, the Newton's Second Law bench period
  Watch for: whether the three trials at each setting are RUN. The method
  asks for three and every table will contain three; what no table can
  show you is whether the second and third were measured or written. A
  group that runs one and decorates it hands in tidier data than a group
  that ran three honestly, which is exactly the problem. That is A1.6 —
  compiling ACCURATE data — and it is only ever visible at the bench.
  Watch also whether mass moves from the cart to the hanger rather than
  simply being added to it, which is A1.5 and the whole of part 1. This
  half you could catch afterwards, from the total-mass column — but only
  if they recorded what they truly had, and by then the period is over
  and the run cannot be done again. Catching it now is worth more.
  Going well: the balance is used between runs, somebody says the total
  out loud, and the third trial happens without anybody asking for it.
  Stuck: one run per setting, three numbers on the page, and a hanger
  that has been growing all period.
  Record: initials on your day plan, not group names — this row is marked
  individually, so note WHO ran the third trial and who watched. Two
  columns is enough: trials actually run, and whether the total held.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 6, as you go round the analysis period
  Ask: "If I gave you ten more minutes at the bench right now, which
  single measurement would you take again, and why that one?"
  Then: "Now double your uncertainty, right now, in your head. Does your
  conclusion still stand? If it does, tell me what a result would have to
  look like before that number started to matter."
  A strong first answer names a measurement, names what was doubtful
  about it at the time, and says what they would do DIFFERENTLY rather
  than just more carefully. That is A1.8 heard out loud, and it is the
  part a written error section fakes best: a report can list every
  textbook source of error without any of them being the one that
  actually bit.
  Record: the class list, one word per student — the measurement they
  chose.

The product evidence is the five write-ups themselves, on Unit 1 Day 10,
Unit 2 Day 11, Unit 3 Day 12, Unit 4 Day 7, and Unit 5 Day 4.
