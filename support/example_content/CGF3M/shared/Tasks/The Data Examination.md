**The final evaluation, written in a three-hour session at the end of the
course.** Individual, in class, with materials supplied. It is worth the
share of the final grade set out in [[How Marks Work]].

This is not a memory test. You are given data you have not seen and asked
to do with it what you have been doing all term.

## What you are given

A booklet of sources: a topographic extract, a climate record or
climograph, a hydrograph or gauge record, a satellite image pair, a
hazard map, a table of disaster figures from more than one publisher, and
a short written source with an identifiable point of view.

## What you are asked to do

**Interpret.** Read the physical evidence — a landform sequence, a storm
response in a hydrograph, a change between two images — and explain the
process that produced it.

**Judge the sources.** Two of the supplied sources will disagree. Say
which you would use, why, and what the disagreement itself tells you.
This is the skill from [[Judging a Source]] and
[[Hazard and Disaster Records]], examined directly.

**Apply the concepts of geographic thinking.** Spatial significance,
patterns and trends, interrelationships, and geographic perspective. You
will be asked to use named concepts on specific data, so practise saying
what each one lets you notice — [[The Geographic Inquiry Process]].

**Explain hazards from their processes.** One question hands you two
kinds of hazard you have met this term — one atmospheric or climatic (a
storm, a flood, a drought) and one geological (an earthquake, a tsunami,
a landslide) — and asks for the characteristics of each and the
processes that produce it. Not the event: the kind of event, what
controls how big it gets, and why it happens where it does.
[[Storms and Floods]], [[Drought and Heat]] and
[[Earthquakes and Volcanoes]] are the three pages to reread.

**Explain the planetary basics.** Why Earth supports life where it is,
and how tilt, orbit and the Earth–Sun–Moon geometry produce seasons,
tides and regional variation.

**Draw a conclusion under uncertainty.** A short written answer, with the
limits of the evidence stated as part of the answer rather than as an
apology for it.

## How it is marked

| Criterion | What earns the marks |
| --- | --- |
| Knowledge | Hazard types, physical processes and planetary mechanisms explained correctly |
| Thinking | Data interpreted; source credibility argued from stated grounds |
| Application | Named concepts of geographic thinking used on the actual data |
| Communication | Precise terminology; answers structured to the question asked |

## What to bring

A pencil, a ruler, a calculator and a highlighter. Nothing written —
everything you need is in the booklet, because the paper tests what you
can do with sources and therefore supplies them.

> [!tip] Practise the reading, not the recall
> The most useful revision here is working through a climograph, a
> hydrograph and a contour extract you have never seen and writing three
> sentences about each. Do that six times and the paper holds no
> surprises.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

Be honest with yourself about this one: the paper is a product, written
alone in silence, and no observation or conversation can be part of its
mark. What the three review periods give you is the last chance to watch
the reading happen while there is still time to reteach it, and evidence
for the term's judgement about A1 rather than for the paper.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 18, while unseen sources are worked together
  Watch for: what a student reaches for first on a graph they have never
  seen. Some go to the axis labels and the units; some start describing
  the shape and only later find out what is on the vertical. The three
  spoken sentences come out sounding similar either way, because the
  second sentence usually repairs the first, and the finished exam script
  shows you neither.
  Going well: a pencil goes along the axis before anything is said.
  Stuck: "it goes up and then down" as the opening move, twice running.
  Record: a class list with one letter — A for axes first, S for shape
  first — and change it when you see it change. This is the one you
  actually reteach from.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 20, on the supplied pair of disagreeing sources
  Ask: "What was the person who published the higher figure actually
  counting, and why was that the useful thing for them to count?" A
  strong answer gives them a purpose that is not dishonesty — an insurer
  counting what it pays, an agency counting what it can verify, a
  ministry counting what it funds. A1.3 asks about the purpose of the
  messaging and its intended audience, and the second half of the
  question is what separates understanding it from having been told it.
  Then: "If you could ask that author one question before you used the
  number, what would it be?" A strong question is about method or scope,
  and not about honesty.
  Record: two columns on the class list — named what was counted, named
  a motive. You will not reach everybody in one review period; take the
  ten you are least sure of, and finish the rest on Day 19.

The product evidence is the paper. It arrives on its own, and it is all
the exam mark rests on.
