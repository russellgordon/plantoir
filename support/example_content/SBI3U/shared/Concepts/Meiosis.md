Two divisions, four daughter cells, half the chromosome number, and no two
of them alike. Meiosis exists to make variation, and every step is
arranged to that end.

## Two divisions

**Meiosis I** separates homologous pairs — this is the reduction step,
diploid to haploid. **Meiosis II** separates sister chromatids, like
mitosis.

## Three sources of variation

1. **Crossing over** in prophase I: homologues exchange segments, making
   chromosomes that never existed before.
2. **Independent assortment** in metaphase I: which member of each pair
   goes which way is random, giving $2^{23}$ combinations in humans.
3. **Random fertilisation**: any sperm with any egg.

Multiply those and the number of genetically distinct children two people
could have exceeds the number of people who have ever lived.

## Seeing it, and drawing what you see

Meiosis is described in five neat phases and does not present itself
that way down a microscope. On a prepared slide of anther or testis
tissue you are looking at hundreds of cells caught at whatever moment
they were fixed — so the work is to find cells in different phases and
put them in order, not to watch one cell proceed.

What to hunt for, in the order it is easiest to find:

- **Metaphase I** — the giveaway. Chromosomes at the equator in
  *pairs*, not singly. That pairing is the whole difference from
  mitosis, and it is visible.
- **Anaphase I** — two groups pulling apart, each chromosome still
  visibly two chromatids joined at the centromere.
- **Prophase I** — the longest phase, so the most cells are in it:
  chromosomes condensed and paired, sometimes with chiasmata visible
  where crossing over is happening.
- **Telophase II** — four nuclei in what was one cell.

Draw at least three phases following [[Biological Drawing]]: pencil,
clear unbroken outlines, no shading, labels on horizontal ruled lines,
and a stated magnification. **Draw what is on the slide, not what is in
the textbook** — the temptation to tidy a real cell into the diagram you
already know is enormous, and it is the single most common fault in
these drawings. If you cannot find anaphase I, say so and draw what you
did find; a drawing of a phase you did not see is worth nothing, and a
sketchy honest one is evidence.

A computer simulation is a fair substitute for a phase your slide will
not show, and it is not a substitute for the slide: the simulation shows
you the idealised sequence, and the slide shows you the variability and
the mess that the idealised sequence is an average of. Where you use
one, say which figures came from which.

## Non-disjunction

When a pair fails to separate, a gamete ends up with an extra chromosome
or one too few. Trisomy 21 is the most familiar outcome, and a karyotype
shows it directly — see [[Reading a Karyotype]].
