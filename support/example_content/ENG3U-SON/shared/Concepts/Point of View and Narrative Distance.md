[[Boys and Girls]] is narrated by its main character looking back on her
own childhood — you are inside her head, but you are also aware, in a way
she was not at the time, of what her memories add up to. That double
awareness is not an accident of the story; it is a deliberate choice
about point of view, and this page gives you the vocabulary to talk about
it.

## Person: who is telling the story

- **First person** ("I") — the narrator is a character inside the story,
  limited to what they personally saw, felt, and understood.
- **Third person limited** ("she," "he," "they") — the narration stays
  close to one character's perspective without using "I."
- **Third person omniscient** — the narrator can move between characters'
  minds, and often knows more than any one character does.

[[Boys and Girls]] uses first person, which means every detail you get is
filtered through what the narrator noticed — and, this is the important
part, through what she chose, as an adult telling the story, to include.

## Distance: how close the narration stands

Person tells you whose eyes you are looking through. Distance tells you
how far the narration stands back from the events as it tells them. A
first-person narrator remembering childhood can hold two distances at
once: the close, immediate perception of the child living through the
moment, and the wider, more reflective distance of the adult who now
understands what that moment meant.

> [!tip] Watch for the seam
> When a narrator writes something like "I did not know it then, but—",
> that phrase is the distance showing. It is the adult narrator stepping
> forward, for one sentence, to tell you something the child living
> through the scene could not have told you. Look for that seam in
> [[Boys and Girls]] — it is where the story's meaning is doing the most
> work.

## Why this matters

Point of view is not neutral. Whoever tells a story decides what you get
to see, and a narrator remembering their own past is never simply
reporting — they are also, consciously or not, shaping what that past
means. Keep this page in mind whenever a text uses a first-person or
retrospective narrator again this year; it returns most clearly in how
Saul narrates *Indian Horse*, which [[Frame Narrative and Structure]]
picks up directly.
