**Thought tracking** starts where [[Tableau]] stops. The image is frozen, the
teacher — or later, a student director — taps a performer on the shoulder,
and that character speaks a single inner thought aloud: first person, present
tense, one sentence. Then they freeze again.

It is the smallest possible speaking part, which is exactly why we use it
early. One honest sentence, spoken from inside a still body, with the whole
group holding the picture around you.

## How to run it

1. Build a tableau and let it settle — count five seconds of real stillness.
2. The tapper circulates slowly. No one speaks until touched.
3. On the tap: one thought, spoken as the character, in the moment of the
   frozen scene. "I hope she didn't see me take it." Then silence again.
4. Tap two or three characters in the same image before releasing the freeze.
5. Ask the audience: which thought changed how you read the picture?

## The gap is the engine

What a character says and what a character thinks are almost never the same
thing, and the distance between them is called subtext.[^1] Thought tracking
makes that gap audible. A tableau of a smiling family photograph becomes a
different scene entirely when one frozen smiler thinks, "Last time we were
all in one room, nobody was speaking."

That gap is where [[Tension]] lives, and hearing it is the core skill of
interpretation — the same move you make when you [[B1.2|analyse]] a
character's motives in your [[Drama Journal]] or build a part from the inside
in [[Role and Character]].

> [!warning] The pitfall: narrating instead of thinking
> "I am standing beside my brother at the funeral" is a caption, not a
> thought. A thought wants something, fears something, or hides something.
> If the sentence could be read aloud by a narrator, tap again and go deeper.

## Check your ear

A tableau shows a family posed for a photograph at a graduation. One
performer is tapped on the shoulder. Nothing you answer here is sent to
anyone.

> [!quiz] Which line is a thought, and not a caption?
> - [ ] "I am standing beside my sister at her graduation."
> - [x] "If she moves away, who is going to notice me?"
> - [ ] "She felt proud of her sister that day."
> - [ ] "Everyone is smiling for the photograph."
>
> > [!explain]- Why
> > A tracked thought is first person, present tense, one sentence, and it
> > wants, fears, or hides something. The second line fears something. The
> > first and the last could be read out by a narrator, and the third is
> > about someone else, in the past.

Thought tracking is the natural second layer of [[Tableau Story Sequence]] —
three frozen images, each with one spoken thought, is already a play.

[^1]: Subtext is the meaning underneath the words — what a line is really
    doing, as opposed to what it literally says. Actors sometimes call it
    "the line under the line."
