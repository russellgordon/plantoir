This course reads work by First Nations, Métis, and Inuit writers. How we
read it matters as much as that we read it, because a story from a
tradition we are guests in cannot simply be run through the same machine
as everything else.

## Four things to know before we start

**Stories are not only entertainment or lesson.** In many Indigenous
traditions a story carries law, history, relationship to land, and
instruction, all at once — and which of those it carries can depend on
who is telling it, to whom, and when.

**Some stories belong to particular seasons, families, or nations.** Not
everything is ours to tell, retell, or adapt. If a writer says a story is
shared with permission, that sentence is doing real work and is worth
reading.

**"Indigenous" is not one culture.** There are more than 630 First
Nations communities in Canada, plus Métis and Inuit peoples, with
different languages, laws, and literatures. Anishinaabe and Haudenosaunee
and Cree and Inuit writing are not interchangeable, and saying which
nation a writer is from is basic accuracy, like getting a title right.

**Oral tradition is not "before" writing.** It is its own complete
technology for holding knowledge across generations, with its own
standards for accuracy, and it is alive now.

## How we read, practically

| We do | We do not |
| --- | --- |
| Name the writer's nation and use the words they use | Say "Indigenous people believe…" as though there were one answer |
| Ask what the story is doing for the community it comes from | Reduce it to a moral we can extract and keep |
| Read residential-school writing as history that is still in living memory | Treat it as a sad thing that happened long ago |
| Notice how land is written — as relation, not backdrop | Assume the landscape is scenery |
| Let a story sit unresolved | Insist on a tidy theme statement for everything |

## Where this course does it

- Rita Joe's ["I Lost My Talk"](I%20Lost%20My%20Talk), a Mi'kmaw poem
  about the Shubenacadie residential school, which we read beside her own
  account of writing it.
- [[Sugar Falls]], the graphic novel David A. Robertson and Scott B.
  Henderson made from the true story Elder Betty Ross of Cross Lake First
  Nation shared with them.
- [[A Mountain Legend]], Jordan Wheeler's story of a boy climbing toward
  something older than he is.
- [[Borders]], Thomas King's story of a border crossing where the whole
  conflict is one word repeated.
- [[The Marrow Thieves]], Cherie Dimaline's novel, in the literature
  circles.

## The Calls to Action

The Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada published 94 Calls to
Action in 2015. Several are about education — about what students in this
province learn, and when. Reading these texts is not an add-on to the
course; it is part of what the course is for, and it is worth knowing
that is a deliberate decision made by people who were listening.

> [!important] If this is your own history
> Some students in this room are reading about their own families. You
> are never obliged to speak for anybody, to answer questions on behalf
> of your nation, or to be the class's source. Say "pass" or come and
> talk to me, and neither will cost you anything.
