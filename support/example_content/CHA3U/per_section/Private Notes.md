## Section __SECTION_NUMBER__ — notes to myself

- Book the culminating audience early in the course. `The Long Argument` needs
  somebody who will push back, and the good ones are booked early.
- `Slavery and the Nation` requires a real statute clause in part one.
  Prepare a shortlist unless the class has archive access — students
  hunting for one cold will burn a period.
- Preview the language rule on that task before setting it. Students
  read "enslaved person, not slave" as squeamishness until it is
  explained as method.
- `The Source Study` makes coloniser-authored evidence the analytical
  problem rather than a disclaimer. That is a harder ask than the Grade
  10 equivalent; expect to model it once.

## Two claims this course deliberately does not make

- The Jackson line "John Marshall has made his decision; now let him
  enforce it" is almost certainly not something he said. The pages do
  not use it, and it is worth handing to students as an exercise in
  where quotations come from.
- The claim that the Haudenosaunee Confederacy directly shaped the
  United States Constitution is genuinely disputed among historians. The
  pages present it as contested rather than settled in either direction.

## What a teacher must add

`Our Working Agreement` and `Getting Help` carry the national crisis
lines, which are stable. **Add your board's own numbers and the guidance
office's hours** beside them — those are the ones a student in this
building will actually use.

## Pages to review before teaching

`Slavery`, `Jim Crow and Resistance`, `Removal and Resistance`, and
`Who Was Excluded, and By What Rule` all carry material a student may
have a personal stake in.
