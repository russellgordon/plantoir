Algorithms increasingly act as gatekeepers in our society. They determine whose resume gets read, who gets approved for a mortgage, what news you see, and even how long someone might serve in prison. 

When these algorithms are proprietary (closed-source "black boxes"), society cannot inspect them for bias, errors, or discriminatory logic. This lack of transparency has profound social impacts.

### Real-World Impacts

- **The UK Post Office Horizon Scandal:** A proprietary accounting software system falsely reported financial shortfalls at local post offices. Because the software's inner workings were trusted implicitly and hidden from scrutiny, hundreds of innocent postmasters were wrongly prosecuted and jailed.
- **Automated Hiring:** Algorithms screening resumes often learn biases from historical data, filtering out candidates based on names, zip codes, or gender-associated keywords.
- **Government Services:** In some jurisdictions, algorithms flag welfare recipients for fraud investigations based on opaque criteria, disproportionately targeting vulnerable demographics.

### The BC Context

In British Columbia, automated decision-making is heavily scrutinized. As the provincial government digitizes services like BC Housing applications, healthcare triage, and Ministry of Children and Family Development risk assessments, the demand for **algorithmic transparency** grows. Citizens have a democratic right to know *how* a machine arrived at a decision that impacts their life.

### Discussion Prompts

1. **The Burden of Proof:** If an algorithm denies you a service (like a student loan), who should have the burden of proof? Should you have to prove the algorithm is wrong, or should the creator have to prove the algorithm is right?
2. **Explainable AI:** Many modern AI systems (like neural networks) are so complex that even their creators cannot fully explain how they reach a specific output. Should we ban the use of "unexplainable" AI in high-stakes areas like criminal justice or healthcare?
3. **Corporate Secrecy vs. Public Good:** Companies argue that forcing them to reveal their algorithms violates their intellectual property and trade secrets. How do we balance a company's right to protect its invention with the public's right to fair treatment?

### Action Item

Look up a recent news article about an algorithm making a mistake. What was the impact? How could transparency have mitigated the harm?

### How we run this

We run this one as a **structured academic controversy** — arguing a
position on purpose, twice, before anyone gets to keep their own.

- Groups of four, split into two pairs. Each group is assigned one of the
  prompts above (most groups get prompt 2, the explainable-AI ban).
- Pair A spends eight minutes preparing the strongest case *for* the
  position; Pair B spends the same eight minutes on the strongest case
  *against*, both using the Real-World Impacts examples above as
  evidence rather than opinion.
- Each pair presents for three minutes, uninterrupted. The other pair may
  only ask clarifying questions — no rebuttals yet.
- **Then swap.** Each pair has five minutes to prepare and argue the
  *opposite* side, as convincingly as it can, including using the other
  pair's own best points if they were good ones.
- Drop the advocacy. For the last ten minutes, the whole group of four
  works together — no assigned side — to write one paragraph they can
  all live with, and hands it in.

Arguing a side you don't hold, twice, is the point: it is much harder to
dismiss a position once you have had to make its best case yourself.
