**The culminating task. Eight periods at the end of Unit 4, ending with
a presentation to a real audience.** Pairs or threes.

Some American arguments never finish. They are settled and then
reopened, in different words, by different people, in every generation.
Take one of them, follow it the whole length of the course, and say
where it stands now.

## The arguments on offer

- How much power the federal government should have, and over what.
- Who counts as a citizen, and what citizenship entitles you to.
- Race and rights: what equality before the law was taken to mean.
- Who may come, who may stay, and who decides.
- What the country owes, or claims, beyond its own borders.

You may propose your own, agreed with me first. It must be arguable in
all four periods — not merely mentionable in them.

## The eight periods

| Period | What it is for |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Choosing, and narrowing until the argument is a single question |
| 2 | Scoping: what evidence from each period would actually answer it |
| 3 | Evidence: primary sources located, with provenance recorded for each |
| 4 | Evidence: secondary reading, and where historians disagree with each other |
| 5 | Analysis: the concepts applied across the span, and the opposing view written out in full |
| 6 | Drafting: the through-line, and the place where it breaks |
| 7 | Rehearsal, and a hostile question from another group |
| 8 | Delivery to your audience, with questions taken live |

## What you deliver

- **A brief** of about 1,200 words: the question, its shape in each
  period with evidence, and where it stands today.
- **A presentation** of twelve minutes to an audience outside this class
  — another class, a library or historical society programme, a
  community organisation, or a recording published somewhere real.
- **A one-page leave-behind** that your audience keeps.

## What is marked as yours

This is part of the thirty per cent, and there is no common mark on it.
**Each period of the brief carries the name of whoever wrote it**, and that
is what your mark is read from. You take an equal share of the twelve
minutes, on your own periods, and you answer the audience's questions on
them yourself. The conferences in the middle of the eight are with each of
you separately.

One question is everybody's: the through-line. You are each expected to be
able to argue the whole of it, and the rehearsal period is where you find
out whether you can.

## How it is marked

| Criterion | What earns the marks |
| --- | --- |
| Question | One question, recognisably the same one in all four periods |
| Evidence | Sourced from every period, weighed, and cited |
| Synthesis | A through-line argued, including where it breaks or reverses |
| The present | The current state of the argument established from evidence, not opinion |
| Delivery | Twelve minutes pitched at the audience you actually have |

> [!tip]- What a through-line is, and what it is not
> Try writing your question in the same words for each period. If you
> have to change the words to make it fit one, you have two arguments
> rather than one — narrow it and start again.
> Then find the period where the pattern breaks: goes quiet, reverses,
> or is settled and then unsettled. Explaining that break is worth more
> than a smooth story, because a smooth story about four centuries is
> almost always one with the inconvenient period left out.

[[Historical Significance]] and [[Continuity and Change]] do the work
here; [[Building an Argument]] and [[Using Evidence]] are the craft. The
seminars [[Who Counts as American|Who Counts as American?]] and [[Is the United States an Empire|Is the United States an Empire?]]
are where two of these arguments have already been opened, and
[[Where History Leads]] is worth rereading before you choose.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

Groups of two or three, and part of the thirty per cent, so the individual
evidence matters more here than anywhere else in the course. Neither prompt
below is on Day 18: that period is the delivery, you are running the room
and hosting an audience, and nothing careful gets recorded during it.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 15, the analysis period
  Watch for: whether the break in the through-line was FOUND or NOMINATED.
  The criteria row asks for the place where the pattern breaks or reverses,
  so every brief will name one — the row guarantees it. What today shows
  you is whether a group went back through its evidence looking for the
  period that would not fit, and was visibly put out to find it, or whether
  a period was volunteered in under a minute because the row demanded a
  candidate.
  Going well: an argument inside the group about whether a period really
  counts as a break, settled by re-reading something.
  Stuck: "we'll say the twenties" agreed instantly and never reopened; or a
  break nominated for precisely the period the group has least evidence
  from.
  Record: on the day plan, the initials of everybody who spoke in each
  group, and one word — found, or nominated.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 14, the conferences on what your evidence will actually
support
  Ask each member separately: "You settled this question on Day 11. What
  did you nearly choose instead, and what would have been easier about it?"
  Then: "Name someone making this argument in public now. Would they
  recognise your history of it, or would they say you have described
  somebody else's fight?"
  A strong first answer names a rejected question and the real reason for
  rejecting it — usually that one period's evidence was not there — which
  is a judgement about scope rather than a preference. On the second, the
  brief is already required to establish where the argument stands now, so
  what this adds is whether the group can DEFEND that description or only
  assert it. A1.6 is the synthesis, heard rather than read; E1.4 is where
  the present-day half of the answer has to come from.
  Record: one line per STUDENT, on the class list, before the next group.

The product evidence is the brief, the leave-behind, and the delivery on
Day 18.
