Conflict leaves better records than cooperation. Wars generate dispatches,
casualty lists and monuments; a working agreement between neighbours
generates almost nothing. That imbalance in the sources is a trap, and the
way out of it is to go looking deliberately for the arrangements that held
rather than the ones that broke.

## Before the Europeans arrived, and after

Trade networks crossed the continent long before 1492. Copper from the Great
Lakes, obsidian from the Rockies, marine shell from the Gulf and the Pacific
all turn up hundreds or thousands of kilometres from their sources. Moving
goods that far requires more than a road. It requires agreements about
safe passage, shared protocols for meeting strangers, and people willing to
be responsible for keeping them.

The Haudenosaunee Confederacy is the clearest political example. Five
nations that had been fighting one another — Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga,
Cayuga and Seneca — bound themselves under the Great Law of Peace into a
council with rules about who spoke, in what order, and how consensus was
reached. The Tuscarora joined much later, making Six Nations. This was a
functioning federal arrangement between sovereign nations, and European
observers took note of it.

> [!example] Ask the harder question about influence
> You will meet the claim that the Confederacy influenced the framers of the
> American Constitution. There is real evidence of contact and admiration —
> Benjamin Franklin knew of the League and wrote about it. There is also
> reasonable disagreement among historians about how far that admiration
> shaped the document itself. Both parts belong in your answer. Saying "the
> Constitution came from the Haudenosaunee" is too strong; saying the framers
> knew nothing of Indigenous government is false. This is exactly the kind of
> question [[Interpretation and Historiography]] exists to handle.

## Cooperation as a political weapon

The colonial resistance of the 1760s and 1770s worked because people who had
almost nothing else in common agreed to act together. Non-importation
agreements asked merchants to forgo profit, and asked households — in
practice, the women who did the buying and the weaving — to do without
British cloth and tea. Committees of Correspondence knitted thirteen separate
political conversations into one. The Continental Congress, first meeting in
1774, gave that coordination an address.

None of this was unanimous. Colonists who stayed loyal to the Crown were
neighbours, not foreigners, and boycotts were enforced on them with pressure
and sometimes with violence. Cooperation among one group is frequently
coercion for another, and a good answer says which group it means.

[[Historical Significance]] to argue which cooperative act mattered most.
