A photo you posted years ago, at a birthday party, a protest, or just
walking down a street, might already be sitting in a database that lets a
stranger with a phone find your name. That is not hypothetical. It
happened, at scale, and Canadian regulators — including BC's own —
investigated it and said so in writing.

### What Clearview AI did

Clearview AI, a US company, built a facial recognition tool by scraping
more than three billion images of people from the public internet and
social media, without asking anyone's permission. It sold access to law
enforcement agencies and other clients, letting them upload a photo of a
stranger's face and get back a list of matching profiles and the personal
information attached to them. The RCMP trialled the service before
ending its contract in July 2020.

### The joint investigation

Canada's federal Privacy Commissioner investigated Clearview AI jointly
with the provincial privacy commissioners of British Columbia, Alberta,
and Quebec. Their findings, released in February 2021, concluded that
Clearview's practices amounted to "mass identification and surveillance"
of Canadians and violated federal and provincial privacy law — including
BC's own Personal Information Protection Act. The commissioners
specifically rejected Clearview's defence that photos posted publicly
online were fair game to scrape without consent: posting a photo
publicly, they found, is not the same as consenting to have your face
permanently indexed in a facial recognition database.

Clearview refused to comply with the recommendations that followed — it
would not agree to stop collecting Canadians' images or delete what it
already held. Because federal recommendations are not legally binding,
BC's Information and Privacy Commissioner, alongside Alberta's and
Quebec's, issued their own **binding orders** in December 2021. BC's
order, issued by then-Commissioner Michael McEvoy, required Clearview to
stop offering its facial recognition service to clients in British
Columbia, and to use its **best efforts** to stop collecting images of
people in BC and delete the biometric data it had already collected — a
weaker instruction than an outright guarantee, and worth noticing as its
own kind of limit on what an order can actually compel.

### Discussion Prompts

1. **Public doesn't mean permitted.** The commissioners rejected the idea
   that a publicly posted photo can be scraped freely. Do you agree with
   that reasoning? Where, if anywhere, is the line between "public" and
   "fair game to collect"?
2. **Non-binding vs. binding.** The federal recommendations alone did not
   force Clearview to change anything — only the provincial orders did.
   What does that tell you about how privacy protection actually works in
   Canada, and why does BC having its own Information and Privacy
   Commissioner matter?
3. **Building it vs. selling it.** Clearview didn't just collect the
   data — it built a searchable product and sold it to police forces. If
   you had written the scraper that built this database, at what point
   in the process would you have had a chance to say no?

### How we run this

Run this one as a four-corners debate.

- Label the four corners of the room: Strongly Agree, Agree, Disagree,
  Strongly Disagree.
- Read this statement aloud: *"A company should be allowed to scrape
  publicly posted photos to build a searchable facial recognition
  database, as long as its use by police is regulated."*
- Everyone moves to the corner matching their actual position — no
  standing in the middle.
- Give each corner five minutes to build its strongest case, using
  specifics from this page: the scraping itself, the "mass surveillance"
  finding, the binding-versus-non-binding distinction.
- Each corner sends one representative to make their case to the room, in
  order from Strongly Agree to Strongly Disagree.
- After all four have spoken, anyone who was persuaded may physically
  move to a new corner — and should be ready to say what changed their
  mind.
