Land is a factor of production, and the one that does not respond to
demand. Labour can be trained and capital can be built, but the supply of
farmland near a city, of a watershed, or of a fish stock is fixed or
slow-moving, and some of it does not return once converted. That is
scarcity in its hardest form, and why land-use decisions get fought over.

You can see the pressure in housing. CMHC's 2025 supply-gap framework
estimates Canada needs between 430,000 and 480,000 new units a year to 2035
— up to 4.8 million over the decade — to restore the affordability of 2019,
against 259,028 housing starts in 2025 and a CMHC forecast of 241,400 in
2026. Every unit occupies land that had another use, so the argument over
which land is an argument about opportunity cost with a map attached.

## Efficiency is not sustainability

A process is efficient when it gets the most output from given inputs; it
is sustainable when it can continue without exhausting what it draws on.
Those are different tests, and a process can pass one while failing the
other — a fishery can be run with excellent efficiency right up to the
point the stock collapses. Efficiency is measured over this year;
sustainability is a claim about the years after. Nor does the comparison
stay inside one country: moving production to wherever capital is used most
efficiently can raise measured output while shifting emissions somewhere
the accounts do not reach.

## Global externalities and citizen action

When economic activity crosses international borders, environmental and
human costs often fall outside domestic legal protections:

- **International environmental degradation.** Export-driven resource
  extraction, agricultural deforestation, e-waste dumping in developing
  nations, and cross-border greenhouse emissions create severe negative
  externalities borne disproportionately by vulnerable global populations.
- **Individual and group responses.** Consumers, workers, and advocacy
  groups attempt to remedy these harms through ethical boycotts and
  buycotts, fair-trade certifications, supply-chain due diligence, and
  international environmental litigation.
- **Assessing effectiveness.** Evaluating these initiatives requires
  examining whether voluntary certification schemes genuinely change producer
  behaviour or merely create high-margin niche markets, and whether citizen
  pressure succeeds in compelling multinational firms to adopt enforceable
  environmental and labour standards.

## Before you judge a development proposal

Copy these questions into your notebook and answer them in writing. Nothing
here can be ticked off — each is a question you must answer with evidence.

- [ ] What is being produced, and who wants it?
- [ ] Which factors does it consume, and are any used up irreversibly?
- [ ] Who receives the benefits, and over what period?
- [ ] Who bears the costs, and are they party to the decision?
- [ ] What does this site look like in twenty years, with and without it?
- [ ] Which of these effects appears in a price, and which does not?

That last question is the whole of [[Externalities]]; whether more output
should be the objective at all is [[Is Growth the Right Goal|Is Growth the Right Goal?]].
