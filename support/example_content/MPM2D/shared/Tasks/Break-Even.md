> [!abstract] At a glance
> Pairs, marked individually · three working periods in Week 5 · pitches
> shared at the end of that week · one page, one graph

## What you are making

Your pair invents a small venture — a bake sale, a car wash, a print
run of a zine, anything with real costs and a real price. You model its
cost and its revenue as a [[Linear Systems|linear system]], find the
point where the two lines cross, and — this is the heart of it — say
what that point *means* for your venture. Then you stress-test the
model: what happens to the break-even point if the price drops a
dollar?

You finish with a **one-page pitch**: the venture, the two equations,
the graph with the break-even point marked, and your recommendation —
written like advice for a friend about to run this venture for real.

## How to work

1. Invent the venture. Name its fixed costs (the table rental, the
   supplies bought up front), its cost per item, and its price. Keep
   the numbers honest — look prices up.
2. Write the two equations: cost as a line, revenue as a line. This is
   [[Crossing Paths]] again, except this time you build the lines.
3. Find the intersection two ways — on a graph and by algebra, using
   [[Solving Systems Algebraically]] — and check that the answers
   agree. Substitution or elimination both work; pick whichever your
   equations invite.
4. Interpret the point. How many items until you break even? What do
   the regions to its left and right mean in dollars? A coordinate
   pair is not an answer until it is a sentence about the venture.
5. Stress-test. Drop the price by a dollar and re-solve. Raise a cost.
   Report how the break-even point moves, and what that tells your
   venture to worry about most.
6. Assemble the pitch — [[Showing Your Thinking]] applies to every
   step that survives onto the page.

The working periods are class time, and the conferences inside them are
how the evidence that never reaches the page gets gathered — see
[[How Marks Work]].

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like in your pitch |
| --- | --- |
| A believable venture | Costs and price are researched, not invented |
| Two honest equations | Each equation is tied to a real cost or charge |
| Agreement by two routes | Graph and algebra find the same point |
| An interpreted point | The break-even is a sentence, not a coordinate |
| A real stress test | At least one change is re-solved, not guessed |
| Visible reasoning | A reader can follow every step without you there |

Your mark for this task is your own, not the pair's. It comes from the
equation you built and initialled, the stress test you ran, and what you
tell me when I come round in the working period and ask each of you
separately. One pitch, two marks, and they need not match: a pair is here
to make the model better, not to average a grade.

## Reflect

End the pitch with three sentences: which change moved your break-even
point the most, and would you actually run this venture? A model that
changed its maker's mind was worth building.

> [!success]- If you cannot pick a venture (click to expand)
> Sell the last thing you spent money on. A snack, a sticker, a
> playlist slot — anything with a cost and a price makes two lines,
> and two lines are all this task needs.

  Stuck: two equations, no units anywhere, and an argument about the
