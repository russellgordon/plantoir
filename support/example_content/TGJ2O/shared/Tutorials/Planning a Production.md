Every product this newsroom ships was planned or was lucky. This page is
how the planning is done, and it takes about twenty minutes for a story
and an hour for an issue.

## Start with objectives, not ideas

An idea is "something about the new turf field". An **objective** says
what the finished thing will be and who it is for:

> "A 500-word news story plus three photographs, for the school
> community, explaining who paid for the turf field and when it opens,
> published on the site before the first home game on the 14th."

Notice what that sentence contains: the product, the audience, the
purpose, and the deadline. If any of the four is missing, the project
will drift.

## Then performance criteria — how you will know it is good

Criteria are testable, and you write them before you start so that they
cannot be adjusted afterwards to match what you managed:

- At least two named sources, one of them the person who signed the
  contract.
- Every figure attributed to a document, not to a rumour.
- Photographs showing the field in use, not an empty rectangle.
- Filed by 4 p.m. on the 12th, so it can be edited and still publish.

## Then constraints, named honestly

Constraints are the things that will shape the work whether or not you
acknowledge them:

| Constraint | Example in this newsroom |
| --- | --- |
| **Time** | Six periods before the deadline, three of them shared with another shoot |
| **Cost** | A print run has a budget; a colour page costs more than a mono one |
| **Technology** | One good microphone; the interview happens where it can be recorded |
| **Access** | The trustee will not be available until Thursday |
| **Skill** | Nobody on the bench has cut a multi-camera sequence before |
| **Policy and law** | Consent and releases, per [[Releases and Consent]] |

A plan that ignores a constraint does not remove it; it just moves the
discovery to the worst possible moment.

## Sketch it — the part students skip

Draw the thing before you make it. Three formats cover almost
everything:

- **A storyboard** for video: one box per shot, with the framing and
  what is said. Six boxes beat six paragraphs.
- **A layout thumbnail** for print: the page at postcard size, with
  blocks for headline, photo, and text — enough to see whether the
  front page has a focal point before anything is typeset.
- **A flow chart or run sheet** for a production: what happens in what
  order, who does it, and where the hand-offs are.

Sketches are also the fastest way to compare alternatives. Two
thumbnails settle an argument about a front page in ninety seconds; two
finished layouts take two periods and somebody's feelings.

> [!tip] The planning document that fits on one page
> Objective, criteria, constraints, sketch, and a schedule with names
> against each step. Pin it on the newsroom board. Every task in this
> course expects one, and [[The Production Pipeline]] shows where it
> sits in the wider workflow.
