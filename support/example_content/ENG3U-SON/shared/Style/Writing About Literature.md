A handful of conventions apply to almost everything you write about a text
in this course. None of them are arbitrary — each one exists because the
alternative genuinely confuses a reader.

## Titles: italics or quotation marks

A long, stand-alone work gets *italics*: *Indian Horse*, *Hamlet*. A short
work published inside something bigger gets "quotation marks": "Boys and
Girls," "The Lottery," "Borders," "Happy Endings." The test is simple — does
this thing usually get published on its own, or as part of a collection?

## The literary present

Write about what happens in a text as though it is always happening, right
now, every time someone reads it:

- Not: *Hamlet learned that his uncle killed his father.*
- Instead: *Hamlet learns that his uncle killed his father.*

This is called the literary present, and it applies even to events that
"happened" earlier in the plot than the moment you're discussing. Saul
Indian Horse remembers his childhood — he does not "remembered" it.

## Quoting without summarizing

A quotation should do work a paraphrase couldn't — capture a writer's exact
word choice, rhythm, or structure. If you can say the same thing in your own
words without losing anything, paraphrase instead and save the quotation
mark for a moment that earns it.

Every quotation gets introduced, not dropped in cold:

> Weak: "Just a girl." (Munro 15)
>
> Better: When her father dismisses her competence with "just a girl"
> (Munro 15), the narrator hears, for the first time, what she is being
> sorted into.

## Citing what you quote

This course uses MLA in-text citation — author's last name and a page
number, in parentheses, right after the quotation: `(Wagamese 112)`. See
[[MLA In-Text Citation]] for the full mechanics, including how to handle a
play (act, scene, and line, not a page number) and a source with no page
numbers at all, like most media texts.

## What analysis is not

Analysis is not a retelling of what happened. A paragraph that spends four
sentences summarizing the plot and one sentence saying what it means has the
ratio backwards. Assume your reader has read the text; your job is to show
them something about it they might have missed, not to prove you read it.

> [!tip] A fast check
> If you could delete a sentence from your paragraph and a classmate who
> hasn't read the text would still follow your argument, that sentence is
> summary. If deleting it would break the argument, it's analysis.
