DNA and RNA are polymers of nucleotides — a pentose sugar, a negatively
charged phosphate group ($-PO_4^{3-}$), and a nitrogenous base. Nucleotides
polymerise by condensation reactions between the 3′-hydroxyl ($-OH$) group
of one sugar and the 5′-phosphate of the next, forming phosphodiester linkages.
The differences between DNA and RNA are small and consequential:

| | DNA | RNA |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sugar | 2′-deoxyribose | Ribose |
| Strands | Double helix (antiparallel) | Single-stranded (dynamic secondary folds) |
| Bases | A, T, G, C | A, U, G, C |
| Cellular role | Long-term genetic storage | Information transfer (mRNA), translation adapter (tRNA), catalytic ribosome core (rRNA) |

## Why DNA stores and RNA works

Deoxyribose lacks one 2′-hydroxyl group, which makes DNA chemically far more
resistant to alkaline hydrolysis — an evolutionary advantage for an archival
genome. RNA's reactive 2′-hydroxyl group allows it to adopt flexible tertiary
conformations with enzymatic properties (ribozymes), which is why the peptidyl
transferase centre of the ribosome is catalytic RNA rather than protein.

That detail is the strongest evidence for the RNA world hypothesis: the
oldest machinery in the cell is made of the less stable, versatile molecule.

## ATP

Also a modified adenine RNA nucleotide with three phosphate groups. Its energy
is not "stored in the bond" in the way textbooks once said — it is released
because the hydrolysis products ($\ce{ADP + P_i}$) are far more stable,
resonance-stabilised, and better hydrated than the reactant.
