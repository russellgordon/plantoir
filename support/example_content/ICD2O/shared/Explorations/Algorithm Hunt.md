You ran a dozen algorithms before first period — you just did not
call them that. A locker combination. The rule for when the
crosswalk is yours. The order you get dressed in. This hunt sends you
out to catch five of them in the wild and pin down what, exactly,
makes each one an algorithm rather than just a habit.

## The task

Over one day, find and document five algorithms you actually follow
— not ones you read about. For each: the steps, written so a
stranger could follow them; what the input is; what the result is;
and one place where the steps *branch* or *repeat*. At least one
specimen must come from outside a screen. Bring all five back — they
become raw material for [[The Algorithm Field Guide]].

> [!question]- Getting started (click to expand)
> - Stuck at zero? Watch your own hands the next time you open your
>   locker, transfer buses, or shuffle a deck. Steps you never chose
>   consciously are the best specimens.
> - Ask of each candidate: could I hand these steps to someone else
>   and get the same result? If yes, you may have caught one.
> - Game rules, recipes, tie-breakers, and "who goes first" rituals
>   are algorithm-rich territory.

## What tends to surface

Comparing specimens, the class starts drawing a line: some steps are
precise enough to hand over, and some quietly rely on judgement —
and that line is the definition this course spends a semester on.
The branches you found are [[Conditionals]] before the name; the
repeats are [[Loops]]; and [[Algorithms in Everyday Life]] picks up
exactly where your five specimens leave off.

## An extension

Take your cleanest specimen and write it as numbered steps a
[[Human Robot]] could execute. Where it breaks — and it will — mark
the judgement your written steps were smuggling in.

> [!note] The answer is not on this page
> There is no master list of correct algorithms to check against —
> every hunter's day is different. The class builds the definition
> together, out of the specimens everyone brings back.
