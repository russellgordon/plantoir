The first game you shot for [[The Athletics Package]] taught you the
difference between taking a photo and making one. Taking is pointing at
what is happening. Making is arranging three things before it happens:
light, moment, and position.

## Light, moment, position

**Light** is the raw material — a camera records nothing else. Notice
where it comes from before you shoot: light from behind you flattens a
face, light from the side sculpts it, light from behind the subject
makes a silhouette. In a gym you rarely control the light, but you
always control which side of it you stand on.

**Moment** is when. Every action has a peak — the ball at fingertips,
the mouth open mid-cheer — and a photograph one second later is a
different, weaker fact. Photographers call the instant when everything
aligns the decisive moment.[^1]

**Position** is where you are, chosen early. The photographers under
the basket did not get lucky; they predicted where the story would
happen and walked there before it did.

## Sports photography is anticipation

By the time you *see* the peak of the action, it is over — your
reaction time has already spent it. So sports shooters aim where the
play is about to arrive and fire as it enters the frame. Knowing the
game is a photographic skill: if you understand what a corner kick
usually produces, your lens is waiting at the far post.

The same thinking gives you shutter speed without any arithmetic. A
photograph is a slice of time: a thin slice freezes motion, a thick
slice lets it smear into blur. Dim gyms push the camera toward thick
slices, which is why gym shots blur — and why the anticipated peak of a
jump, where the athlete hangs still for an instant, is the sharpest
moment available. Freeze action when the fact matters; allow blur when
the speed *is* the story. Bring the results to
[[Photo Critique Practice]] and see which choice each frame made — then
copy the keepers somewhere safe, as [[Backing Up Your Media]] insists,
because a made photograph cannot be remade.

Where things sit inside the frame is its own discipline, stated cleanly
in [[Composition and Framing]].

[^1]: The phrase belongs to the French photographer Henri
    Cartier-Bresson, who spent a career arguing that a photograph is
    made in the fraction of a second when meaning and geometry line up.
