**Due near the end of Unit 1**, handed in at the close of the revision
period. Individual. About 800 words, written for somebody who has to make
a decision and has four minutes.

A brief is not an essay. It is the document a minister's staffer, a
councillor, or a board chair actually reads: what the issue is, who wants
what, and what the reader should know before the meeting. Every word that
does not help them decide is in the way.

## What you choose

One civic issue, live now, that you can find real sources on. It may be
local, national or global, but it must be specific enough to have a
decision attached — not "climate change" but a particular decision about
a particular thing. If you cannot name a body that could act on it, the
issue is too broad.

## The structure

1. **The issue**, in three sentences. What has to be decided, by whom,
   and by when.
2. **Why it matters**, and to whom. Say who is affected and how many, with
   a figure and a source.
3. **The positions** — at least three groups, each stated in their own
   terms. One must be a group you disagree with, and it has to be their
   strongest version, not the version that is easy to answer.
4. **The values in play.** Which of democracy, human rights, freedom, the
   rule of law or the common good are pulling against each other here?
   Name them; see [[Values That Pull Against Each Other]].
5. **What is not known**, and what evidence would settle it.
6. **Sources**, cited so the reader could check any claim in under a
   minute.

Your own opinion does not appear. This one is deliberately withheld —
[[Where You Stand Now]] is where you take a position, and writing a brief
that does not leak your view is harder and more useful than it sounds.

## How it is marked

| Criterion | What earns the marks |
| --- | --- |
| Question | The issue is stated as something decidable, with a decision-maker named |
| Evidence | Real sources, more than one kind, at least one primary — a bill, a budget, a minute, a decision |
| Perspectives | Three groups whose members would each accept your description of them |
| Values | The value conflict is named specifically, not gestured at |
| Communication | A busy reader could act on this. Length is a constraint, not a target |

> [!warning] The commonest failure is a brief with two sides
> Almost no real civic issue has two sides. It has a set of groups with
> partly overlapping interests, at least one of which is a government
> body with its own position. A brief with "supporters" and "opponents"
> is describing an argument on the internet, not the issue.

Build it from [[Your Civic Notebook]], check every source against
[[Judging Civic Information]], and use [[Who Decided This|Who Decided This?]] if you
cannot work out who holds the power.

OBSERVE — Unit 1, Day 10, the work period
  Watch for: where the three positions come from. A student writing them
  with a source open in front of them, and one writing them from memory
  of what "those people" think, hand in three equally tidy paragraphs.
  Going well: they go back to a source and change a sentence about the
  group they disagree with.
  Stuck: the paragraph on the group they disagree with is written fastest
  of the three, and nothing is open on the desk.
  This is where A1.2 is visible — sources chosen so that they reflect
  multiple perspectives, rather than perspectives supplied from memory.
  Record: three columns on the class list, a tick when you see a source

TALK — Unit 1, Day 10, the conferences already on that agenda
  Ask: "Which of your three groups would be angriest at how you have
  described them, and what would they say you got wrong?"

  Record: a name and one phrase on the conference sheet, before the next
