The United States removed Native nations from the eastern half of the
continent using law, treaty, and finally soldiers, and it did so while
insisting the process was lawful. Both parts of that sentence are the
history. The nations involved did not accept it, and the forms their refusal
took — courtrooms, petitions, diplomacy, armed defence — are as much a part
of the record as the removals themselves.

## The Act, and the argument about it

The Indian Removal Act of 1830 authorized the president to negotiate the
exchange of Native land in the east for territory west of the Mississippi.
It passed narrowly and against loud opposition in Congress, which matters:
Americans at the time knew what was being proposed and a substantial number
of them said it was wrong.

The Cherokee Nation answered in the American legal system, on American terms.
It had a written constitution, a newspaper, and counsel. In 1832 the Supreme
Court held in *Worcester v. Georgia* that Georgia's laws had no force within
Cherokee territory. The decision was not enforced. A famous line in which
President Jackson dares the Chief Justice to enforce it himself is almost
certainly not something he said — but the outcome it describes is accurate,
and that distinction between a good story and a documented fact is one you
should carry through this whole course.

Removal then proceeded on a treaty the Cherokee Nation had not agreed to.
The Treaty of New Echota was signed by a small faction with no authority to
sign it, and Principal Chief John Ross carried a protest with thousands of
signatures to Congress. It was set aside.

## What the removal was

> More than 16,000 Cherokee Indian people were forcibly evicted from their
> homelands. … More than a thousand Cherokee — particularly the old, the
> young, and the infirm — died during their trip west, hundreds more
> deserted from the detachments, and an unknown number — perhaps several
> thousand — perished from the consequences of the forced migration.
>
> — National Park Service, Trail of Tears National Historic Trail

Notice how that body reports its own uncertainty: a documented minimum for
deaths in transit, and an explicitly unknown number for deaths that followed.
You will see round figures elsewhere. Prefer the wording of the institution
that keeps the trail, and say why the count cannot be exact.

The Choctaw, Muscogee, Chickasaw and Seminole were removed in the same
programme. The Seminole fought instead, in wars that ran for years in the
Florida swamps at enormous cost to the United States, alongside Black
Seminoles who had escaped slavery and had everything to lose from removal.

## Resistance did not end there

Earlier, Tecumseh and his brother Tenskwatawa had tried to build a
confederacy of nations across the Ohio country on the principle that no
single nation could sell land belonging to all. Later, on the plains and in
the northwest, the Lakota and their allies and the Nimíipuu fought the army
over the same question in the 1870s. The reservation system created by
federal legislation from the 1850s onward was the container built to hold the
outcome.

read John Ross's petition as its authors meant it.
