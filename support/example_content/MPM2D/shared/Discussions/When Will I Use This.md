This course can answer the oldest question in math class without
dodging. Every parabola with a vertex is a *best possible* question
in disguise — largest enclosure, highest arc, the price that
maximises revenue — and optimisation is quietly everywhere adults
make decisions, which is what [[Maximum Enclosure]] rehearses.
Triangulation, the angle-and-ratio reasoning of
[[Inaccessible Heights]], is how GPS locates your phone and how
surveyors lay out roads. And whenever two options grow at different
rates — plans, jobs, prices — the decision point is the intersection
of two lines: [[Break-Even]] wearing work clothes.

The second answer is about transfer: you may never complete a square
after this course, but you will spend a lifetime modelling messy situations,
checking claims before trusting them, and arguing from constraints.
That training happens here whether the algebra resurfaces or not.

Questions worth arguing about:

1. "When will I use this?" is sometimes an honest question and
   sometimes code for something else — frustration, being lost, a
   fair complaint. How could a classmate tell which they just heard?
2. A GPS receiver knows nothing but its distances from satellites, yet
   it finds you. Why does triangle reasoning keep appearing wherever
   people must measure what they cannot reach?
3. List three "best possible" questions from your own week that are
   secretly vertex questions. Whose job is it to notice they are?
4. Nobody asks a basketball player when they will use wind sprints.
   What is different about mathematics — or is anything?
5. Suppose the honest answer for some topic really is "you,
   personally, may never use this directly". Does the transfer answer
   rescue it, or is that a dodge too? Defend your position.

The test of today's talk is what you do at [[Break-Even]] and
[[Inaccessible Heights]] — tasks where the mathematics is not dressed
up as useful, but simply is.
