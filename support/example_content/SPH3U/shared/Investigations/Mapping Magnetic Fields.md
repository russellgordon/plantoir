> [!abstract] At a glance
> **Time:** one class. **Groups of three.** Bring your right hand; you
> will be using it constantly.

Field lines are a drawing convention, not a thing. This is the class where
you produce the drawing from evidence instead of copying it, and where
the right-hand rule stops being a mnemonic and becomes something you have
checked.

## Safety

The battery packs are low voltage, but a wire shorted straight across a
cell gets hot in seconds. Every current-carrying circuit gets a resistor
or a lamp in it, and nothing stays connected while you are not looking at
it.

## Station A — a bar magnet

Place the magnet under paper, sprinkle iron filings, tap the paper gently.
Then check the pattern with a plotting compass at eight points around the
magnet and mark the direction the needle points at each.

**Record:** a sketch with arrows, not just filings. Filings show you where
the field is; only the compass shows you which WAY it goes.

## Station B — a long straight conductor

Run a vertical wire through a horizontal card, put four compasses around
it, and switch the current on.

1. Sketch the compass directions with the current flowing one way.
2. **Reverse the current** and sketch again.
3. Curl your right hand around the wire, thumb along the conventional
   current. Does the field go the way your fingers do — both times?

## Station C — a solenoid

Wind 30 turns around a tube and energise it.

1. Map the field with a compass, inside and outside.
2. Identify which end is the north pole, and state how you knew.
3. Reverse the current and confirm the poles swap.
4. Grip the solenoid with your right hand, fingers following the
   conventional current in the coils. Your thumb should point at the
   north pole.

## The distinction that trips people up

| | Conventional current | Electron flow |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direction | From + to − outside the source | From − to + |
| Why it exists | Chosen before anyone knew what a charge carrier was | What is physically happening in a metal |
| Which hand | **Right** hand rules | **Left** hand rules, same physics |

Both descriptions predict the same compass needle. This course uses
conventional current and the right hand throughout — but if a textbook or
a video contradicts your answer, check which convention it used before you
assume you were wrong.

## Analysis

1. From your Station B sketches, state the rule connecting current
   direction and field direction, in your own words, without saying
   "right-hand rule".
2. Where was the field strongest in Station C, and what is your evidence?
3. Predict what doubling the number of turns would do, and say what you
   would measure to check it.

> [!tip] What to keep for your portfolio
> The Station B pair of sketches — same wire, opposite currents — is the
> cleanest evidence in this unit that the field direction is *caused* by
> the current direction rather than merely correlated with it.
