Eight poems, spread across the course rather than blocked, each read beside
something else.

| Poem | Poet | Read beside |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "This Is a Photograph of Me" | Margaret Atwood | The short-fiction unit: what a text withholds |
| "The Cariboo Horses" | Al Purdy | Setting as claim rather than scenery |
| "Blues Spiritual for Mammy Prater" | Dionne Brand | A photograph that becomes an argument |
| "Letter to Sir John A. Macdonald" | Marilyn Dumont (Métis) | *The Inconvenient Indian* |
| "I Lost My Talk" | Rita Joe (Mi'kmaw) | The residential-school material in *Monkey Beach* |
| "Tomorrow, and tomorrow" (5.5) | Shakespeare | Read as a poem, out of the play |
| "Invocation for the Bones in My Back" | Gregory Scofield (Métis) | Inheritance, and what a body carries |
| One of your own choosing | You | The last week: bring one and defend it |

## How we read a poem here

1. **Aloud, twice**, before anything is said.
2. **Annotate**: the turn, the line breaks, the sound.
3. **One claim each**, with a line as evidence.
4. **A third reading aloud**, by somebody with a different reading —
   because a reading aloud is an interpretation.

## What to look for

- **The turn**, and what it costs.
- **Line breaks**: the word left hanging is being pointed at.
- **Sound**: where the poem slows, and for what.
- **Syntax**: a sentence spread over six lines is doing something a short
  one cannot.
- **The last line**, which most poems are built backwards from.

> [!note] The Shakespeare in the list
> Reading 5.5 as a free-standing poem, and then back in its place in the
> play, is the fastest way to see how much of a speech's meaning comes
> from where it sits.
