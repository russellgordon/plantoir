> [!abstract] At a glance
> Crews from Unit 2, one printed part per crew, every member evaluated
> on their own work · thirteen periods, one 3D printer shared by the
> class · a model, a first print tested against numbers, a better
> second print, and a process record

## What you are making

A piece of gear that fixes a real problem your Unit 2 crew had on
[[The Athletics Package]]: a clamp that holds a phone on a tripod, a
clip that keeps a microphone cable off the floor, a wallet that stops
memory cards going missing. Your crew is also your user — you know
exactly what went wrong, and you will use the finished rig on a shoot
before the unit ends.

The crew prints **one** part, twice: a first version, tested against
numbers, and a second version changed because of what the test found.
There is one printer for the whole class, so the print is small by
design: **no more than thirty minutes and fifteen grams**, as the
slicer estimates it. Those two limits are criteria like any other — a
rig that needs three hours of printer is a rig this room cannot make.

## What is yours, and what is the crew's

The crew shares the problem and the printed part. The evaluation is
individual, and each of these carries your name:

- **your candidate model** — every member models a first version; the
  crew chooses one to print, against the criteria, and says why;
- **your test log** — you test the printed part yourself, against every
  criterion, and write down what you measured;
- **your change** — you propose and model one change for the second
  version, traced to a number in your test log;
- **your process record** — written by you, for the next crew who will
  make this rig;
- **the conference** on Day 17.

## How to work

The days are Unit 3's.

1. **The problem (Day 10).** Pitch it with the shop version of
   [[One-Minute Pitch]]: what the problem is, who has it, and how you
   will know it is fixed.
2. **The brief (Day 11).** Interview your crew as users, then one
   person outside it who uses the same gear differently — a left-handed
   shooter, someone who films outdoors, someone who has never held a
   tripod. Find what others have already shared with
   [[Finding Answers Online]] — and their licences — and write the
   brief in the shape
   [[Planning a Production]] shows: objective, criteria (at least three
   you can measure, including the time and grams limits), constraints,
   sketch.
3. **Numbers first (Day 12).** Read [[3D Printing]], then measure the
   class coupons in [[The Tolerance Coupon]]. The clearance goes into
   every fit you design.
4. **Model (Days 13–14).** Build your candidate from a dimensioned
   drawing, the way [[Modelling in 3D]] shows. On Day 14 the crew
   chooses one candidate, slices it, and books its slot in the queue
   using [[The Production Pipeline]].
5. **Print and wait well (Days 14–17).** While the printer works
   through the queue, the class runs the [[Made in Canada]] discussion
   and a waste audit using [[The Footprint of Media]].
6. **Test (Day 17).** Test the first version against every criterion
   and log the numbers, using [[Quality Control]]. Then the checkpoint
   conference, and straight after it each member models their own
   change as v2, traced to a number in their test log.
7. **Reprint (Days 18–20).** On Day 18 every crew chooses the change
   the data supports best — crews 1 and 2 in the first ten minutes,
   because they print that day; crews 3 to 5 later in the period, and
   crew 6 once it has tested — then slices it, and it goes into the
   printer in its queue slot. A print that fails is data too —
   [[Getting Unstuck]] has the move.
8. **Credit and record (Days 19–21).** If you remixed a shared model,
   credit it as [[Copyright and Fair Dealing]] requires. Then write
   your process record and judge your work against the criteria with
   [[Judging Your Own Work]].
9. **Use it (Day 21).** Your crew uses the rig on a real shoot — a
   [[Record a Standup|standup]] — and the printer gets its maintenance.

## The printer's time budget

Six crews, one printer, and a printer that runs only while somebody
responsible is in the room — which in this plan means only during our
periods. Each print is budgeted at thirty minutes, so two fit in a
period with time to change over:

| Day | In the printer | Printer minutes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 14 | First version, crew 1 | 30 |
| 15 | First versions, crews 2 and 3 | 60 |
| 16 | First versions, crews 4 and 5 | 60 |
| 17 | First version, crew 6 — then free for a reprint | 30 (+45 spare) |
| 18 | Second versions, crews 1 and 2 | 60 |
| 19 | Second versions, crews 3 and 4 | 60 |
| 20 | Second versions, crews 5 and 6 | 60 |
| 21 | Free for reprints of anything that failed | 60 spare |

Twelve prints, six hours of printing, and nearly two hours of slack for
failures, all inside class time. Two things make the table work: the v2
models are made on Day 17, so each print day starts printing within
ten minutes; and the class's two or three tolerance coupons were
printed before the unit started. If your school allows supervised
printing outside class, the spare slots become breathing room.

Thirty minutes and fifteen grams is a quick printer's pace. **Set the
two limits from your own printer's slicer times** before the unit
starts — slice a small clamp and see what it says — and if your printer
is slower, shrink the parts, not the testing.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like in your work |
| --- | --- |
| A real problem | The rig answers a problem your crew actually had, in the crew's words |
| Criteria that can fail | At least three measurable criteria, written before modelling, including time and grams |
| Built from numbers | Your model matches a dimensioned drawing, with the class clearance added to every fit |
| Tested honestly | Your test log records what you measured, including what failed |
| A change with a reason | Your change traces to a number in your test log |
| Better, and you can show it | The second version beats the first on the criterion you targeted |
| Credit given | Any shared model you started from is named, with its licence |
| A record someone can use | The next crew could build a better rig from your process record |
| Materials handled right | Supports and failed prints go where [[Safety in the Shop]] says |

## The process record

One page, written for the next crew who will make this rig. Tell them:
the problem and who has it; what you tried; what the first print's test
found, with the numbers; what you changed and why; what still is not
right. Then one paragraph on a bigger question — **what if every crew
in every school printed its own gear instead of buying it?** Name one
good effect on people, one harm, and one thing that would reduce the
harm.

## Reflect

Write a [[Newsroom Journal]] entry: which number in your test changed
your mind about your own design?

> [!success]- If your print fails (click to expand)
> It happens to everyone, and it is why the budget has spare slots.
> Photograph the failure, name what you saw, find the likely cause in
> [[3D Printing]], change one thing, and book the next spare slot. A
> failure you can explain is worth more in your process record than a
> print that worked by luck.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 3, Day 18, the first ten minutes, when crews 1 and 2
choose which member's change to print (crews 3 to 5 do the same later
in that period)
  Listen to how the choice is argued. Some members put their test log
  on the table and point at a number — "the clamp opened 2 mm too far,
  my change closes it by 1.5" — and the crew weighs changes against the
  criteria; some argue for the change that looks best on screen, a
  rounded corner or a thicker wall, with the logs still closed. Both
  crews print a v2 that differs from v1. The criteria row "A change
  with a reason" asks for the first, and the file cannot tell you which
  happened; this conversation can. That is A3.4, refining the design
  from data collected in the development process.
  Going well: the logs are open, and the chosen change names its number.
  Stuck: the change is to something no criterion mentioned.
  Record: on the queue sheet, a tick beside each name — argued from the
  log / not from the log.

TALK — Unit 3, Day 17, the checkpoint conference already on that
agenda
  The criteria and the test log will be on the desk; do not ask them to
  read out the results. Ask:
  "Which number in this test surprised you — and what do you think
  caused it?"
  Then: "Your second print fails tomorrow. What do you do in the next
  ten minutes, and how do you stop it failing the same way twice?"
  A strong answer names a cause, not a symptom — "the hole shrank
  because printed holes come out small, and we had not added enough
  clearance" — and a plan that changes one thing and tests it. That is
  A3.1 (a challenge, thought through so it does not recur) and A2.2 (a
  factor that could sink the project, and a strategy ready for it),
  heard in conversation where the log only shows the outcome.
  Record: one line per student in the conference list — the cause they
  named, or a dash.

The product evidence is the second print, the test log, the model
files, and the process record handed in on Day 21. Those arrive on
their own.
