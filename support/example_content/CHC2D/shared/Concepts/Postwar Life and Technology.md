Between 1945 and 1982 the ordinary texture of life in Canada changed
faster than in any comparable stretch — where people lived, what they
owned, what work looked like, and what they expected next.

## The economy that paid for it

The postwar decades were unusually good for a large part of the
population: employment was high, real incomes rose, and resource
exports, manufacturing, and construction all grew. That is the backdrop
to everything on this page, and it is also why the slowdown of the
1970s — inflation, unemployment, and an oil shock at the same time —
felt like a rupture rather than an adjustment.

## What arrived, and what it changed

| Development | What it changed about daily life |
| --- | --- |
| Television | A shared national audience, and an argument about Canadian content |
| The private car and the highway | Where people could live, and how far from work |
| Suburban housing at scale | The shape of cities, and who could get a mortgage |
| Household appliances | Hours of domestic labour, and the assumptions about who did it |
| The jet aircraft | Distance, for people who could afford it |
| Computing, in institutions first | Records, payroll, and later everything else |

## The part worth arguing about

Technology is easy to narrate as arrival: a thing appears and life
improves. The historical question is harder — **who got it first, who
paid for it, and who was left outside it?** Suburban expansion depended
on mortgage rules that excluded some buyers. Highways were routed
through some neighbourhoods and not others. Television reached most
homes and represented a narrower Canada than actually existed.

> [!tip] Ask what the new thing replaced
> Every technology on that table displaced something — a streetcar
> system, a neighbourhood, a kind of work, an evening spent otherwise.
> The displaced thing is usually where the evidence about power is.

[[Building the Postwar State]] is the government half of this period;
this page is the private half, and the two were built together.
