> [!abstract] At a glance
> Individual · six class periods, drafted and rewritten · 900–1100 words
> · assessed on all four categories

## The task

One argument about *Romeo and Juliet*, proved with three passages, and
complicated once.

"Complicated once" means: somewhere in the essay you deal with the best
evidence *against* your reading. That single move is what separates a
level 3 from a level 4 at this grade, and it is learnable in an
afternoon.

## Choosing a question

Start from your annotations, not from a list. Questions that have worked:

- What does the play do with characters who are never left alone on
  stage?
- Is the Friar's failure one of judgement or of nerve?
- The play's imagery of light reverses in Act 3. What does the reversal
  argue?
- Whose tragedy does the Prologue say it is, and does the play agree with
  its own Prologue?

Bring your question to the conference in period 2; we will sharpen it
together. A question sharpened early saves two periods later.

## Structure

Thesis, three body paragraphs, the complication, a conclusion that says
what follows. [[The Essay]] has the detail; [[The Paragraph]] has the
shape of each unit; [[Writing About Literature]] has the conventions.

## How it is built

| Period | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Question chosen from your own annotations |
| 2 | Conference; passages gathered and paraphrased |
| 3 | Outline: thesis, three claims, the complication |
| 4 | Draft |
| 5 | You judge your own draft against the criteria table; then a partner marks every claim without evidence, and the weakest paragraph |
| 6 | Rewrite, read aloud, check every quotation and citation; the three sentences written in the last ten minutes |

## Three sentences, handed in with the essay

Written in the last ten minutes of period 6, on the front of the essay:

1. **Three things you did that you did not do for
   [[The Story Behind the Story]]** — one before you started writing, one
   while you were drafting, and one while rewriting — and which of the
   three actually helped.
2. **The stage that cost you the most**: choosing the question, finding
   the passages, ordering the claims, or the complication.
3. **The next thing to fix**, and where you will fix it.

"I planned more" is not a strategy and "all of it" is not a stage. Name
things somebody could check.

## How it is marked

| Category | What I am looking for |
| --- | --- |
| Knowledge | The play is known accurately, including the parts your argument does not need |
| Thinking | The thesis is arguable, the evidence supports it, and the complication is the real one |
| Communication | Paragraphs are ordered and cohesive; the reader never has to reread to follow |
| Application | Conventions of literary writing are used correctly, including verse quotation and citation |
| Thinking, in the three sentences | One strategy from before the writing, one from the drafting and one from the rewriting, all three actually named; the costly stage identified; and a next step precise enough to check |

> [!warning] The single most common failure
> Writing about the play in general. An essay that could have been
> written after watching a summary is an essay with no passages in it.
> Three passages, quoted short, analysed at length.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 2, Day 24, the last rewrite, while every quotation is
checked against the text
  Most of the essays you collect on Day 25 will have accurate quotations
  in them, and the page cannot tell you which were verified and which
  happened to be right. That is the whole of it: the checking leaves no
  trace in the product, and a student who has never done it will meet the
  examination with the same habit.
  Watch for: whether the play is open. Not the draft — the book, at a
  page, being turned. It is the one thing in this period you can see from
  the front of the room without moving.
  Going well: a page found, a quotation read where it sits, and then the
  student's own sentence retyped because the line did not say quite what
  they had made it say. That last move is the good one, and it is rarer
  than you expect.
  Stuck: a closed book beside an open document, however tidy the citation
  formatting becomes.
  Record: at minute twenty, walk the room once and write down only the
  names with a shut book. It is a short list, it takes as long as the
  walk, and it is the only thing worth keeping from the period.
  Evidence for C3.5: proofreading and correcting their own writing using
  the guidelines worked out with the teacher — the ones on Citing Sources
  that this period sends them to. Both of the behaviours above are that
  expectation; the essay shows you neither.

TALK — Unit 2, Day 20, the conferences already on that agenda, while the
room gathers and paraphrases passages
  The task page promises the question gets sharpened here. Sharpen it,
  and use these two to do it rather than asking what the question is.
  Ask: "What would have to be true in this play for your question to have
  a boring answer?"
  Then: "Who in this room would disagree with your answer, and what would
  they quote at you?"
  A strong first answer describes a version of the play in which the
  answer is obvious — a Friar who admits everything, a Juliet nobody
  threatens — and can then say what stops this play being that. A question
  that survives is arguable; one that cannot be made boring was never a
  question.
  That is one of C1.2's strategies, heard: the expectation asks for a
  variety of them, and this task supplies two — mining your own
  annotations in period 1, and this conference in period 2. The
  conference is the one that leaves nothing behind on paper. Do not
  record either answer as C1.4: identifying, sorting and ordering the
  claims is tomorrow's work, it is a different expectation, and it
  happens in writing.
  A strong second answer names a real person in the room and produces a
  passage on their behalf. That is the complication rehearsed out loud,
  the period before the outline that has to contain it, and a student who
  cannot do it is the one whose complication turns out to be a sentence
  beginning "on the other hand".
  Record: two words each on the conference list — the boring version, and
  the name of the person who disagrees. Anyone with neither gets ten more
  minutes with you in the drafting period on Day 22.

The product arrives on its own: the essay handed in on Unit 2, Day 25.
