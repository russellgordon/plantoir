Choose two of the four stories we've read this unit — "Boys and Girls,"
"The Lottery," "Borders," "Happy Endings" — and find the thread that
connects them: a theme, a technique, a kind of character, a way the ending
lands. Build an essay that argues for that connection, using real evidence
from both texts.

This is your first essay of the course. It doesn't need to be long — about
600–800 words — but every sentence in it should be doing something.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| A real thread, not a coincidence | The connection you argue for is something both writers actually built, not just a topic both stories happen to touch |
| Evidence from both texts | At least two specific moments or lines from EACH story, introduced and explained, not dropped in cold |
| A thesis that takes a position | Not "these two stories are similar," but a claim someone could disagree with |
| Literary present | Characters act, not acted — see [[Writing About Literature]] |
| Organized | Each paragraph does one job; the order builds toward something |

## How to work

1. **Day 15 — launch.** Pick your pairing and start a thesis.
   [[Integrating Evidence - The Quote Sandwich\|Integrating Evidence]] is today's workshop
   — you'll need it for the evidence you gather over the next few days.
2. **Day 16 — outline.** Bring your thesis and a rough plan; I'll circulate
   and give feedback on both before you draft.
3. **Day 17 — draft and peer conference.** Write a full draft, then trade
   with a partner for structured feedback. [[Editing and Proofreading]] is
   also today's workshop — proofread before you trade, not after.
4. **Day 18 — revise and hand in.** Act on the feedback from Day 16 and 17,
   check your draft against [[Judging Your Own Work]] on your own, then
   submit.

## The Process Note

Along with your essay, hand in a short Process Note — 4-6 sentences,
written on Day 18, after your revision. Answer: which strategy from this
week actually helped, and what would you do differently starting your next
essay? This is marked as part of the task, separately from the essay
itself, and it's evaluated on how specific and honest your answer is — not
on whether you liked the process. "It went fine" earns nothing; "the
outline checkpoint caught a thesis that was really two theses, and I'd
build the outline before drafting next time too" does the actual work.
