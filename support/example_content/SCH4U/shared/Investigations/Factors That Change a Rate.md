Iron rusts over years. Magnesium in acid is finished before you have put
the stopwatch down. Both reactions release energy, both go the way the
energy says they should, and one of them takes ten million times longer
than the other.

So "will it happen" and "how fast will it happen" are two separate
questions, and nothing you have learned so far answers the second one.
Today you take the second one apart, one variable at a time.

## What you are trying to find out

Four things are commonly said to change a reaction's rate:
**concentration**, **temperature**, **surface area**, and a
**catalyst**. That is a claim you can test, and the only honest way to
test it is to change one of them while holding the other three still.

Each team takes **one** factor and does it properly. Then the class
pools the results, and by the end of the period you will have four
well-controlled experiments instead of four half-controlled ones.

| Team | Factor | The version of the question you are answering |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Concentration | Does doubling the concentration double the rate, or do something else? |
| 2 | Temperature | How much faster per 10 °C, and is the effect the same size at every temperature? |
| 3 | Surface area | Is it the amount of solid that matters, or the amount of solid *exposed*? |
| 4 | Catalyst | Does the catalyst get used up, and does it change how much product you get? |

Notice that three of those four questions ask for a **size**, not a
direction. "It goes faster" is where Grade 10 stopped. This year the
answer is a ratio with a number in it.

## What you have to work with

Choose one reaction. All three work, and they measure rate in different
ways, which is itself worth noticing.

| Reaction | How you measure rate | Suits which factors |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Magnesium ribbon + dilute hydrochloric acid | Time for the ribbon to disappear completely | Concentration, temperature |
| Marble chips + dilute hydrochloric acid | Mass loss on a balance, timed | Surface area, concentration |
| Hydrogen peroxide decomposition | Volume of oxygen collected, timed | Catalyst, temperature |

- **Hydrochloric acid, 1.0 mol/L**, which you may dilute with distilled
  water to make the lower concentrations you need — show the
  calculations before the lab.
- **Magnesium ribbon**, cut to a stated length, and fine sandpaper.
- **Marble chips**, in two or three size grades, and a balance.
- **Hydrogen peroxide, 3%**, and **manganese(IV) oxide** as a catalyst.
- Water baths, gas syringes or an inverted measuring cylinder over
  water, conical flasks, thermometers, stopwatches.

Your design decisions, written and justified before the lab:

- **Your one independent variable, and the values you will give it.**
  At least four values, spread out. Two points make a line whether or
  not the relationship is linear, which is why two points are worth very
  little.
- **Your dependent variable, stated as a quantity with a unit.** "How
  fast it went" is not a variable. "Time in seconds for the ribbon to
  disappear" is. If you plan to report a rate rather than a time, say
  now how you will get from one to the other.
- **Every controlled variable, listed, with two things beside each
  one**: how you will hold it constant, and **which way** a failure to
  hold it constant would push your result. There are more of these than
  you think. Volume of acid, mass of solid, shape of the container,
  whether you swirl, the temperature of the room, the age of the acid,
  and the surface condition of the magnesium are all on the list.
- **Your replication plan.** How many trials at each value, and what
  agreement you will accept before moving on.
- **How you will get an *initial* rate** rather than an average over the
  whole run. This matters and most groups miss it: the concentration
  falls as the reaction proceeds, so the rate at the end is not the rate
  at the start, and an average over the whole run belongs to neither.

> [!danger] Hydrogen, hot water, peroxide, and one reaction left off the list
> - **Magnesium in acid produces hydrogen, which is flammable and forms
>   an explosive mixture with air.** No flames anywhere in the room, no
>   hot plates with exposed elements, and nothing that sparks. The gas is
>   invisible and it collects under anything you put over the flask.
> - **Nothing that makes a gas is ever sealed.** Not a stoppered flask,
>   not a syringe with the plunger held, not a bottle with a cap resting
>   on it. Both of the acid reactions here make gas fast, and a sealed
>   rigid container is a pressure vessel you built by accident.
> - **Dilute hydrochloric acid is still an acid.** It stings on broken
>   skin, it ruins clothing quietly overnight, and in an eye it is an
>   emergency. Eye protection from the first move to the last of the
>   cleanup.
> - **If you dilute the acid, add acid to water, never water to acid.**
>   Dilution releases heat; with the water already there it spreads
>   through a large volume, and the other way round it concentrates in a
>   few drops that can boil and spit acid back out at you. Do your
>   dilutions before you set up, at the bench, with the container on the
>   bench and not in your hand.
> - **Hot water baths, not flames.** Water at 60 °C scalds, hot
>   glassware looks exactly like cold glassware, and a flask lifted out
>   of a bath drips onto whatever is below it. Tongs or a cloth, and
>   assume anything near a bath is hot until you have watched it cool.
> - **Hydrogen peroxide bleaches skin, hair, and clothing**, and it is an
>   oxidiser — keep it away from anything combustible, including paper
>   towel. Rinse contact immediately and at length.
> - **Manganese(IV) oxide is a fine powder and is harmful if inhaled.**
>   Do not tip it from a height and do not blow it off the bench. Add it
>   with a spatula, close the container, and wash your hands afterwards.
> - **Waft, never sniff**, and nothing is tasted. Nothing returns to a
>   stock bottle. Check the pH of the waste and follow the route I give
>   you.
> - **One reaction is deliberately not on the list.** The classic
>   "disappearing cross" clock reaction, sodium thiosulfate with
>   hydrochloric acid, releases **sulfur dioxide** — a respiratory
>   irritant that is genuinely dangerous for anyone with asthma, in a
>   room where I cannot know everybody's history. It is a good
>   experiment and it belongs in a fume hood, not on twelve open
>   benches. If you want to see it, ask and I will run it at the front.
> - **Report every incident immediately**, however small.

## The prediction you write first

Before you touch a reagent, for **your** factor:

1. **A sketch graph** of your dependent variable against your
   independent variable, with both axes labelled, drawn from your
   expectation rather than from data. Straight line? Curve? Which way
   does it bend?
2. **A ratio.** If you double your variable, what happens to the rate?
   Commit to a number — "doubles", "quadruples", "increases by about
   half". This is the prediction the whole lab turns on.
3. **The controlled variable you think is most likely to escape from
   you**, and which way it would push your result if it did.
4. **What you would expect the total amount of product to do.** Not the
   rate — the amount. Write it down before you find out, because this is
   the one nearly everybody gets wrong.

> [!warning] Faster is not further, and this is the misconception to kill today
> The most common error in this unit is to treat "the reaction is
> faster" and "the reaction makes more product" as the same statement.
> They are unrelated.
>
> A catalyst gets you to the finish sooner. It does not move the finish
> line. Powdering a solid gets you there sooner. It does not change how
> much gas comes off, because the amount of gas was fixed by how much
> solid you weighed out. Warming a reaction gets you there sooner, and
> whether it changes the final amount is a genuinely separate question
> that Unit 4 will take up.
>
> So when you design your experiment, plan to measure **both** — how
> long it took, and how much you got. If your factor changes the first
> and leaves the second alone, that is a result worth reporting in those
> exact words, and it is the result the next class is built on.

Plus, on every single run, a row of controlled quantities recorded
rather than assumed: the temperature of the room and of the solution,
the volume of acid, the mass or length of the solid, and the time of
day. Recording a controlled variable is what lets you find out
afterwards that it was not actually controlled.

If you are timing a reaction to completion, **a time is not a rate**.
Rate goes up when time goes down, so plot $1/t$ against your variable if
you want a graph whose shape means something. Say in your write-up which
you plotted and why.

## What to bring to the consolidation

- Your prediction sheet, including the sketch graph, dated before the
  data.
- Your full table and your graph, with axes labelled and units on both.
- **Your ratio, as a number.** "Doubling the concentration multiplied
  the rate by 1.9" is the sentence the class needs from you. Not
  "increased".
- **Your answer on the amount of product**, separately from your answer
  on rate.
- **The controlled variable that got away from you**, if one did, and
  the direction it pushed things. Every group has one. The groups that
  cannot name theirs did not look.
- **One sentence for the other three teams**, because they are going to
  build their picture of this unit out of your result and you are going
  to build yours out of theirs. Make it a sentence with a number in it.

## What you should not claim

- **You did not measure an initial rate unless you designed for one.**
  Timing a reaction to completion gives you an average over a period
  during which the concentration was falling continuously. That average
  is lower than the rate at the start and higher than the rate at the
  end, and it belongs to neither. If you want the initial rate, take the
  slope at the beginning of a graph, or time a small early fraction of
  the reaction.
- **These reactions heat themselves, and it pushes rates up.** Both acid
  reactions are exothermic, so the mixture is warmer at the end than at
  the start, and the later part of the run is faster than the same
  concentration would give at your stated temperature. Your quoted
  temperature is the starting temperature, not the reaction's
  temperature, and the effect gets worse the more concentrated you go —
  which distorts a concentration series in a specific direction. Say so.
- **Magnesium ribbon carries an oxide coating**, and it takes time for
  acid to get through it. That delay is added to every timing and it is
  roughly the same size regardless of concentration, so it flattens your
  measured relationship — it makes fast runs look slower relative to
  slow ones. Sandpapering every piece the same way is a controlled
  variable, and if you did not do it, say which way that pushes your
  ratio.
- **Carbon dioxide dissolves in water, and hydrogen barely does.** If
  you collected gas over water in the carbonate reaction, some of your
  product went into solution instead of into the measuring cylinder, so
  your volumes are **underestimates** and the effect is worse for slow
  runs that spend longer in contact with the water. That is a systematic
  error that varies across your own series, which is the most awkward
  kind.
- **Marble chips are not a controlled surface area.** "Small chips" and
  "large chips" is an ordering, not a measurement — you know which had
  more surface area and not by how much. So you can defend "increasing
  surface area increased the rate" and you cannot defend a ratio, unless
  you found a way to quantify the area. Say which claim your data
  supports.
- **A stopwatch cannot resolve a two-second reaction.** Human reaction
  time is a couple of tenths of a second at each end, so a run lasting
  3 s carries a much larger percentage uncertainty than one lasting
  60 s. Design your concentrations so the fastest run is still slow
  enough to time, and if you could not, report the uncertainty rather
  than the number alone.
- **You cannot explain *why* yet, and you are not being asked to.**
  Report what changed, by how much, and in which direction. The
  mechanism arrives next.
