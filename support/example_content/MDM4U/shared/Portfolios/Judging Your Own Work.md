Every task page carries a table of success criteria, published before
you start. Most people read it once, on launch day, and never again —
and then hand in a report that misses two rows they could have fixed
in twenty minutes. This page is the twenty minutes.

We do it together the first time, on somebody else's audit, so the
move is not new to you when it is your own work being judged.

## The routine

1. Put the task's criteria table beside your own work.
2. Go row by row. For each one write **yes**, **not yet**, or
   **partly** — and one sentence of evidence: the table, the page,
   the sentence a reader would point at. "Yes" with nothing to point
   at is a wish, and in this course we do not accept those from
   anybody else either.
3. Find your weakest row. Not the easiest to fix — the weakest.
4. Write one sentence saying what you will change, and when.
5. Change it, in the working period that follows. That is why this is
   never scheduled on a due date: a judgement you have no time to act
   on is just a worry.

Steps 2 and 3 are the whole exercise. Naming your weakest row honestly
is harder than any of the fixing, because it means writing down the
thing you were hoping nobody would look at.

> [!example]- One row, judged honestly
> From [[The Survey Autopsy]]: *"A named absence — the missing group,
> and the direction of its effect."*
>
> **Partly.** I named the group — people without a phone number in
> the frame — but I only wrote that the result would be "less
> accurate". I have not said which way it bends. I think those
> households are younger and poorer, so the published support figure
> is probably too high, and I can say that in one sentence. Weakest
> row. **Change:** write the direction and the reason for it, this
> period, then check whether my own conclusion still survives it.
>
> That entry is worth more than "looks good to me" on all five rows,
> and it took less time to write.

## Why this is not a mark

Your judgement of your own work is not part of your mark, and neither
is a classmate's — see [[How Marks Work]]. That is deliberate, and it
cuts both ways: nothing you write here can cost you anything, which is
exactly what makes it worth writing honestly.

What it changes is the work. A self-check that leads to a fix is the
cheapest improvement available in this course, and by January it is a
habit you run without being asked — which is the actual point. It is
also the same move the whole subject is built on: [[What Makes a Model Good]]
asks where a model fails before it asks how well it fits, and
[[The Culminating Investigation]] gives its limitations section real
weight for the same reason.

## Where it goes

Write the judgement in your [[Math Journal]], dated, on the day you
make it. Two months later, when [[Showing Growth]] asks you to prove
you changed, a row you called "not yet" in October and fixed by
November is the cleanest evidence in the notebook.

One last thing worth doing: read your judgement out loud to the person
beside you. It catches the rows where you were being generous, and you
will usually hear it yourself before they say anything.
