Everything so far this unit has been a reaction somebody chose for you.
This one is yours: your question, your procedure, your controlled
variables, and your conclusion — with the reaction connected to a
safety or environmental problem that somebody actually has.

## The situation

Chemistry turns up in the world as a problem before it turns up as a
subject. A bridge corrodes. A spill has to be made safe. A kettle fills
with scale. Water leaving a site is too acidic for what it flows into.
A gas leaves a stack and somebody has to take it out.

Choose a question of that kind that you can genuinely investigate with
school equipment and school chemicals. Some that work well:

- **What slows the corrosion of iron?** Nails in different conditions —
  dry, damp, salt water, coated, in contact with a second metal.
- **How much acid can an antacid tablet neutralise**, and do the
  brands differ once you account for tablet mass?
- **What does an acidic solution do to limestone or chalk**, and how
  does the rate depend on concentration or on surface area?
- **How effectively can a gas be removed from a stream of air** by
  bubbling it through a solution — a bench-scale version of a scrubber.
- **What conditions change the rate of decomposition of hydrogen
  peroxide**, using the dilute peroxide from the prep room?

You may propose something else entirely. Bring it to me early rather
than late, because the approval step below is real and some ideas will
come back for a redesign.

## What you have to decide, and defend

This is the assessed thinking, and it happens before you touch
anything.

- **The question**, written so that it names a variable you will change
  and a quantity you will measure. "Does rust happen faster in salt
  water?" is a question. "Rusting" is a topic.
- **The prediction, with a mechanism**, written and dated before the
  first measurement. Not "the salt water one will rust more" but why —
  and what result would show the mechanism you named was wrong.
- **The controlled variables.** Name what you held constant, and be
  honest about the ones you could not: room temperature over a week,
  the exact surface of each nail, how much the solution was disturbed.
- **How many trials, and why that many.** One of anything tells you
  nothing about spread.
- **What you will measure, and with what resolution.** Mass to the
  hundredth of a gram, volume to the half-millilitre, a colour change
  judged by eye — each of these is a different quality of evidence and
  the report should say which you have.

> [!danger] Your procedure is checked before you run it — every time
> Submit the written procedure, the equipment list, the hazards, and
> what you would do if each hazard materialised. I will send some back,
> and that is the system working rather than a judgement of you.
>
> Non-negotiable, whatever you design:
>
> - **School chemicals only, at the concentrations supplied.** Nothing
>   from home, nothing from another room, no concentrated acids.
> - **Combine only what your approved procedure lists.** Substances
>   that are harmless separately can produce a toxic gas together, and
>   there is no way to tell by looking. This is why the rule is
>   absolute rather than a matter of judgement.
> - **Add acid to water, never water to acid.** The heat released has
>   somewhere to go when the water is already in the container.
> - Eye protection from first move to last of the cleanup. Hair tied
>   back, sleeves secured, closed-toe shoes.
> - Hot glassware looks exactly like cold glassware. Never pipette by
>   mouth. Waft odours; never inhale over a container. Nothing is
>   tasted.
> - Know where the eyewash, the shower, the extinguisher, the blanket,
>   and the spill kit are **before** you start, and tell me about every
>   incident the moment it happens, however small.
> - **No reaction is sealed in a rigid container**, and anything
>   producing a gas is run with a route for that gas to leave.
>
> The full agreement: [[Safety in the Lab]]. Labels and hazard symbols:
> [[Lab Safety and WHMIS]].

## What to hand in

**A full lab report**, in the seven sections set out in
[[Writing a Lab Report]] — question, prediction, variables, materials
and procedure, observations, analysis, conclusion and limitations.

**With the mass accounted for.** Whatever your question, weigh the
system before and after: the reactants and the vessel, then the products
and the vessel. If the totals differ, say by how much and where the
missing mass went — a gas that left, water that evaporated, solid left
on a filter paper. Mass is conserved in every reaction on this list, so
a difference is always a measurement telling you something about your
method rather than about chemistry. Use the correct words throughout —
reactant, product, precipitate, aqueous, endothermic — because a report
that says "the stuff" cannot be checked by anybody.

**Plus a one-page recommendation**, addressed to a named audience: a
building manager, a municipal water operator, a person deciding what to
buy, a small business. This is the part that connects your bench to the
problem, and it needs three things:

1. **What you found**, in language that audience can use.
2. **What you recommend**, and what it would cost them in money, time,
   or inconvenience. A recommendation with no cost named is not a
   recommendation.
3. **Research on the wider issue** — the safety or environmental
   context around this reaction, from at least two sources of the right
   kind, cited. Government agencies, professional bodies, a safety data
   sheet, a peer-reviewed article. Every figure traceable to something
   you can open.

## How it will be judged

| What I am looking for | Level 3 sounds like | Level 4 sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The question | Clear, and answerable at the bench | Sharp enough that the design falls out of it, and worth answering |
| Prediction | States what will happen | States a **mechanism**, and what would show the mechanism wrong |
| Design | A fair test with the variables named | Controls chosen with reasons, and the uncontrollable ones named honestly |
| Hazards, planned before you run it | Every hazard in your procedure named, with what you would do if it happened | Hazards read off the containers you will actually open, and the response matched to the amount you are actually using |
| Data | Complete, with units, and the mass before and after every trial | Enough trials to show spread, the spread reported, and any missing mass accounted for |
| The chemistry, said correctly | Reactant, product, precipitate, aqueous, endothermic used accurately throughout | Used accurately, and any of them restated in plain words on request |
| Conclusion | Answers the question with the data | Justifies the answer, and says how strongly the evidence supports it |
| Limitations | Names a source of error | Names it, says **which way** it pushed the result, and what it does not settle |
| The recommendation | Sensible, follows from the findings, and rests on two sources of the right kind | Fits the audience, names the cost, and does not overclaim |

## What sinks an otherwise good investigation

- **A prediction written afterwards.** I can tell, and it removes the
  only record of how your thinking moved. See [[Mistakes Are Data]].
- **"Sources of error: human error."** A limitation is something about
  the method that would still be there if you ran it perfectly.
  Measuring the wrong tube is a blunder — repeat the trial.
- **A conclusion the data cannot carry.** Two nails and one week does
  not establish what a coating does. Say what you saw and where it
  stops.
- **A recommendation with no cost in it.** Everything you recommend
  costs somebody something. Naming it is what makes the advice usable.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 2, Day 15, the first bench period
  Watch for: WHEN the balance gets used. The empty vessel has to be weighed
  before anything is mixed, and it is the one reading that cannot be
  recovered afterwards. By marking time every report will contain a mass
  account, and a reconstructed one looks exactly like a measured one on the
  page — the period is the only place the difference is visible.
  Going well: the vessel on the balance before the reagents are collected,
  the number written straight into the notebook, and the balance zeroed
  again between benches rather than assumed.
  Stuck: the mixing started first, then a hunt for the empty mass; or a mass
  taken off the bottle's label and entered as though it had been weighed.
  Record: two ticks per bench on your seating plan — weighed first, or
  reconstructed. One circuit in the opening ten minutes. That is C2.4's
  first half — comparing the values before and after — and the accounting
  afterwards has nothing to stand on without it.

TALK — Unit 2, Day 14, while you are redesigning procedures with them
  You are already at every bench that day. Do not ask what they held
  constant, how many trials, or what would show the mechanism wrong — the
  task page asks all three in print, so the answers are prepared.
  Ask: "Your bench makes a few millilitres of waste. The problem you chose
  happens at the scale of a bridge, or a water plant, or a smokestack. Who
  deals with the waste at THAT scale, and what do they have to do with it?"
  Then: "Why this reaction, and not one of the others on the list? What did

  A strong first answer gets past "they recycle it" to something concrete —
  a neutralisation step, a licensed contractor, a permit, a cost per tonne —
  which is the environmental half of C1.1 said out loud rather than cited. A
  strong second answer names a measurable quantity and an instrument, which
  is A1.1 heard: the question was chosen because it could be answered, not

  Record: one line per bench, two ticks. The second column is the one to
  carry into Unit 2, Day 17.
