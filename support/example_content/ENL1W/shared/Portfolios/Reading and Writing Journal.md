One notebook, kept all semester, for two purposes: to think on paper, and to
leave a trail of what you can now do that you could not at the start of the
course.

## What goes in it

- **Responses to what we read** — not summaries. What you noticed, what
  you argued with, what you did not understand.
- **Drafting.** Every major piece starts here.
- **Your own reading.** A line or two about the book in your bag, every
  couple of weeks. What it is doing, not whether you liked it.
- **Words.** The vocabulary page at the back, per
  [[Where Words Come From]].
- **After discussions.** The position you came in with and where you left
  it.
- **Anything you notice about how writing works.** A sentence from a
  novel that made you stop. An advertisement that annoyed you and why.

## What it is not

Not a diary you must open to me — the personal parts stay yours. Not a
neatness contest. Not a place where being wrong costs you anything.

## How it is checked

Twice a term I read a page you choose, and once a term we look through
the whole thing together for two minutes. None of that is marked — it is
how I find out what to teach next. The [[Portfolio Checklist]] tells you
what has to be there by the end of the course.

The one exception is the reading log for [[The Book Club]]. Those entries
are written in the meeting, in class, and they are part of that task's
mark, which that page explains. Everything else in this notebook is
yours.

## The habit that makes it work

Write the entry within a day of the class. An entry written at the end of
the course about a the first class is a memory of a memory; an entry written
that night is evidence.

> [!tip] If you hate journals
> Plenty of people do, usually because "write your feelings" was never a
> useful instruction. Try this instead: one sentence about what happened
> in the text, one sentence about what you thought, one question you
> would ask the writer. Three sentences. That is a complete entry, and
> three of those a week is a real journal.
