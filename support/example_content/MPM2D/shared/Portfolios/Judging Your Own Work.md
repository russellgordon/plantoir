Every task page carries a table of success criteria, published before you
start. Most people read it once, at launch, and never again — and then
hand in a case file or a report that misses two rows they could have
fixed in twenty minutes. This page is the twenty minutes.

We do it together the first time, on somebody else's work, so the move is
not new to you when it is your own being judged.

## The routine

1. Open the task page and put the criteria table beside your work.
2. Go row by row. For each one write **yes**, **not yet**, or **partly**
   — and one sentence of evidence: the calculation, the diagram, the
   line a reader would point at. "Yes" with no evidence is a wish.
3. Find your weakest row. Not the easiest to fix — the weakest.
4. Write one sentence saying what you will change, and when.
5. Fix it — in the rest of this period, or in the next one. This is why
   the self-check is never scheduled on the day something is due: a
   judgement you have no time to act on is just a worry.

Steps 2 and 3 are the whole exercise. Naming your weakest row honestly is
harder than any of the fixing, because it means saying the thing you have
been hoping nobody would notice.

> [!example]- One row, judged honestly
> From [[The Quadrilateral Case File]]: *"Evidence for every claim — no
> property is asserted without a calculation."*
>
> **Partly.** We computed all four side lengths and both diagonals, and
> the file says so. But the sentence "the diagonals bisect each other"
> is followed by nothing — we found the two midpoints on the board on
> Tuesday and never copied them across, so the strongest claim in the
> verdict is the one with no arithmetic under it. Weakest row.
> **Change:** redo both midpoints, put them in the evidence table, and
> label them with the claim they support — next period, before the
> argument gets written.
>
> That entry is worth more than "looks good to me" on all six rows, and
> it took less time to write.

## What makes a maths self-check different

A criteria row like *"visible reasoning"* is not judged by whether you
believe your reasoning was visible. Read your own page the way
[[Checking Your Own Work]] reads an answer — as a stranger who was not in
your group, cannot hear you, and will not give you the benefit of the
doubt. Where you find yourself explaining out loud what the page should
have said, that is the missing line, and you have just found it.

Two rows repay this most, on every task in the course: whether every
claim is tied to a calculation, and whether a reader can follow the route
without you standing there. They are also the two rows that never
improve by working harder on the mathematics — only by rewriting.

## Where this fits

Your judgement of your own work is not part of your mark, and neither is
a classmate's — see [[How Marks Work]]. That is deliberate, and it cuts
both ways: nothing you write here can cost you anything, which is exactly
what makes it worth writing honestly.

What it does change is the work. A self-check that leads to a fix is the
cheapest mark improvement available in this course, and it belongs in
your [[Math Journal]] too — a weakest row named in October and closed in
November is precisely the evidence [[Showing Growth]] will ask you for.

One last thing worth doing: read your judgement out loud to the person
next to you. It catches the rows where you were being generous, and you
will usually hear it yourself before they say anything.
