A three-hour exam, near the end of the course, worth thirty per cent of
your final mark — see [[How Marks Work]] for how that fits with the
seventy per cent from the semester's four tasks.

It has two parts, and both draw on the whole semester, not just the last
unit you studied.

## Part One: Unseen Passage Analysis (about 60 minutes)

A short prose or poetry passage you haven't seen before. You'll answer
questions on it that ask you to identify technique and explain effect — the
same skills [[Symbolism and Motif]], [[Diction, Tone, and Style]], and
[[Conflict and Plot Structure]] built across the semester, applied to a
text you're meeting for the first time.

## Part Two: Essay (about 100 minutes)

A choice of prompts, each asking for a developed argument using evidence
from at least one text studied this semester — a short story, *Indian
Horse*, or *Hamlet*. This is the same essay discipline [[Building a Thesis Statement]],
[[Two Stories, One Thread]], and [[The Weight of the Ice]]
already practised, done once more, under exam conditions, without the
checkpoints and revision days that supported it earlier in the course.

The remaining time is for planning and proofreading — see [[Editing and Proofreading]]
for what a final read-through is actually checking for.

## What to expect

- Closed book — no notes, no access to the texts themselves, though you may
  bring one clean copy of any play or novel studied, unannotated.
- Silent, individual, invigilated — this is the one piece of work this
  course asks you to produce with no conferencing, no peer feedback, and no
  revision day built in.
- [[Judging Your Own Work]]'s four questions are still worth running through
  in the planning minutes before you write — there just isn't a checkpoint
  built into the exam period itself to act on what you find.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| Answers the actual question | Part One's questions and Part Two's chosen prompt, not a memorized response to something adjacent |
| Uses real evidence | Specific lines, moments, or details — not vague plot summary |
| Organized under pressure | A plan sketched before writing shows, even in a first-and-only draft |
| Draws on the whole semester | Part Two's essay uses a text confidently, the way a semester of practice should let you |

This page is the one place in the course where the usual triangulation note
doesn't apply, and that's worth saying plainly rather than forcing one.

A supervised, closed final evaluation is deliberately PURE PRODUCT
evidence — the whole point of exam conditions is that there is no
conversation or observation to gather during it, because none is meant to
happen. Every other task in this course names where to watch and what to
ask; this one exists precisely so the semester's four tasks aren't the only
evidence a mark rests on, and it does that job by being the one piece of
work produced with nobody circulating and nobody conferencing.

The product evidence is the completed exam booklet, collected at the end of
the sitting.
