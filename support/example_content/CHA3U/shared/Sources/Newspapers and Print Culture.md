For most of the nineteenth century the newspaper was not a neutral record of
the day. It was a party instrument, a commercial venture, and often both,
printed for a town that knew exactly whose paper it was. That makes the
American press an unreliable narrator and a superb source — provided you ask
it the questions it can answer.

The curriculum's own caution applies to every period: it asks what biases you
would expect in a piece of writing produced at a particular political
moment, and why it matters to place such sources in historical context.

## What a newspaper is good and bad at

| What you want to know | How good the press is | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What happened, on the day | Mixed | Early reports are fast and frequently wrong; check the next week's issue |
| What a town was told | Excellent | This is the paper's actual subject |
| What could be said in public | Excellent | Including what had to be said carefully |
| Who held power locally | Very good | Read the advertisements and the notices, not the editorials |
| What people privately believed | Poor | Use letters, diaries, and [[Testimony and Narrative]] |
| Prices, sailings, sales, arrests | Very good | Routine columns had no reason to flatter anyone |

The two most useful pages in any issue are the advertisements and the
notices. Nobody wrote them to persuade posterity, and they will tell you
what a place produced, sold, feared, and — in the American South before
1865 — bought and sold in human beings.

## Read across the divide, always

One paper is one party's account. Two papers from opposed towns, on the same
week, is a method. When the curriculum asks you to compare press reports
from different regions, it is asking for exactly this: same event, two
front pages, and an explanation of the difference that goes beyond calling
one of them biased.

## Print culture is wider than news

The same presses produced pamphlets, almanacs, sermons, songsters, party
platforms, magazines, and the narratives and reform literature that the
curriculum treats as key developments in American culture. A magazine that
lasts decades is a record of what an educated readership expected to see;
a pamphlet that runs to fifteen editions is evidence of an argument
spreading. Print runs and editions are data.

**Chronicling America** at the Library of Congress is where much of this is
free. Its own research guide describes it as "a searchable and freely
accessible digital collection of historic newspaper pages," containing
millions of pages "from nearly every state and territory in the United
States published through 1963," produced by the National Digital Newspaper
Program, "a partnership between the National Endowment for the Humanities
(NEH) and the Library of Congress." The companion Directory of U.S.
Newspapers in American Libraries indexes titles published since 1690, so you
can find out what a town had before you go looking for it.

Search by date first and keyword second — optical character recognition on
old type is imperfect, and the issue you want is often two days later than
you assumed. Record the paper, place, date, page and column every time, as
[[Citing Historical Sources]] requires.
