The last portfolio entry, written in class in the period where the portfolio
closes, after the culminating inquiry. Two parts: what your historical
training does to the news you read, and where this kind of thinking is paid
for. It is marked with the rest of [[The Historian's Portfolio]].

## Part one — apply it to something happening now

Choose one current issue that is genuinely being argued about, and treat it
as a historian would treat a document.

- **Who is making this claim, and what do they gain if it is believed?** The
  question you have been asking of a nineteenth-century newspaper works
  unchanged on a post published this morning.
- **What is the comparable case?** [[Continuity and Change]] is the tool.
  Naming an earlier episode is not the same as saying history repeats — say
  precisely what is alike and what is not.
- **What would settle it?** If no evidence could change the claim, it is not
  a factual dispute, and knowing that early saves the argument.

Write 300 words. Name the earlier episode in this course you drew on, and be
honest about where the comparison breaks. The two that most often apply are
[[Immigration and Exclusion]] and
[[Foreign Policy and Its Justifications]].

> [!tip] The hardest part is restraint
> Most bad historical analogies fail because they are used to end an
> argument rather than to open one. The strongest version of this entry
> names the difference between then and now before anyone else can — and if
> your current issue involves Canada and the United States, that discipline
> matters twice over, because a comparison that flatters one country is not
> an analysis of either.

## Part two — where the skill is paid for

The curriculum's own list is a good start: archaeologist, archivist,
curator, educator, game designer, journalist, librarian, policy analyst,
political speech writer, politician, researcher. Less obvious, and closer to
what you actually did this year:

- **Archivists and records managers**, who decide what an organisation keeps
  and how anyone will find it later — [[Where the Records Live]] is that job
  described from the outside.
- **Researchers in land, title and treaty work**, where the evidence is a
  document from 1851 and the decision is being made this year.
- **Policy and program analysts**, who are asked what was tried before and
  what happened.
- **Anyone who has to establish what occurred** from incomplete records:
  insurance, journalism, law, audit, and public inquiries all do it.

Pick one. Describe, in a paragraph, a task that job does in a week which you
have already practised in this course. Do not describe a programme you would
apply to; describe the work.

Close with one sentence on the habit you expect to keep. For most students
it is asking who made a claim, and why.
