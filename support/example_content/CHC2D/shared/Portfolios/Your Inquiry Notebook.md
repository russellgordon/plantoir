One notebook, kept from the first week to the last. It is not a set of
class notes and it is not a diary. It is the running record of what you
asked, what you found, where you found it, and how your thinking moved —
and it is the reason the culminating inquiry will not start from nothing.

Start it now, in Unit 1, while the questions are still small.

## What an entry holds

| Part | What goes in it | Why it earns its place |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The question | One sentence, in question form | [[Building an Argument]] cannot begin until the question is decided |
| The source | Author, title, date, where you found it | You will need it months later; see [[Citing Historical Sources\|how to record it]] |
| What it says | A short quotation or a fact, copied exactly | Paraphrasing too early loses the wording you will want |
| What it does not say | The gap, stated plainly | Half of historical judgement is knowing what the record omits |
| So what | Two sentences of your own thinking | This is the part nobody else can write for you |

Four of the five are mechanical. The fifth is the work.

## Three kinds of question

[[Historical Significance]] and [[Cause and Consequence]] both start from
a question, and the type decides what evidence will settle it. A factual
question wants a document. A comparative question wants two cases held to
the same standard. A causal question wants a sequence you can defend.
Label every entry with which kind you are asking, because a question that
changes type halfway through is the commonest way an inquiry stalls.

## Recording how your thinking changed

This is the entry students skip and the one worth most. When a source
moves you off a position, write three things: what you believed, what you
read, and what you believe now. Date it. By April you will have a record
of your own reasoning that you could not reconstruct from memory.

If nothing has moved you all term, that is a finding too — and usually it
means you have been reading sources that already agreed with you.

## What happens to it

The notebook is one of the three pieces of [[The Portfolio Case]], and it
is marked there — once, at the end, alongside the two reflections. What
carries the mark is what you have written by the portfolio check in Unit
3, and what you write in class at the milestones after it. Adding to it
between classes is practice, it is worth doing, and it is not itself
marked; see [[How Marks Work]].

Long before that, though, it is the thing that makes the rest possible.
Every task in this course begins from a question and a source, and the
students who arrive at [[The Commemoration Inquiry]] with four months of
entries behind them start from something rather than from nothing — the
question they settle on is very often one they first wrote down in
October. It also feeds [[Skills You Are Building]], which asks you to put
a specific piece of your own work behind every claim you make about
yourself.
