**Due: near the end of Unit 1.** Pairs — with one section written and
marked on your own.

One physical region of Canada, explained well enough that someone who
has never been there could picture it and say why it looks like that.

## What you produce

| Part | What it must contain |
| --- | --- |
| A map | Your region's boundaries, built in a web GIS or a mapping tool, with a legend, scale, and north arrow — a pencil sketch is how you plan it, not what you hand in |
| The landscape | Landforms and characteristics that make this region different from the ones beside it |
| The making of it | The geological, glacial, hydrological, or climatic processes that produced it — and which are still working |
| The meaning | One way this landscape shows up in how Canadians picture their country, and who is doing the picturing — **each partner writes their own, about 150 words, and signs it** |
| Sources | Every claim traceable, cited as [[Citing Geographic Sources]] sets out |

## How it is marked

- **Accuracy** — the processes you name really did shape that region.
- **Specificity** — "there are mountains" is worth nothing; a named range,
  its rock, and the process that lifted it is worth everything.
- **The map** — readable, correctly labelled, and it supports your
  argument rather than decorating it.
- **The meaning section** — you treat identity as something people make
  and contest, not as a fact about rocks.
- **Your own meaning section** carries your mark. Nothing in this course
  is given a common pair mark: the map and the landscape work are built
  together, and what they earn is judged for each of you from your own
  section and from what you can account for at the conference. Two
  partners who wrote the same meaning paragraph have written one between
  them. You write yours in the working period after we compare
  boundaries, while [[Landscape and Identity]] is still fresh.

> [!tip] Start from the boundary
> The most useful question is where your region *stops*, and how you can
> tell. A boundary you can defend is a region you understand — and it is
> the fastest way into [[Canada's Physical Regions]].

Build the map with [[Making a Thematic Map]] or [[Using a Web GIS]]. The
shoreline visit in this unit is evidence you already have: what you saw
there is a process in your own region, still running.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

Done in pairs, so ask each partner on their own: the map is shared and
the mark is not.

OBSERVE — Unit 1, Day 12, the map clinic
  Watch for what happens before the boundary gets drawn. Does anyone
  read the scale bar of the base they are about to trace, and work out
  what a centimetre is worth, or does the pencil go down on whatever
  zoom happened to be open? A boundary drawn on a distorted base looks
  exactly as confident on the finished sheet as one drawn on a
  projection that suits the region — that is the whole problem with it.
  Going well: someone says a distance out loud before drawing anything,
  or changes the base because the one they had stretched the north.
  Stuck: a screenshot being traced, with no scale on it at all.
  Record: pair initials on the seating plan, tick or dash. Two seconds.

TALK — Unit 1, Day 13, in the working period, after the whole class has
compared boundaries
  The whole class has just compared boundaries, so do not ask them to do
  that again — use it.
  Ask: "Which of the other regions made you doubt your own boundary, and
  what did theirs have that yours does not?"
  A strong answer names a rival criterion someone else used — bedrock
  where they used relief, drainage where they used climate — and says
  what it would take to settle between them. That is B1.1, the
  characteristics used to tell one region from the next, heard rather
  than read: the study is marked on the boundary, not on whether the
  boundary was reasoned or remembered.
  Then: "Where have you seen this region's landscape used to sell
  something — a bank, a beer, a tourism campaign? What did whoever made
  that image want you to feel?"
  A strong answer treats the image as somebody's decision, with a
  purpose behind it. That is B1.5's second half, the significance of the
  symbols and images, which the written meaning section usually reaches
  for only in the abstract.
  Record: one line per student, the rival criterion and the image.

The product evidence is the study handed in on Day 15.
