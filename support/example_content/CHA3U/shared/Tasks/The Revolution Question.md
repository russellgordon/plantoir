**Due: the end of Unit 1.** Individual. An argument of about 900 words.

One question about the American Revolution, answered with evidence. Not
a retelling of it — an argument someone could disagree with.

## The questions on offer

- Was independence the point from the beginning, or did it become the
  point once the war had started? What evidence separates those two?
- The war ran through communities that were already there. Choose one
  Native American nation and argue what the Revolution was, from where
  it stood.
- The Declaration of Independence, the Articles of Confederation, the
  Constitution, and the Bill of Rights were four different answers to
  the question of who governs. Choose two and argue what changed
  between them, and for whom.
- Whose liberty was enlarged by the Revolution, and whose was not? Argue
  it for two specific groups, with evidence from the period.
- Choose a person or a symbol from before 1791 and argue what work it
  has done since. Say who has used it, and what for.

You may propose your own, agreed with me first.

## What the argument must do

1. **State a claim** that a reasonable person could reject.
2. **Support it** with at least four pieces of evidence, at least two of
   them primary and at least one made by someone who lost.
3. **Take the strongest objection seriously** and answer it — the
   strongest version, not the easiest one.
4. **Say what would change your mind**, specifically.
5. **Name where you are reasoning about significance, cause and
   consequence, continuity and change, or perspective**, and use the
   concept to do work rather than to label a paragraph.

## How it is marked

| Criterion | What earns the marks |
| --- | --- |
| Claim | Arguable, specific, and actually answered by the end |
| Evidence | Weighed against itself rather than listed, and cited |
| Perspective | Participants other than the winners are present and taken seriously |
| Counter-argument | The strongest version, genuinely engaged |
| Writing | Clear structure; the reader always knows where they are |

> [!question] Who does the word "Americans" name in your sentence?
> The people living in the Thirteen Colonies included the enslaved, the
> free but unenfranchised, Loyalists, and Native American nations whose
> territory the war ran through. A sentence beginning "the Americans
> wanted" quietly decides that none of them count. Every time you write
> it, check whether you mean all of them — and if you do not, say who
> you do mean.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 1, Day 18, the working period, while the counter-argument is
being drafted
  Watch for: what happens when the hard objection is actually put to a
  student — by you in passing, or by whoever is sitting next to them. Two
  things can follow. The objection goes into the draft, or the claim
  quietly widens until the objection no longer touches it. The criteria
  require a counter-argument, so the finished essay will contain one either
  way, and it will be presented as the strongest available. Nothing on the
  page can tell you whether a harder one was in the room an hour earlier.
  Going well: the claim gets NARROWER and the objection stays hard.
  Stuck: a redefinition offered as an answer — "that's not really what I'm
  saying" — after which the thesis means less than it did.
  Record: two columns on the day plan, took it or moved the claim. Put the
  objection to at least half the room; it is a thirty-second intervention.

TALK — Unit 1, Day 17, the conferences on claims that are not yet arguable
  Ask: "Who alive in 1776 would be most annoyed by your claim, and what
  would they say first?"
  Then: "If they said that, which document would you reach for?"
  A strong answer produces somebody from the period with an interest — a
  Loyalist printer, a Mohawk council, a merchant with debts in London, a
  woman with property she could not hold in her own name — rather than a
  modern sceptic, and their first move is one the record actually
  preserves. B1.4 asks for the significance of the political changes to be
  assessed for DIFFERENT GROUPS, and a student who can only imagine a
  present-day objector has an opinion about the Revolution rather than a
  perspective on it. The essay cannot show you the difference.
  Record: initials and the name of the objector they produced, one line.

The product evidence is the argument handed in on Day 19.
