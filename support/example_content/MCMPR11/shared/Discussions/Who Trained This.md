Somewhere behind every AI system that recognizes speech is a pile of
recorded voices, and somewhere behind every one of those recordings is a
person who spoke into a microphone. Whose voice, whose language, and
whose permission — those turn out to be some of the most contested
questions in AI right now, and one of the clearest answers came from
Aotearoa New Zealand.

### The Kōrero Māori campaign

In May 2018, Te Hiku Media — an iwi (tribal) radio station and media
organization in the far north of Aotearoa New Zealand — learned that
Lionbridge, a US-based language-technology company, was recruiting Māori
speakers to record voice data at US\$45 an hour, to be used for training
AI language systems. Te Hiku Media publicly rejected the offer and pushed
back hard against an outside company profiting from Māori voice data,
calling this kind of extraction a continuation of colonisation rather
than a fair transaction.

Instead of letting the data go to an outside company, Te Hiku Media ran
its own campaign: within ten days, over 2,500 people had signed up and
recorded more than 300 hours of speech. They used that data to build
their own Māori automatic speech recognition system — Papa Reo —
reporting roughly 92% accuracy for te reo Māori. The project grew into a
wider platform, including Kaituhi, an automatic transcription tool, and a
growing archive of digitized radio recordings.

### The license, not just the model

The technical achievement is not really the point. What Te Hiku Media
built alongside it matters more: the **Kaitiakitanga License** —
kaitiakitanga means guardianship, not ownership. It states the language
data is held in trust for Māori communities rather than owned outright by
Te Hiku Media itself, with any benefit required to flow back to the
people who provided it. Comparable Indigenous-led speech-AI projects have
since started among Native Hawaiian communities and the Mohawk people in
Canada, built on the same underlying idea.

### Closer to home

FirstVoices and the OCAP® principles you have already met in this course
apply the same reasoning to language and cultural data collected here in
BC. Te Hiku Media's case is useful precisely because it plays out the
same question in a different country, against a named company with a
dollar figure attached — and shows that the answer does not have to be
"let the data go." A community can build the tool itself, on its own
terms, and still end up with something that works.

### Discussion Prompts

1. **The price tag.** Lionbridge offered US\$45 an hour for voice
   recordings. Why might a fair hourly rate still be the wrong offer?
   What would have to be true for an offer like that to actually respect
   data sovereignty?
2. **Guardianship vs. ownership.** The Kaitiakitanga License is not a
   standard open-source license, and it is not a normal "all rights
   reserved" copyright either. What does it change about who can use the
   data Te Hiku Media collected, and what would a programmer need to
   check before using a Kaitiakitanga-licensed dataset in a project?
3. **The training data nobody asks about.** Most AI voice and language
   systems are trained on data scraped from the internet with no consent
   process at all. Why did this particular case get attention, when so
   much everyday scraping does not?

### How we run this

Run this one as a jigsaw — everybody teaches a piece of the same case.

- Split into small "home" groups of four. Inside each home group, number
  students 1 to 4.
- Everyone with the same number leaves to form an "expert" group with
  that number from every other home group. Each expert group gets one
  lens on this page to dig into for about ten minutes, using the
  sections above plus one thing they look up themselves:
  1. What actually happened — the Lionbridge offer, the ten-day
     campaign, the numbers.
  2. Why voice and language data is treated differently from ordinary
     training data — what makes it "cultural heritage" rather than just
     information.
  3. What the Kaitiakitanga License actually changes, in practical
     terms, for anyone who wants to use the data.
  4. How this connects to FirstVoices and OCAP® — what is the same, and
     what is different, about the BC context.
- Expert groups return to their original home group. Each person teaches
  their piece — no notes read aloud, actual explaining — until the home
  group has all four lenses.
- Home groups finish with the Discussion Prompts above, now armed with
  all four angles.
