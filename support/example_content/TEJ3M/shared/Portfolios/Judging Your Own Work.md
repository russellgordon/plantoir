Every job in this course publishes its criteria table before the first
period of work. Most benches read it on launch day and never open it
again — then hand in a build that misses two rows they could have
closed in twenty minutes with the iron still warm. This page is those
twenty minutes.

The first time, we do it as a room, out loud, on somebody else's
calculation sheet, so that the move is already familiar when the work
under the meter is yours.

## Four moves, and the second one is the job

**Set the table beside the bench.** Not on a screen behind three tabs —
beside the thing, where you can look from one to the other. Half of
what follows is just putting the criteria and the hardware in the same
field of view.

**Take the rows in order and answer each one out loud: yes, not yet, or
partly.** Then say what an inspector would point at. "Yes — 4.7 V at
TP2, measured this period." "Partly — the schematic matches except the
diode I swapped on Tuesday." A yes with nothing to point at is a hope,
and you will hear that in your own voice before anyone tells you.

**Pick the weakest row, not the quickest.** This is the whole exercise
and it costs more than any of the fixing does, because it means writing
down the thing you were quietly hoping nobody would put a meter on. The
quickest row is a comfort; the weakest row is the mark.

**Book the fix.** One sentence: what changes, and in which period. Then
do it — in what is left of today, or first thing next class. The
self-check is never scheduled on a due date for exactly this reason: a
verdict you cannot act on is a worry, not a judgement.

> [!example]- One row, judged honestly
> From [[The Working Circuit]]: *"Ratings respected — power
> dissipation checked for every part, before power was applied."*
>
> **Partly.** I checked the two resistors in the divider: 41 mW and
> 63 mW, both well inside a quarter-watt part. I never checked the
> series resistor on the lamp branch, and that branch carries 90 mA.
> Weakest row. **Change:** work out its dissipation this period, and
> if it is over about 125 mW, swap it for a half-watt part before I
> solder anything else.
>
> Three sentences, one of them a number, and a decision at the end. It
> took less time to write than the sentence you are reading, and it is
> worth more than a clean sweep of ticks down the whole table.

## Judging a build is not admiring it

A bench that has just got something working is the worst available
judge of it, and knowing that is most of the skill. Three habits fix
it:

- **Read the row first, then go looking for the evidence** — never the
  other way round. Deciding it is fine and then finding a row that
  agrees is how every rushed inspection in the trade goes wrong.
- **Put the meter on it.** Half of these criteria are claims with
  numbers in them, and a number you have not measured today is a claim
  about yesterday's circuit.
- **Hand the criteria to the bench across the aisle** and give them
  five minutes with your build. They owe it nothing and they have not
  spent three periods loving it.

## Why none of this is marked

Nothing you write here reaches your mark, and neither does anything
your neighbour writes about your build — [[How Marks Work]] explains
why. That runs in both directions: there is no way for this to be used
against you, which is the only condition under which anybody writes
honestly about their own work.

What it does reach is the build. One self-check that produces one fix
is the cheapest improvement available in this course. Put the verdict
and the fix into your [[Tech Journal]] as you go: it is the same loop
[[Showing Growth]] will ask you to point at in January, and by the
capstone it is something you run without being told, which is the
actual point of it.
