> [!abstract] At a glance
> Four write-ups, one per unit, each written **in class** in the period
> after the bench work · individually, from data your bench collected ·
> the same six criteria every time

Four of this course's ten investigations are written up formally, and
those four write-ups are marked. They are the part of your seventy per
cent that arrives steadily rather than in five large pieces, which is
what lets a report card say something about your most recent and most
consistent work rather than about five afternoons.

The criteria below do not change from the first one to the last. That is
the point of them: improvement across four reports is only visible if
the ruler stays the same.

## The four, and when they are written

| Report | The investigation behind it | Written in class on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One | [[Testing for Bond Type]] | Unit 1, Day 5 |
| Two | [[Oxides and Neutralisation]] | Unit 2, Day 7 |
| Three | [[Finding an Empirical Formula]] | Unit 3, Day 7 |
| Four | [[Preparing a Standard Solution]] | Unit 4, Day 6 |

Each investigation page carries its own question, its safety notes, and
what its analysis has to answer. The shape of the report itself — the
eight sections, and what belongs in each — is on
[[Writing a Lab Report]].

The other six investigations are not written up formally. They are read
back in the consolidation discussion, they turn up in your
[[Chemistry Journal]], and two of them are the rehearsal for a task that
is: [[Percentage Yield of a Precipitate]] before
[[The Yield Investigation]], and [[Titrating an Acid]] before
[[The Water Report]].

## Written in class, and why

Every one of these is written in the period straight after the bench,
with your data in front of you, your bench partner beside you, and me in
the room.

That is deliberate. A report written at home four days later is a report
about what you can remember, and the thing worth marking — what you made
of the numbers while they were still strange — has gone by then. It also
means the help is available at the moment you need it, which is while
you are staring at a ratio of 1 : 2.16 wondering what to do with it.

## Individually, from shared data

The bench work is done in pairs and the raw readings belong to the
bench. **From the analysis onward the report is yours alone.** Two
people may hand in the same observations table; two people handing in
the same analysis have handed in one report between them.

## How these are judged

| Quality | What it looks like in your write-up |
| --- | --- |
| Observations kept clean | Every reading recorded, including the one that told you nothing, with the instrument's resolution beside it. No interpretation in this section, and nothing quietly dropped |
| Numbers you could defend | Units on every quantity and every column, and a final answer to the figures your worst instrument entitles you to — not to whatever the calculator showed |
| One calculation shown in full | The sample calculation written out end to end, with the route visible; the rest may be a table |
| A conclusion the data allows | The question answered in a paragraph, with the evidence attached, and a difference called real only when it is bigger than your uncertainty |
| Limitations with a direction | Each one named, and which way it pushed your number. "There may have been experimental error" is a sentence, not a finding |
| Written to be checked | Somebody who was not at your bench could repeat what you did and arrive at your numbers |

The last two rows are what separate a Grade 11 report from a Grade 10
one, and they are the two that vanish under time pressure. Run them
against your draft with [[Judging Your Own Work]] before the period ends.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

These four are the steadiest evidence in the course, and the cheapest
place to gather the other two kinds: watch on a bench day, when the
numbers are being made, and ask on the writing day, when they are lying
on the desk between you.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 5, making up the standard solution
  Watch for: the choice of glassware, made before anything is weighed. A
  volumetric flask, a rinse of the beaker into it, the last few drops
  from a dropper with the eye level at the mark — or a measuring
  cylinder, a top-up by sight, and a concentration quoted to four
  figures anyway. The report gives you the concentration and the
  arithmetic; it cannot tell you which vessel the number came out of, and
  the student who chose well may not think it worth writing down.
  Going well: the flask picked up first; a rinse of the beaker; a pause
  at eye level.
  Stuck: solid tipped straight into the flask, or the last of it left in
  the beaker; a meniscus judged from standing height.
  Record: your class list, two columns — chose and used the volumetric
  glassware, or did not. That is A1.2, and it is over in fifteen seconds
  a bench.

TALK — Unit 3, Day 7, while they write up the empirical formula
  Ask: "Your ratio came out at 1 point something. What would have to be
  true about your masses for that to really be a whole number, and what
  would have to be true for it not to be?"
  Then: "If I gave you twice as much hydrate to start with, which of
  your numbers would get better, and which would not move at all?"
  A strong answer separates the error that shrinks with a bigger sample
  from the one that does not — a balance reading to 0.01 g matters less
  on 4 g than on 0.4 g, while water driven off early is the same
  fraction either way. That is D2.4 and A1.13 heard together, and a
  report can round a ratio to the right whole number without any of that
  thinking having happened.
  Record: three columns — separated the two, rounded and hoped, did not
  get there. The third column is who to sit with on Day 8.

The product evidence is the four reports, each handed in at the end of
its own writing period.
