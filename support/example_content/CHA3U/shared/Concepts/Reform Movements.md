Reform movements cluster in the decades before the Civil War, and they
overlap so heavily that the same names appear in three or four of them. That
overlap is the finding, not a coincidence. A generation persuaded that
society could be deliberately improved applied the conviction to whatever
they found intolerable, and they found a great deal.

## The movements, and the people in more than one of them

Abolition was the largest and the most dangerous to belong to. William Lloyd
Garrison began publishing *The Liberator* in 1831 demanding immediate
emancipation without compensation to enslavers. Frederick Douglass, who had
escaped slavery, published his own narrative and then his own newspaper, and
argued in public against people who insisted he was too articulate to have
been enslaved. Sojourner Truth spoke for abolition and women's rights at
once, and refused to let either audience treat the two as separable.

The women's rights movement grew directly out of abolition, in part because
women who organized against slavery kept being told to organize quietly. The
convention at Seneca Falls in 1848 produced a Declaration of Sentiments
modelled deliberately on the Declaration of Independence — a rhetorical
choice that forced readers to notice which half of the population the
original had left out. Elizabeth Cady Stanton drafted it; Lucretia Mott
helped convene it; Frederick Douglass spoke in favour of the demand for the
vote, which was the resolution the room found hardest.

Dorothea Dix documented the conditions in which people with mental illness
were confined — often in jails, often without heat — and presented the
evidence to legislatures rather than to crowds. Her method was investigation
and testimony, and it produced new institutions in state after state.
Temperance drew enormous support, particularly from women whose legal
position gave them no protection against a husband's drinking. Early trade
unions organized around hours and wages and were repeatedly held to be
illegal conspiracies.

> [!tip] Ranking movements needs criteria before it needs opinions
> The curriculum asks which movement had the greatest impact. Decide what
> "impact" means before you argue: number of people affected, permanence of
> the change, whether it altered law or only opinion, or whether it changed
> what later movements believed was possible. Abolition ends an institution
> and wins on the first three. Seneca Falls changes almost nothing in law for
> seventy years and wins on the fourth. State your criterion and the argument
> becomes assessable. That is the whole point of [[Building an Argument]].

## Why so many Quakers

The question in the curriculum has a real answer. Quaker meetings held that
the divine light is in every person, which makes hierarchy and human property
theologically awkward; they permitted women to speak and lead; and they had
long experience of being a persecuted minority themselves. That combination
produced organizers, and it produced them early.

into [[North and South]].
