**Four short entries, written across the term and marked once as a set
in the last week.** Individual.

Every other task in this course points at an organization. This one
points at you: what kind of manager you are becoming, tested against
evidence rather than announced.

## The four entries

| Entry | Written in class on | What it asks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [[Your Management Profile]] | Unit 2, Day 6 | Take an instrument, then test its result against how you actually behave |
| [[A Week of Your Time]] | Unit 3, Day 22 | Seven days of real data, one change made, and what happened |
| [[Feedback You Received]] | Unit 4, Day 12 | Three pieces of feedback, assessed as feedback |
| [[Your Leadership Statement]] | Unit 4, Days 11 and 22 | Your style with evidence, your weakest situation, and a route into the field |

Each entry has its own instructions on its own page. The criteria below
are the same for all four, and they are the criteria the set is marked
against at the end.

## How the set is marked

| Criterion | What earns the marks |
| --- | --- |
| Specific | Named occasions, dates, and what was actually said — never a general account of yourself |
| Tested | You check your claim against something that could contradict it, and say what you found |
| Course tools | The unit's own concepts do the analysing, named and used properly, not decorating a diary entry |
| Usable later | The conclusion is precise enough to still mean something to you in a year |

Those four rows apply to all four entries, on purpose. By the third one
you should be able to predict what a strong entry looks like before you
write it — which is what [[Judging Your Own Work]] is for.

One entry carries a fifth row, and only that one:

| Criterion | Where it applies | What earns the marks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Technique used | [[A Week of Your Time]] only | You name the time-management technique you actually ran, show the block in your own seven days that it changed, and say what it did — including if it failed |

That row exists because **D2.3** asks you to *demonstrate the ability to
use time-management techniques*, which is the one place in this course
where a habit is itself the curriculum. So the habit needs a published
standard like everything else, and this is it. Being organised in
general is still not marked anywhere — see [[How Marks Work]].

> [!warning] Honest beats flattering, and it is not close
> The weakest entries are the ones that decided the conclusion first:
> "this showed me I am a natural leader". The strongest ones find
> something the writer did not want to find and follow it anyway. A
> mismatch you report costs you nothing here and is worth more than a
> match you assert.

## Written in class, and why

Every entry is written in the period — or, for the last one, the two
periods — that the class pages name for it, not at home. Three reasons,
and they are the same three reasons a manager writes a review at the
desk rather than on the train:

- It is evaluated work, so it is done where I can see it being done. A
  reflection is the easiest thing in a course to hand off to somebody
  else, and neither of us wants that conversation.
- I read over shoulders while you write, which is the only checkpoint
  an entry this short needs — across the five writing periods that
  reaches everybody more than once. You get the "that sentence is a
  claim, where is the occasion?" while there is still time to answer
  it.
- The entries that get written at eleven at night are the general ones.
  The ones written in class, with the unit still on the board, are the
  specific ones.

Collecting the raw material — taking the instrument, tracking your
week, digging out what somebody once said to you — happens outside
class. That part is preparation and is not marked. The writing is the
task.

> [!tip] Keep them where you will find them
> [[Your Leadership Statement]] asks you to re-read
> [[Your Management Profile]] from the first week and say what changed. That
> is much easier if the file is still somewhere sensible — see
> [[The Tools of the Trade]].

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 3, Day 22, the last twenty minutes, when the decision
  section of A Week of Your Time is written in class
  Watch for: who opens their own seven days of tracking and works from a
  number in it, and who writes the decision from memory. The finished
  entry reports a technique and a change either way — plausible totals
  are easy to invent, and a page cannot show whether the record behind
  them exists. This is D2.3 (demonstrate the ability to use
  time-management techniques) seen rather than claimed.
  Going well: the student's chosen change points back at a specific
  block on a specific day, and they scroll to it to check.
  Stuck: the change is a resolution rather than a finding — "manage my
  time better", "less phone" — with no number under it.
  Record: a tick or a dash beside each name on the class list, as you
  walk the room once. Two minutes for the whole class.

TALK — Unit 2, Day 6, while you go round the room during the writing
  period for Your Management Profile
  You will reach eight or ten students in twenty minutes, not the whole
  class, so choose. This is the first entry, so nobody has a thin one
  yet: go to the students you have heard least from since the first week.
  There are five of these writing periods across the term (Unit 2 Day 6,
  Unit 3 Day 22, Unit 4 Day 11, Unit 4 Day 12 and Unit 4 Day 22) — keep
  the list, choose by thinnest written entry after this one, and everyone
  gets two conversations by the end of the course.
  Ask: "Your answers to that instrument were shaped long before you sat
  down to it. Which of your family's habits, or your community's, is in
  there — and would you have answered the same at twelve?" A strong
  answer names something specific that was normal where they grew up and

  Record: a plus, a dash or a question mark beside the name on the same
  class list, right then.
