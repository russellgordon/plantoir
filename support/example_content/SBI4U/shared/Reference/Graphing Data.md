In biology a graph is rarely an illustration of the answer. It usually IS
the answer: the slope, the intercept or the shape of the curve is the
quantity you are after.

## The rules that make a graph readable

1. **Manipulated variable on the horizontal axis**, responded on the
   vertical. Time almost always goes horizontal.
2. **Label both axes with the quantity AND its unit** — "Position (m)",
   not "Position".
3. **Scale the axes evenly**, and do not feel obliged to start at zero
   unless the intercept matters.
4. **Plot the points, then draw the trend** — a straight edge for a linear
   trend, a smooth curve otherwise. Never join the dots.

## Slope and area carry meaning

| Graph | The slope means | Where it flattens means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rate against light intensity | How strongly light limits the rate | Something else has become limiting |
| Population against time | Growth rate | Carrying capacity reached |
| Growth against nutrient concentration | Sensitivity to that nutrient | Another factor is limiting |

In biology the graphs are usually rates against a factor — growth
against light, transpiration against wind — and the shape is the result.
See [[Modelling Population Growth]].

## Straightening a curve

If a graph curves, you have not failed — you have found a relationship
that is not linear. Plotting against $x^2$, or $1/x$, and getting a
straight line tells you what the relationship IS. Plotting the log of a
population against time is exactly that trick — exponential growth becomes
a straight line whose slope is the growth rate.
