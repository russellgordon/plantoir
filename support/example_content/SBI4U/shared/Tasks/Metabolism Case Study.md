> [!abstract] At a glance
> **Format:** four pages with an original pathway diagram. **Individually.**
> **Due:** handed in on Unit 2, Day 14.
> **Assessed on:** Knowledge, Application.

Take one situation where metabolism is pushed to a limit, and explain it
at the level of the pathway.

## Choose one

A sprinter's last 100 m · a diving seal · yeast in a brewery · a person
with a mitochondrial disorder · brown fat in a hibernating mammal · a
marathon runner at thirty kilometres.

Every one of these is a story about respiration under a limit, which is
why photosynthesis is not on the list: it gets its own question on the
[[Final Examination]] instead.

## What to produce

1. **The normal pathway**, diagrammed by you, with the inputs, outputs,
   and locations marked.
2. **What changes** in your situation, and at which step.
3. **The consequences**, in ATP yield and in what the organism can then
   do.
4. **The evidence**: one measurement, from a source you cite, that shows
   the effect is real.
5. **Your own measurement.** Run a bench investigation into respiration
   — [[Fermentation Rate]] is the standard one — and report the products
   you detected and the rate you measured, with its uncertainty. Use the
   proper terminology throughout: glycolysis, the Krebs cycle, the
   electron transport chain, energy carriers, the proton pump,
   chemiosmosis, ATP synthase, oxidative and substrate-level
   phosphorylation, aerobic and anaerobic. A case study written entirely
   from sources is a literature review, not an investigation.

6. **The system it sits in.** Half a page. What outside the cell sets the
   limit in your case — the oxygen supply, the light, the temperature,
   the water, the substrate that runs out — and one place where the same
   metabolic process has to be managed or is deliberately put to work
   beyond your organism: a fermenter, a wastewater treatment plant, a
   crop in a hot field, a patient on a ward. Then the decision that
   changes for whoever is doing the managing.

## Not accepted

A pathway diagram copied from anywhere. Draw it — badly is fine; copied is
not.

## How this is assessed

Published on launch day, Unit 2, Day 7. Run them against your draft with
[[Judging Your Own Work]] on Unit 2, Day 12.

| Quality | What it looks like in your case study |
| --- | --- |
| The pathway is yours | Drawn by you, with inputs, outputs and locations marked, and correct at the level of the stage rather than the arrow |
| The change is located | Named at a step, not at a process — "the electron transport chain" is a region, "complex IV, with oxygen unavailable" is a location |
| The consequences are counted | ATP yield worked rather than quoted, and followed through to what the organism can then do and for how long |
| Evidence from outside your head | One measurement from a cited source that shows the effect is real, with what it was measured on |
| Evidence from your own bench | Products identified and a rate reported from your respiration investigation, with its uncertainty and to a number of figures the apparatus supports |
| The vocabulary does the work | Glycolysis, the Krebs cycle, the electron transport chain, energy carriers, proton pump, chemiosmosis, ATP synthase, oxidative phosphorylation, substrate-level phosphorylation, aerobic and anaerobic — the ones your case touches, used precisely enough that swapping two of them would make the sentence wrong |
| The system around it | What outside the cell sets the limit, one managed or industrial setting where the same process matters, and the decision it changes there |

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 2, Day 11, the bench period on the respiration investigation
  Watch for: what happens to the control tube. It has no sugar and it is
  supposed to produce nothing, which makes it the easiest thing in the
  room to set up carelessly and the only thing that tells them their gas
  came from fermentation at all. Also: whether the ten minutes is timed
  from the moment of mixing or from the moment somebody looks up.
  Going well: the control set up with the same care as the four that
  matter, and a note made of the bubbles it produced anyway.
  Stuck: the control abandoned once the sugars start working, or filled
  last with whatever is left.
  Record: one tick per group — control run properly, or not. The write-up
  reports a ranking of sugars; a missing control does not make the
  ranking look any different.

TALK — Unit 2, Day 10, at the conference already on that agenda
  Ask: "You will measure one rate at the bench. Suppose it comes out twice
  as fast as you expect — what in your case study would you have to
  rewrite, and what would you have to leave alone?"
  Then: "Whose number is your case resting on more, yours or the
  paper's?"
  A strong answer separates the parts of the argument that depend on a
  measured rate from the parts that depend on the pathway being what it
  is, which is C3.1 reasoned rather than recited. The finished case study
  presents both as one settled story, so you cannot see the seam in it
  afterwards.
  Record: two columns on the conference sheet — separated the two, or
  treated every claim as equally supported.

The product evidence is the case study, handed in on Unit 2, Day 14.
