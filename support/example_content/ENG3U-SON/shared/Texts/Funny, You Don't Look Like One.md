Drew Hayden Taylor is Anishinaabe — Michi Saagiig, from Curve Lake First
Nation in Ontario — and one of Canada's best-known Indigenous playwrights
and humorists. Raised by his mother at Curve Lake and never having known
his father, Taylor has spent much of his career writing, often through
humour, about what it means to belong to two heritages that most people
around him assume must be one or the other. *Funny, You Don't Look Like
One: Observations from a Blue-Eyed Ojibway* collects columns and essays
built out of exactly that experience.[^1]

[^1]: Taylor went on to expand this into a four-volume series under the
    same *Funny, You Don't Look Like One* title — this course reads only
    from the original, first collection.

## Why we're reading it

Unit 4 turns from literary texts to persuasive non-fiction and media, and
Taylor's essays are the bridge: personal, funny, and unmistakably making
an argument the whole time, even when the tone is light. He often opens a
piece with a joke and lands, a paragraph later, somewhere much more
serious — which makes his writing a genuinely useful model for the
persuasive voice this unit is asking you to build.

## What to watch for on a first read

Notice where the humour is doing the argument's work, not just decorating
it — a joke that makes a reader laugh can also be the moment an essay
makes its sharpest point, and Taylor uses that constantly. Notice, too,
how often he addresses assumptions readers might be making about him
directly, rather than around them.

> [!quote]
> "I've spent too many years explaining who and what I am repeatedly, so
> as of this moment, I officially secede from both races."

That line, from the opening of this collection, is the closest thing
Taylor gives you to a thesis for the whole book — worth returning to once
you've read a few of the essays yourself.
