Grade 11 English in Ontario has traditionally been the Canadian
literature course. That makes one question unavoidable: what is being
called Canadian, and who decided?

## Four things a Canadian text is often doing

1. **Naming a specific place** — and the naming is rarely neutral. A
   town, a reserve, a river, a road: whose name, and what was it called
   before?
2. **Writing about arrival**, or about being here first, or about being
   made to leave.
3. **Handling official stories** — a national account of itself that
   individual texts keep interrupting.
4. **Deciding what language to write in**, which for many writers here is
   a live question rather than a default.

## The two texts this unit puts side by side

[[Monkey Beach]] is a novel by Eden Robinson, who is Haisla and Heiltsuk,
set in her own community. [[The Inconvenient Indian]] is Thomas King's
argument about how stories about Indigenous people have been told in
North America, and by whom — written, as it turned out, from a position
its author did not hold. See that page before you read it.

Reading them together produces a question the course keeps returning to,
and the pairing now asks it twice over: what happens when a story about a
community is told from inside it, and what happens to an argument about
who gets to tell it when the arguer was not who he said he was? The
second question does not cancel the first, and it is not a reason to stop
reading King. It is the reason this unit is worth a month.

## What we do not do

- Treat one text as speaking for a nation, a people, or a century.
- Convert a specific family's grief into a general symbol because that is
  easier to write essays about.
- Assume a text's job is to educate us.

## What we do

- Name the writer's nation and community.
- Read what the text says about land as a claim about land.
- Notice when a text withholds — and treat the withholding as a decision.
