> [!abstract] At a glance
> Individual · **three submissions** across the semester — one in
> the first unit, one in the middle, one in the final weeks · each **written in
> class**, in the period it is handed in · **Format:** one page each, five
> questions answered

Finding the article is the part that is yours to do between classes. Taking
it apart happens here, with me in the room.

## The task

Find a news article making a scientific claim, and take it apart.

## Every submission answers these

1. **The claim.** In one sentence, in your own words.
2. **The source of the evidence.** Is there a study behind it? Who paid for it?
   Can you find the study itself, or only reporting *about* it?
3. **The sample.** How many? Of what? Over how long?
4. **The gap.** What does the headline say that the study does not?
5. **Your verdict.** Believable, overstated, or wrong — and what would change
   your mind.

> [!example] Where headlines usually go wrong
> - **Correlation reported as cause.** "Coffee drinkers live longer" — or do
>   people who can afford coffee have better health care?
> - **Sample too small to matter.** Twelve participants is a pilot study.
> - **Relative risk without absolute risk.** "Doubles your risk" means little
>   if the risk was 1 in 2 million.
> - **A single study treated as settled.** One result is a hypothesis with
>   evidence, not a conclusion.

## One of the three has to matter to somebody

At least one of your submissions must take on a **social or environmental
issue** — something with a consequence for people, here or elsewhere: air
quality, a drug trial, a fishery, a heat wave, a landfill. Pick that one
deliberately rather than hoping it happens. Taking apart a claim about a
distant galaxy is good practice; taking apart a claim that changes what
somebody does next is the skill this course is actually after.

## For the third submission

Choose an article about **artificial intelligence in science**, and additionally
address: what work is the AI actually doing, and what claim is being made about
it that the evidence does not support? See
[[A2.2|what this course asks about emerging technologies]].

## Success criteria

The same five rows all three times, so that the third submission can be laid
beside the first and the difference read straight off. Use them with
[[Judging Your Own Work]] while there is still time in the period.

| Quality | What it looks like on your page |
| --- | --- |
| The claim, stated fairly | One sentence in your own words that the article's author would recognise as theirs — not the version that is easiest to knock down |
| The evidence tracked down | The study named, or the plain statement that there is no study to name; who paid for it, where that is findable |
| The sample described | How many, of what, over how long — in numbers, not "a lot of people" |
| The gap made specific | The words the headline uses set beside the words the study uses, both quoted |
| A verdict you could defend | Believable, overstated, or wrong, with the one thing that would change your mind named |

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

All three submissions are written in class, which is the only reason either of
these is available at all. A page written at a kitchen table gives you the
product and nothing else.

OBSERVE — Unit 1, Day 12, the first thirty minutes, while submission one is
written
  Watch for: the order the five questions get answered in. Plenty of students
  write the verdict first and then go looking for evidence that agrees with
  it; the finished page is in numbered order either way, so it cannot show you
  which happened.
  Going well: question 5 is still blank at the twenty-minute mark, and the
  sample size is already on the page.
  Stuck: the verdict written, boxed, and underlined before anything has been
  looked up.
  Record: three columns down your day plan — verdict last, verdict first, or
  changed their mind partway. The third column is the interesting one, and it
  is worth a sentence in your notes about what changed it.

TALK — Unit 3, Day 10, the last half hour, while submission two is written
  You are circulating anyway, and everybody has their article open. The five
  questions themselves are printed on this page, so asking any of them back
  tells you only that they can read.
  Ask: "Which of the five questions actually changed your mind this time, and
  which one did you answer because the page asks for it?"
  Then: "Name a headline you decided not to use this term. What made you put
  it down?"
  A strong answer names one question and one moment — most often the sample,
  and a number they can still say out loud — and the rejected headline comes
  with a reason about the evidence rather than about the topic being boring.
  That is A2.4 heard: scientific literacy applied to something with a real
  personal or local stake, which a page of five tidy paragraphs can imitate
  without any of it having happened.
  Record: one line per student in the margin of your day plan — and you will
  not reach everybody in half an hour, so take a third of the class each time
  this task comes round. Across Unit 1, Day 12, Unit 3, Day 10 and Unit
  5, Day 12 that is everybody, most of them twice.

The product evidence is the three submissions, handed in at the end of the
periods they were written in — Unit 1, Day 12; Unit 3, Day 10; and Unit 5,
Day 12.
