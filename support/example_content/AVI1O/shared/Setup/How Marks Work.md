You are marked on what you make, and on how you think about what you
make — not on how much art you had done before September.

## The seventy and the thirty

Every Grade 9 credit in Ontario is built the same way. **Seventy per
cent** of your mark comes from work spread across the whole semester,
and it leans towards your **most recent and most consistent** work
rather than averaging your first month against your last. That matters
more in this subject than in most: almost nobody's September drawing is
their best one, and it is not meant to be.

The other **thirty per cent** comes from a single final evaluation at
the end of the course, which is deliberately built to reach back across
the whole of it rather than test the last unit.

**The seventy** is the eight tasks you do during the semester, in
roughly the order you meet them: [[The Elements Study]],
[[The Sketchbook Habit]], [[The Media Trials]],
[[The Information Piece]], [[The Interpretation]],
[[Art and Society Study]], [[The Body of Work]], and
[[The Exhibition]]. They are not equally weighted — the body of work
and the exhibition run for weeks and ask for everything at once, and
the earlier tasks are the rehearsals that make them possible. Ask me
for the exact split whenever you want it. It is not a secret; it is
just not the useful thing to memorise.

**The thirty** is [[The Portfolio and Reflection]]: your portfolio
handed in complete, plus writing done in the examination period about a
work you have never seen. A course that spends its whole timetable
making things should not be settled by an hour of writing about
somebody else's, so the portfolio is genuinely half of it.

Everything else on the timetable — studio periods, critiques,
conferences, gallery walks, the sketchbook checks between markings — is
how the work gets better before it is marked. None of it is a mark in
itself. What I see and hear on those days is still evidence about the
tasks that are marked, which is what the section after next is about.

## Four kinds of thinking, not one

Every task asks for more than one of these, and the balance shifts with
the task. A finished-looking object is never the whole of it.

| What is being judged | What it means here |
| --- | --- |
| What you know | The elements and principles, the right word for a tool or a technique, the stages of both processes |
| How you think | Choosing an idea, choosing a medium, reading a work from the evidence in it, deciding what to change |
| How you communicate | What the work itself says to a viewer, and what your labels, statements, writing and talk add |
| Where you apply it | Making something work in a situation nobody walked you through |

The fourth is why [[The Information Piece]] has a real audience and why
the examination gives you a work nobody has taught you. Anyone can
repeat the demonstration; the course is asking whether you can handle
the picture you have not been shown.

## Three kinds of evidence, and two of them are not on paper

**What you make** is the obvious one: the studies, the trials, the
pieces, the sketchbook, the writing.

**What I watch you do** is the second, and in a studio it is enormous.
What you do with a piece that has stopped working. Whether you test a
technique on scrap before committing it to the good paper. Whether a
decision was made or drifted into. None of that survives into the
finished object, and some of it is the strongest evidence in the room.

**What you tell me** is the third. The conferences on studio days are
not a progress check — they are evidence in their own right, and there
are things you understand that will only ever show up in one.

If you are quiet and your piece is unfinished, the conference is often
where the best evidence of the day comes from. That is not a
consolation prize. It is how the mark is meant to work.

## What earns a high mark here

- **Process evidence.** The sketchbook shows the thinking that led to
  the piece: sources, false starts, the version you abandoned —
  [[The Sketchbook]].
- **Revision.** The second version is genuinely better and you can
  point at what changed and why — [[Looking Twice]].
- **Written self-assessment that is honest.** You can name a weakness
  in your own work in writing, with evidence, and say what you would do
  about it — [[Judging Your Own Work]] is the routine, and
  [[Assessing Your Own Work]] is the harder judgement underneath it.
- **Accurate terminology.** The right word for the medium, the tool,
  the technique, and the element — used because it is precise, not
  decorative.
- **Safe and responsible practice.** Handling, clean-up, sourcing, and
  credit are assessed, not assumed — [[Materials and Safety]] and
  [[Copyright, Ownership, and Credit]].

## Feedback arrives while you can still use it

On every one of those eight tasks there is a critique or a conference
that looks at the work while it is still unfinished, and at least one
working period after it and before the due date whose job is acting on
what you heard. That period is on the class page as studio time, and it
is the cheapest mark improvement available in this course. The final
evaluation is the one piece of work with no such period, and it is why
the portfolio is built across the whole course rather than assembled at
the end of it.

Be careful about which one you count. There is often a second
conference the day before something is due, and that one is for
finishing, not for rethinking — if you are still deciding what the
piece is about at that point, you have used the wrong period. Nothing
said in a critique changes anyone's mark, so the only thing it can do
for you is change the work, and only while there is time left to change
it.

## What is not in your mark

How you work is reported separately, on the same report card, as **E,
G, S or N** — six habits: responsibility, organization, independent
work, collaboration, initiative, and self-regulation. They matter, we
will talk about them, and they do not move your percentage. Your mark
is about the work; that column is about the worker, and mixing the two
tells you less about both. So there is no mark here for turning up, for
how tidy your bench is, or for how hard a piece of work looked to make.
What you do with a blade, a solvent and a sink IS marked, and the next
paragraph says why.

There is one exception, and it is narrow. Where a habit is written into
the curriculum itself, it is marked like anything else in the
curriculum, and in this course that is the whole of Strand C's
responsible-practice section and nothing else. C3.2 asks you to
demonstrate safe and conscientious practice with materials and tools;
C3.1 asks you to demonstrate legal and ethical practice; C3.3 asks you
to apply environmentally responsible practice. Each has a row of its own
— safe practice on [[The Media Trials]], ethics and environment on
[[The Exhibition]] — and each has the studio days that go with it.
Nothing outside those three is a habit you are marked on.

Your own judgement of your work is not part of your mark either, and
neither is a classmate's. You will judge your own work against the
criteria often, because it is the fastest way to get better — see
[[Judging Your Own Work]] — and the class will tell you what your work
did to them in every critique. None of it is a rating and none of it
reaches the mark. What I evaluate is your written analysis: the
thinking in it, and whether the evidence you point at is really there.

## Late work

Tell me before the due date and we will set a new one. Work handed in
late without that conversation is still marked, but the feedback
arrives after it is useful to you, which is the real cost. A piece that
is genuinely unfinished on the day is better shown unfinished than not
shown at all — that is a conversation, not a zero.
