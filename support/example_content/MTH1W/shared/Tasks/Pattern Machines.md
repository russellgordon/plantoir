> Groups of 3 · built and traded across seven classes · one pattern,

## What you are making

Your group will build a **pattern machine**: a growing visual pattern of
your own design, expressed five ways — in words, as a table of values,
as an algebraic expression, as a graph, and as running code. The five
representations must agree with each other perfectly, because another
group is going to test them.

Then the machines swap. Your group receives another group's pattern and
decodes it, building your own expression for their rule — and predicts
term 100 two independent ways: algebraically, and by running their code.
If the two predictions disagree, some representation is keeping a
secret, and finding it is part of the task.

## How to work

1. Design a growing pattern you find genuinely interesting — the ones in
   [[Visual Patterns]] are your starting gallery, but yours should not
   be one of them. Build terms 1 to 4 so the growth is visible.
2. Say the rule in words first. Then a table. Then generalise into an
   expression — [[Algebraic Expressions]] shows the move from "what
   stays, what grows" to $an + b$.
3. Graph it. Then put your table and graph beside [[The Painted Cube]]'s
   — one of the two is linear and the other is not — and write down what
   in the TABLE separates them, and what in the GRAPH.
   [[Linear Relations]] tells you what to look for, and why the answer
   matters.
4. Code the rule so it can print any term — [[Coding Visual Patterns]]
   has the skeleton. Test that your code matches your table.
5. Swap machines. You receive the other group's pictures and table, and
   their code stays sealed until you have written your own expression
   from them.
6. Predict their term 100 algebraically, then run their code out to
   100 and compare. Disagreement is not failure — it is information
   about which representation to go back and check.

Every build day is a class day. That is deliberate: work that carries a
mark is built in the room, where I can watch a group argue with its own
table — [[How Marks Work]] explains why. Before the demos you will judge
your machine against the table below, using [[Judging Your Own Work]],
with a working period left to fix the weakest row.

## What is marked as yours

The machine belongs to the group; the mark does not. On decode day each
member writes their own **decode note** — half a page, in class, on
which representation you trusted while reading the other group's
machine, where your two predictions of term 100 parted company (or why
you believe them both), and what you would now change about your own
machine. Your group's spoken reflection is not a substitute for it, and
nobody may write it for you.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like in your machine |
| --- | --- |
| A real rule | The pattern grows by a rule a stranger could continue |
| Agreement | Words, table, expression, graph, and code all match |
| Justified expression | The $an + b$ comes from the picture, not a guess |
| Working code | The code runs, and prints the same values as the table |
| Visible decoding | Your reasoning about the other machine is on the page |
| Linear or not | You name what in a table, and what in a graph, tells a linear rule from one that is not |
| Two roads to 100 | Term 100 is defended algebraically and by running code |
| Your own decode note | Your half page names the representation you trusted and what it cost you |

## Reflect

After the swap, your group records a short spoken reflection together:
which representation did you trust most while decoding, which one did
the other group say made your machine clearest, and what would you
redesign if the machine shipped again?

> [!success]- If your group is stuck decoding (click to expand)
> Look at what stays the same in every term, and what gets added each
> time. The constant part of the picture is the $b$; the growing part,
> counted per step, is the $a$. Colour them differently on the printout
> and the expression almost writes itself.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 2, Day 7, the period where the three representations must
agree
  Watch what a group does the moment two of them disagree. Going back to
  the drawn terms and recounting is the mathematics; quietly editing the
  table row until it matches the expression is not. Both routes end with
  three representations that agree, and the finished machine looks the
  same either way.
  That is C3.2 forming or not forming — the connections between the
  representations are either being made or being faked, and only the
  period shows which.
  Going well: somebody puts a finger on term 3 and counts it again out
  loud.
  Stuck: a number in the table changes and nobody looks at the picture.
  Record: three columns on the day plan — recounted, edited, not yet in
  disagreement.

TALK — Unit 2, Day 13, while they are coding, group by group
  Ask: "Walk me through what your code does for term 7 — what happens
  first, and what is the variable holding by the end?"
  Then: "What did you have to decide that your expression on paper never
  told you?"
  A strong answer names a decision the code forced: where the count
  starts, whether the loop runs n times or one fewer, what the variable
  is holding at the end of a pass. That is C2.2 — decomposing a
  situation into computational steps — and a program that prints the
  right numbers hides every one of those decisions.
  Record: three words per student — the decision they named. The code
  is the group's; the mark is not.

The product evidence is the machine demonstrated on Day 16, the decoded
expression from Day 15, and each member's decode note written in class
that day.
