Shirley Jackson was an American writer, born in San Francisco in 1916 and
based for most of her adult life in North Bennington, Vermont — a small New
England town not unlike the one this story is set in. "The Lottery" was
published in *The New Yorker* on June 26, 1948, and the magazine received
more mail about it than about any piece of fiction it had ever printed
before, most of it bewildered or angry. It has been anthologized more than
almost any other American short story since.

## Why we're reading it

Where "Boys and Girls" showed you a change arriving quietly, this story
shows you an entire community agreeing, without ever quite saying so, not
to question something. It's a short, plain-spoken piece of writing that
turns out to be doing something much stranger than its opening paragraphs
let on — small-town neighbours, a box, a summer morning, and a tradition
nobody in the story stops to ask the point of.

## What to watch for on a first read

Jackson gives you almost nothing at the start to suggest where this is
heading — the strangeness builds through details you might not register as
strange the first time past them: what the box is made of, how casually
people talk while they wait, which details about "the lottery" the
villagers explain to each other and which they don't. Notice what nobody in
the story questions out loud, and notice who benefits from that silence.

> [!warning]- A note before you start
> This story builds toward a moment of ritual violence against one member
> of the community. Nothing here will spell out what that moment is — that
> is the story's own reveal to make, not this page's — but if you'd rather
> know the shape of what's coming before you read, ask.
