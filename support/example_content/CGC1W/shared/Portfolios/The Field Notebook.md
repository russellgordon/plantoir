One notebook, carried to every field visit this term, written on site and
never afterwards from memory. It is the first thing in your portfolio and
the thing everything else is built on.

## What goes on every page

Start each entry with the same four items, in the same place, every time:
the **date**, the **time**, the **location** — a place name plus
coordinates from your phone or a GPS — and the **weather**, including
which way the wind was coming from. Those four turn an observation into
data. Without them you have a memory, and a memory compares with nothing.

Then record what is there, keeping the two kinds of note apart:
measurements and counts on one side, impressions on the other. Both
matter; confusing them is what makes fieldwork unusable.

## Sketching

You will resist this, and should do it anyway. A field sketch is not art;
it is a labelled diagram. Draw the shape of the ground, then name only
what your question needs: the water line, the slope, the tree edge, the
fence. Add a north arrow and a rough scale. A photograph records
everything and so emphasizes nothing; a sketch records your reasoning.

## Why dated beats remembered

Six weeks after [[The Shoreline Study]] you will be certain the beach was
steeper at the north end. Your notebook will say otherwise, or say
nothing, and both beat certainty. This is also what makes it a **primary
source** — evidence you generated, which [[Judging a Source]] weighs
differently from anything you found published.

> [!tip] Write the thing you cannot explain
> The entry that pays off later is the one that says "the grass stops in
> a straight line here and I don't know why." Questions recorded on site
> become the inquiry questions you actually have evidence for. Answers
> invented on site become mistakes you will defend.

## Across the term

The notebook travels to [[The Woodlot Study]], [[The Farm Visit]], and
[[The Land Use Walk]], and every entry is governed by
[[Fieldwork Safety and Ethics]] — including who owns the ground you stand
on. Four of those entries are the marked ones — [[The Field Record]] says
which, and what each has to do — and your evidence for
[[The Risk Report]] comes from these pages too, so leave margins wide
enough to annotate later.
