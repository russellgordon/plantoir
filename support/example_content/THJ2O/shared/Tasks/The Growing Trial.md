**Set up in the first two weeks; results due at the end of Unit 1.**
Pairs — with an individual mark.

One variable, changed on purpose, with everything else held the same —
and an honest account of what happened.

The trial is run by two people and the data belongs to both of you. Your
mark does not. **Sections 5 and 6 below are written by you alone,
signed, and marked as yours**, and so are your own dated entries in the
trial log. There is no shared mark: if your partner explains the
physiology beautifully and you cannot, that is their mark, not yours.

## The trial

Choose one factor and test it: light level, water frequency, temperature,
growing medium, sowing depth, or spacing. Grow at least two groups —
one changed, one not — with enough plants in each that one failure does
not end the trial.

Run it for a fortnight of classes, measuring at least twice a week.

## What you hand in

1. **The question**, written as something measurable.
2. **The method**, in enough detail that another pair could repeat it.
3. **The data**, recorded as you went — dated, in a table, with units.
4. **What happened**, described before it is explained.
5. **Why**, using the physiology from this unit — germination, water
   uptake, photosynthesis, respiration.
6. **What you would change**, and what you could not control.

## What is marked

| Criterion | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Control | One variable changed; the rest genuinely held constant |
| Measurement | Consistent, dated, in proper units |
| Explanation | Physiology, not guesswork |
| Honesty | Failures reported and accounted for |
| Record | The log was kept as you went, not written up afterwards |

The first four rows are about a bench two of you set up, so they are
judged on your named part of it: the entries in your own hand, the
set-up I watched you do, and what you can tell me about the trial when
I ask you on your own. Rows five and six are your writing already.

> [!tip] A dead trial is still a trial
> If everything died, say so, work out why, and report it. Trials that
> fail teach the physiology better than trials that succeed, and marks
> here follow the reasoning rather than the plants.

[[How a Plant Actually Works]] and
[[Light, Water, Temperature, Soil]] are what you will be explaining
with.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

All three codes on this task are "describe" expectations, so the
conversation carries most of the weight. What the set-up period gives
you is whether there is anything true to describe.

OBSERVE — Unit 1, Day 7, trial set-up on the bench
  Watch for: whether the control tray is filled alongside the treatment
  tray, or added at the end of the period because the task asks for one.
  Then whether the label carries the variable and the date, or only a
  species name. A2.1 asks them to describe the environmental factors
  affecting growth, and a method paragraph written in Day 18's writing
  period can describe factors the bench never actually held constant.
  Going well: two trays filled a row at a time, labels written before
  the seed goes in.
  Stuck: one beautiful tray and a control assembled in the last five
  minutes.
  Record: sketch the bench layout once, then a tick per pair against
  their position. One minute of drawing, and it stays useful until the
  trays come off the bench.

TALK — Unit 1, Day 13, during the trial measurements, each partner on
their own
  Not Day 9: that period's lesson IS why the same conditions give
  different results, so anything you hear there is ten minutes old.
  Day 13 is a tool-maintenance period with measurements in it, and the
  trial is nowhere near the front of their minds, which is the point.
  Ask: "Two seeds went in the same tray at the same depth. One is up and
  one is not. What is going on inside the one that has not appeared?"
  Then: "Your two groups have pulled apart. Name what is happening
  differently inside the plants, not what you did to them."
  A strong answer reaches for a process, not a circumstance: uptake,
  photosynthesis, respiration, a seed still living off its own store.
  That is A1.3 and A2.2 in the same breath, and both are "describe"
  expectations, so this is the mode they were written for. Hearing it on
  Day 13 tells you whether the pair understood it; reading it on Day 19
  only tells you they found it.
  Record: two entries side by side on the bench sketch, one under each
  name. The report is written together; the sketch is where the two of
  them come apart.

The product evidence is the trial write-up handed in on Day 19.
