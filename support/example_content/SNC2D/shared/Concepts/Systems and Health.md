The microscope you used in [[Looking at Tissue]] is a medical imaging
technology. That is not a stretch — a pathologist looking at a stained slice of
tissue is doing what you did, with better optics and a great deal more
training, and for a large number of conditions that slide is still the thing
that settles the diagnosis. Everything on this page follows from one problem:
the systems in this unit are sealed inside a living person, and to treat them
you first have to see them without opening them up.

## Four ways to see inside without cutting

Each of these technologies exploits a different physical property, and each
one's strengths and limits come straight out of the physics.

> [!info]- How each one actually forms its picture
> **X-ray.** Dense material absorbs X-rays more strongly than soft tissue, so
> what reaches the detector is a shadow. Bone shows beautifully, soft tissue
> barely at all. Fast, cheap, widely available; uses ionising radiation, and
> everything is flattened into one plane.
>
> **CT.** The same X-ray absorption, but measured from many angles as the
> source rotates around the patient, with a computer reconstructing
> cross-sections from those projections. You gain three-dimensional detail and
> pay for it with a considerably larger radiation dose than a single X-ray.
>
> **Ultrasound.** High-frequency sound pulses are sent into the body and the
> echoes from boundaries between tissues are timed. No ionising radiation at
> all, which is why it is the standard choice in pregnancy; it is portable and
> inexpensive, but sound does not travel usefully through bone or gas, so the
> lungs and the adult brain are largely out of reach.
>
> **MRI.** A very strong magnetic field aligns hydrogen nuclei in the body's
> water and fat; a radio pulse knocks them out of alignment, and the signal
> they give off as they realign differs from tissue to tissue. Superb
> soft-tissue contrast with no ionising radiation — at the cost of a long
> scan, a loud and confining machine, high expense, and real danger to anyone
> carrying certain metal implants.

The pattern to take away is that there is no best imaging technology. There is
a question, and a technology whose physics suits it. A suspected fracture and a
suspected torn ligament are the same limb and two completely different
machines.

## What a technology costs, and who pays it

Assessing a technology means more than describing how it works. The honest
account includes at least these:

- **Risk.** Ionising radiation carries a small increase in long-term cancer
  risk that scales with dose, which is why a CT scan is ordered when the
  information is worth it and not as a matter of routine.
- **Access.** A machine that exists is not the same as a machine you can get
  to. In Canada, availability and waiting times differ sharply between cities
  and rural or northern communities, and a technology that requires a
  thousand-kilometre trip is not equally available to everyone.
- **Cost.** Money spent on one machine is money not spent elsewhere in the
  same health system.
- **What a picture cannot tell you.** Imaging finds structures. Whether a
  structure found by chance is actually causing harm is a separate question,
  and often a hard one.

That last point is where most of the interesting argument lives, and it is
exactly the kind of claim [[What Counts as Evidence]] asks you to weigh.

## Public health works on populations, not patients

Individual treatment is one lever. The other is changing the odds for an
entire population before anyone is sick, and the strategies look different
because the reasoning is different.

**Screening programmes** test people who have no symptoms — cervical screening
and HPV testing, mammography, colorectal screening. The logic is that finding a
cancer early, while it is still local, makes treatment far more likely to work.
The trade-off is genuine and is not a scandal: any screening test produces some
false positives, leading to follow-up procedures for people who turn out to be
well, and it can detect slow-growing disease that would never have caused harm.
This is why screening programmes have age ranges and intervals rather than
being offered to everybody constantly, and why the measure of success is
whether the programme reduces deaths — not how many things it finds.

**Vaccination** removes susceptibility rather than detecting disease. The HPV
vaccine is unusual and worth noticing: it prevents infection by viruses that
cause several cancers, so it is a cancer-prevention programme delivered years
before any cell has gone wrong. The MMR vaccine works at the level of the
population as well as the individual — when enough people are immune, the
chain of transmission breaks and the small number who cannot be vaccinated are
protected too.

**Education and access** — HIV prevention and treatment being the clearest
example — change behaviour and remove barriers rather than acting on tissue at
all. It is still systems biology: the intervention is upstream of the organ.

Take a technology or a programme apart properly in
[[The Systems Case Study]], where the assessment, not the description, is the
work.
