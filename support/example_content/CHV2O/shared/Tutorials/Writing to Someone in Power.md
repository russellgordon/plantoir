Elected officials receive fewer letters than you would guess, and on local
issues a great many receive almost none. A specific, polite, well-argued
message from someone in the riding is read. This tutorial is how to write
one that gets an answer.

## Before you write

**Find the right person.** A letter to the wrong government is forwarded
or filed. Use [[Who Decides What, and Where]] and, if you are unsure,
[[Who Decided This|Who Decided This?]] to trace the actual authority. Federal members are
listed at ourcommons.ca, Ontario members at ola.org, councillors on your
municipality's site. Most offices prioritise people in their own riding
or ward and say so openly; being outside it does not disqualify you, but
it changes what to expect.

**Find the moment.** A letter three weeks before a budget vote or a
committee meeting can affect something. The same letter three weeks after
cannot. [[Bills and What Happens to Them]] tells you which stage a bill
has reached.

## The shape of the letter

| Part | What it does | Length |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Subject line | Names the issue and what you want | One line |
| Who you are | Constituent, student, affected how | One or two sentences |
| The ask | The specific thing you want them to do | One sentence, early |
| Why | Two or three reasons, at least one with evidence | A short paragraph |
| The other side | The strongest objection, and your answer | Two or three sentences |
| Close | Thank them, ask for a reply, give contact details | Two lines |

Around 300 words. Longer letters are not read more carefully; they are
read less.

## What makes the difference

**Be specific about the ask.** "Do something about transit" cannot be
acted on. "Vote against the fare increase in the operating budget on the
14th" can, and the office knows at once whether it agrees.

**Say what it costs you.** A concrete consequence in your own life is
evidence an office cannot get from a briefing note, and it is the part
most likely to be quoted at a meeting.

**Anticipate the objection**, and answer it. That is what separates a
letter from a complaint.

**Be courteous, including when angry** — not for their sake, but because
a rude letter gives the office an easy reason to file it.

> [!success]- A skeleton you can adapt
> *Subject: Please oppose the closure of the Elm Street branch — I live
> in this ward*
>
> "I am a Grade 10 student living on X Street in your ward, writing to
> ask you to vote against the branch closure at the January council
> meeting. I use the branch after school three days a week; without it
> the nearest is a 40-minute bus ride, which for me means it is not
> available. I understand the budget pressure and that circulation there
> is below average — but circulation does not count after-school use,
> which staff record separately, and those figures are in the library
> board's own service report. I would be grateful for a reply, and would
> gladly speak at the meeting if that is possible."

## Digital channels, social media, and formal deputations

How you deliver your message depends on the decision and the timeline:

- **Constituent email and mail**: Direct, formal, and logged by staff into the
  official constituent correspondence database.
- **Online consultation portals**: Many municipalities and ministries run
  digital portals where citizens submit evidence directly into public
  consultation records.
- **Social media advocacy**: Public posts and tagging officials can build broad
  awareness and demonstrate community concern, but rarely replace a detailed
  formal submission on complex policy questions.
- **Public deputations**: Registering to speak for five minutes at a committee
  meeting places your spoken testimony directly on Hansard or the council
  record.

## After you send it

Keep a copy, note the date, and note when a reply arrives. A reply, a
form reply, and silence are three different findings, and all three
belong in your [[The Civic Action Project]] report exactly as they
happened.
