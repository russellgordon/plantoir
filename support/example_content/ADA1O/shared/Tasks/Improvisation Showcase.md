## What you are making

You and a partner will perform a short improvised scene built from three
slips of paper drawn from three envelopes: a **location**, a
**relationship**, and an **objective** — something one character wants from
the other. You will not know your slips until moments before you begin.
There is no script and no rehearsal of the scene itself; what you rehearse,
for two weeks of in-class games and exercises, is the skill of building a
scene with another person in real time.

Performed cold does not mean unprepared. It means the preparation is in
you, not on paper.

## How to work

The whole showcase rests on one habit: **accept and build**. Whatever your
partner offers, you treat as true and add to it — "yes, and".

- If your partner says "Doctor, it's spreading," you are a doctor now, and
  it is spreading. Denying the offer ("I'm not a doctor") is called
  *blocking*, and it kills a scene faster than any bad idea can.
- Make your partner look brilliant. The strongest improvisers are not the
  funniest — they are the most generous. That generosity is the same trust
  we built in [[Why Trust Comes First]].
- Let the objective drive you. A character who wants something is
  interesting; two characters who want different things is a scene. That
  gap is [[Tension|tension]], and it is your engine.
- Use [[Voice]] and [[Movement and Gesture|movement]] to establish who and
  where you are within the first few seconds, so your partner has
  something solid to build on. [[Status]] is the fastest tool you have:
  who stands taller, who looks away first.

> [!note] You play with a partner, and you are marked as yourself
> There is no shared mark for a pair. What I judge is your offers, your
> acceptances, and the character your voice and body make readable — not
> whether your partner had a good night. A scene can go sideways and you
> can still meet every criterion below; that is most of what improvisers
> get paid for.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Accepting offers | Every offer is treated as true and extended — no blocking |
| Building | Each exchange adds new information; the scene moves somewhere |
| Character | Voice and body make who-you-are readable without announcing it |
| Objective | We can tell what your character wants without being told |
| Partnership | Both players share focus; neither drags nor dominates |

> [!success]- If you go blank (click to expand)
> Going blank is normal, and there is a reliable exit: stop inventing and
> react instead. Look at your partner, name what you actually see — "you're
> shaking" — and the scene restarts itself. The audience never knows the
> difference between a plan and a recovery.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 2, Day 9, the working period rebuilding a game around mimed
objects
  Watch the gaps between rounds, not the rounds. A player who arrives at
  the showcase with a plan will build that plan out loud in rehearsal, and
  once it is performed you cannot tell a plan from a save — that is exactly
  what makes improvisation hard to assess from the product alone.
  Going well: between rounds they talk about what they noticed — "I missed
  your offer about the dog" — and then go again.
  Stuck: a pair in the corner scripting the content of the next round — who
  they will be, what will happen — rather than the rules of the game they
  are meant to be rebuilding.
  Record: two columns on your class list, noticing and scripting, one tick
  per student. It takes about four minutes for a whole class.

TALK — Unit 2, Day 10, the conference already on that agenda
  The agenda has already told them you will ask which game they would cut,
  so they will have that one ready. Use the two minutes for these instead.
  Ask: "Which of your partner's offers did you nearly refuse this week, and
  what stopped you?"
  Then: "What is the offer you keep making — the one you reach for when you
  have nothing?"
  A strong answer names a specific moment that was hard to take, and then
  names their own default honestly: the accent, the phone call, the sudden
  injury. That is A1.1 in conversation — knowing where your ideas come from,
  and noticing when the supply is one idea deep.
  A3.2 is on this task as well, but voice and movement are watched, not
  discussed; do not try to get at them here.
  Record: one line per student, their default in their own words. Reread
  the list before you mark the showcase — a student who named it and then
  did not use it has shown you something.

The product evidence is the showcase on Day 17, performed cold.
