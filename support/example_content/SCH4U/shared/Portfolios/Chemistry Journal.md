An examination mark tells you where you stood on one morning. Your
journal shows what changed across a semester, and you are the only
person who can build it.

## What it is for

This course asks you to predict from a model, measure, and then say
where the model held and where it did not. Most of the interesting work
happens **before** you touch any equipment — choosing the model,
committing to a prediction, deciding what would count as the model
failing — and most of the rest happens in the twenty minutes afterwards,
when you are working out what your numbers are allowed to mean.

None of that survives in a data table. It survives here or nowhere.

## What goes in

| Goes in | Does not go in |
| --- | --- |
| Predictions, written before you measured, with the model they came from | Marks and grades |
| The raw readings, with their units and the instrument that gave them | Only the final calculated answer |
| The temperature the system was actually at | Copies of the concept pages |
| Which data-booklet value you used, and the conditions it assumes | A summary of what we covered |
| Reasoning you later found out was wrong | Everything you handed in |
| A number that surprised you, and what you did next | Anything you wrote to impress me |
| Questions you still cannot answer | |

The left column is what a reader learns something from. The right column
already exists somewhere else.

## Record the model, not just the prediction

This is the entry habit that separates a Grade 12 journal from a Grade
11 one, and it costs one extra sentence.

Every prediction you make this year comes out of a model: collision
theory, VSEPR, Le Châtelier's principle, the Brønsted-Lowry picture, a
table of standard reduction potentials. Write down which one, and write
down the assumption it is making about your set-up. Then, when the
result disagrees, you have something to interrogate. Without it you have
only "it did not work", which is not a finding about anything.

> Prediction: doubling the concentration of the thiosulfate will roughly
> halve the time to the endpoint. **Model:** collision theory — more
> particles per unit volume means more collisions per second, so a
> proportionally faster rate. **What the model assumes here:**
> temperature constant across all trials, and one reaction rather than
> two competing ones.

That last clause is the part worth training yourself to write. It tells
you in advance what evidence would count against you, which is the only
thing that makes a prediction a prediction rather than a hope.

## Raw readings, not just answers

Write down the numbers the instruments actually gave you, before any
arithmetic.

If your entry says only *"$K_c = 48$"*, and you later discover you used
the wrong initial concentration, the entry is dead — there is nothing
left to recompute from. If it says *"absorbance 0.412 on the colorimeter
at 470 nm; initial $[\ce{Fe^3+}] = 0.0020$ mol/L; bath at 22.0 °C"*,
you can fix the error in one line, three weeks later, and the record is
still honest about what you did at the time.

Two quantities are worth recording every single time this year, even
when they seem beside the point:

- **Temperature.** Every equilibrium constant, every $K_a$, every
  standard potential in the booklet is quoted at a temperature. An entry
  that does not say what yours was cannot be compared with any of them.
- **Where a looked-up value came from**, and under what conditions it
  applies, the way [[Reading a Data Table]] describes. A number with no
  provenance cannot be checked, which means it cannot be defended.

## The habit that makes it work

Write the entry the day of the work. The details you will want at the
end of the course — what you tried first, which reading you doubted at
the time, the moment your group changed the plan — are gone within a
week, and no amount of effort in the final month brings them back. Ten
minutes on the day beats an hour in the last week of the semester.

## Rhythm

- After every investigation, one entry.
- After every discussion, two or three sentences on what moved.
- Whenever a model failed you, an entry saying where its boundary is.
- At the end of each unit, the milestone entry — written in class, in
  the time set aside for it on that day's agenda, and the part of this
  folder that is marked.
- Once mid-semester, a [[Showing Growth]] comparison, also in class.
- At the end, [[Final Reflection]], written in the last twenty minutes
  of the showcase.

> [!important] Predictions get written down before the measurement
> Not after, and not "roughly what I was expecting". A prediction
> recorded afterwards is not a prediction, and your memory of what you
> basically expected is generous to you in a way you will not notice.
>
> Grade 11 asked you to make the prediction a number with a direction.
> Grade 12 asks for one more thing on top: **name what would have to
> happen for the prediction to be wrong in an interesting way rather
> than a boring one.** A boring wrong is a technique problem. An
> interesting wrong is the model's edge, and you cannot recognise it
> after the fact if you did not write down where you thought it was.
