> [!abstract] At a glance
> **Four write-ups**, one per unit, September to January · each written **in
> class**, in the period after the bench work · **individually**, from data
> your bench collected together · **Format:** the seven sections on
> [[Writing a Lab Report]]

Eight investigations run this year. Four of them are written up properly,
and those four write-ups are marked. They are the part of your mark that
arrives steadily rather than in four large pieces at the end of each unit —
which is what lets a report card describe your most recent and most
consistent work instead of four busy weeks.

The other four investigations — [[Looking at Tissue]],
[[Balancing by Counting]], [[Reaction Types Lab]], and
[[The Law of Reflection]] — are not written up and carry no mark. They exist
to get an idea into your hands before it has a name, and what you owe them
is a journal entry, not a report.

## What each report has to settle

The shape is the same every time; the question underneath it is not. Each
report answers its own investigation's question from your own data, and
these are the sentences I look for first.

| Report | What its analysis has to settle |
| --- | --- |
| One | Whether your membrane was selective by size — and, from the simulated dissection, one hand-off where two systems each depend on the other, with what fails in the organism if the exchange stops |
| Two | Which kinds of evidence actually distinguish a new substance from the same substances rearranged, ranked, with your reason for the top of the list and your reason for the bottom |
| Three | What your two containers showed, what water, sand, and soil showed about heat moving into different surfaces, and then a closing paragraph that uses your model to explain the human-caused greenhouse effect — including the point where the model stops standing in for it |
| Four | How the bending grows with the angle of incidence in each material, and by how much the two materials differ; the speed of light in each from the index you measured; why a ray bends toward the normal on entering the slower one; and whether your predicted image distance survived being measured |

## Written in class, and why

Each of these is written here, in the period after the bench work, with your
data in front of you and me in the room. That is deliberate twice over.

A report written at home three days later is a report about what you can
still remember, and the part actually worth marking — what you made of the
numbers while they were still strange to you — has gone by then. And writing
it here means help arrives at the moment you are stuck, which is never the
moment you are stuck at a kitchen table. Ask.

## Individually, from data you shared

The bench work is done in pairs or threes, and the raw readings belong to
the bench. **From the analysis section onward, the report is yours alone.**
Two people may hand in the same table of temperatures. Two people handing in
the same analysis have handed in one piece of work between them.

## Success criteria

Eight rows, the same eight every time, so that by January you can run them
from memory. Use them with [[Judging Your Own Work]] before you hand a
report in.

Several of the rows bend, and the table says where. The first bends because
each investigation asks a different question. Three more bend because
reports one and two produce no graph and no arithmetic at all — a column of
colour tests is not a data set you can plot, and pretending otherwise would
cost you a row for following the investigation exactly as it is written. The
last bends because only one of the four names a disposal step. The list
itself never changes, which is what makes improvement across four reports
visible.

| Quality | What it looks like in your work |
| --- | --- |
| The question this investigation was asking, settled from your own data | The sentence in the third column of the table above, answered — not gestured at. Level 3 answers it. Level 4 answers it and says how far the evidence reaches: which part of the claim your data carries on its own, and which part is standing on what the class did or on what the concept page told you |
| A question and a prediction with a mechanism | The question in one sentence, and a prediction with a **because** in it — the reason you expect it, not the outcome you expect |
| Variables named, and a procedure that could be repeated | Independent, dependent, and controlled all three named, the ones you could not control admitted, and steps another bench could follow without asking you anything |
| Observations recorded straight, and plotted where there is something to plot | Units on every column, the reading that did not fit written down rather than quietly dropped, and no interpretation anywhere in this section. Reports three and four end in a graph with labelled, scaled axes; reports one and two end in a table, and no graph is expected of them |
| Numbers expressed to the precision you actually have | Reports three and four only. The calculation shown once in full, and the final answer rounded to what your instrument can support, with one line saying why you rounded there. Reports one and two produce no calculation and this row does not count against them |
| Evidence weighed against your own prediction | The result set beside what you predicted, said either way — supported, refuted, or unable to decide. Where you have numbers, the uncertainty in a single reading estimated and the difference compared against it; where you have observations, the pattern across your rows named, and the row that would not fit it |
| A conclusion the data allows, and its limits | One paragraph answering the question, justified from your own numbers or observations, and the step that could have misled you named with **which way** it pushed the result |
| Safe practice, planned and then done | The only row not in the report, and the only one I mark from the period rather than the page. Every time: hazards read off the container or the apparatus rather than guessed, and the part **you** designed brought to me before you ran it — the blanket rule on [[Investigations/index\|the Investigations page]]. Where the investigation page names a disposal or make-safe step, done the way it is written; report two is the one that names several, and reports one, three and four are not judged on a step their page does not have |

The seventh row is the one that separates a report from a recipe. "There may
have been experimental error" is a sentence, not a finding. Naming the step
that could have gone wrong, and saying roughly how far it could have moved
your result, is a finding.

## The eighth row, and why it carries a mark

Working safely is not a favour you do me, and it is not the same thing as
behaving well. It is [[A1.5|part of what this course is required to teach]] —
the curriculum asks you to use equipment and materials **safely, accurately,
and effectively** while you collect your data — so it is marked like
anything else in the curriculum.

That wording is the whole of the boundary, and it is worth being exact about
which side of it you are on.

- **Marked**, because it is knowledge applied: the pictograms on the bottle
  read before you open it, the waste in the labelled container rather than
  the sink, the follow-up test you designed checked before you ran it.
- **Not marked**, because it is conduct rather than knowledge: everything
  else in [[Safety in the Lab]] — tidying your station, arriving on time,
  following an instruction because it was an instruction. Those matter, and
  they are reported with your learning skills, not inside your percentage.

The test is simple. If getting it wrong would show you did not know a
hazard, it is in this row. If it would only show you did not feel like it,
it is a learning skill. See [[How Marks Work]].

Format: [[Writing a Lab Report]]. Drawings and graphs:
[[Drawing Scientific Diagrams]] and [[Reading a Graph Honestly]]. Labels,
symbols, and disposal: [[Lab Safety and WHMIS]].

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

These four are the steadiest evidence in the course, and they carry one row
that cannot be read at all, only watched. So this task gets two observation
prompts rather than one.

OBSERVE (the design) — Unit 3, Day 1, while they set the two containers up
  Watch for: how many things differ between container one and container two
  when the lamp goes on, and whether anybody has looked at the clock and
  worked out when the lamp has to go off to leave room for the cooling half.
  The report will present the design as though it was settled in advance,
  because by then it was; only the period shows you whether it was settled
  before the lamp warmed up or reconstructed afterwards from what happened.
  Going well: the pair written down first, then physically checked against
  each other — same lamp distance, same lid seating, thermometer at the same
  depth — before anything is switched on.
  Stuck: the lamp on before the second container is filled; or the whole
  period spent warming, with the lamp still on at the bell and no cooling
  data at all.
  Record: one tick per bench on your seating plan, three columns — matched
  pair, one thing adrift, or switched on before the check. One circuit of the
  room while they set up. That tick is most of the second criteria row for
  report three, which is why it is worth the circuit.

OBSERVE (the eighth row) — Unit 2, Day 1, the six stations
  The one day of the year when the whole of it is in front of you at once:
  copper sulfate solution, dilute acetic acid, a warm water bath, six waste
  containers, and a follow-up test each pair designed themselves. None of
  the other three write-ups puts this many different hazards in front of
  them at once.
  Watch for: what happens to what is left over. A report cannot show you
  where the dregs went, and the bench looks identical afterwards either way.
  Going well: the station card read to the end, waste in the labelled
  container, nothing tipped down the sink, and nothing returned to a stock
  bottle.
  Stuck: a rinse into the sink because the container looked full; or a
  splash of the blue solution wiped up with a bare hand.
  Record: a tick per station on your day plan as you pass it, not per
  student — the station is what tells you, and there are six of them.

TALK — Unit 1, Day 8, while they write report one up
  You are in the room for the whole period and the hand-off table is on the
  desk in front of them, so this costs you the walk you were making anyway.
  Do not ask about the control or about what the tubing fails to model: the
  investigation page asks for both and the agenda spends an item comparing
  limitations, so those are spent.
  Do not ask what fails in the organism if the exchange stops either — that
  is the third column of the table on this page, so they are writing it as
  you walk past.
  Ask: "Of the two systems in your hand-off row, which one would notice
  first if the other slowed down — and what would somebody outside the body
  actually see?"
  Then: "Your dissection followed one nutrient molecule. Pick a different
  passenger — water, or oxygen — and tell me which systems it goes through
  instead, and where it joins yours."
  A strong first answer names the direction of the dependency and a sign
  somebody could actually notice, rather than restating the definition. A
  strong second answer routes the new molecule without being led, which is
  B3.5 again and harder than the first: the interaction between systems held
  as a general fact, rather than the one path they happened to record.
  Record: three columns on the seating plan — direction named, structures
  only, or neither. Carry the third column to Unit 1, Day 15, where they
  answer questions on their case study out loud, and see who has moved.

The product evidence is the four reports themselves, each handed in at the
end of the period it was written in.
