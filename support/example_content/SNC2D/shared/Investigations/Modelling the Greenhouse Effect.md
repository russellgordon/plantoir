A planet with no atmosphere would sit at a temperature you could work
out from the sunlight falling on it and the fraction it reflects. This
one is warmer than that. Today you build something on a bench that
might show you why — and then you spend an equal amount of effort
working out how far your model is allowed to be trusted.

## What you are trying to find out

Two things, in two halves of the period.

1. **Does adding an atmosphere-like layer change how a surface warms
   under a light source?** You design the comparison and you decide
   what "atmosphere-like" is going to mean.
2. **Do all surfaces warm and cool the same way?** Water, dry sand, and
   dark soil under the same lamp, warming and then cooling. Whatever
   difference you find is the reason a lake and a parking lot behave
   differently on the same afternoon.

## What you have to work with

- Two identical containers, and lids or clear plastic film
- A lamp on a stand, with the bulb specified on the station card
- Thermometers or temperature probes — **alcohol or digital, never
  mercury**
- Sand, dark soil, water, measuring cylinder, balance
- Timer, ruler, graph paper or plotting software
- Baking soda and vinegar in a small dish, if your group wants to add
  carbon dioxide to one container

This is a **design and build** task, so almost nothing above is a
procedure. Decide and write down, with reasons:

- **What your two containers differ by.** Exactly one thing. If one is
  covered and also closer to the lamp, your investigation is finished
  before it started.
- **What you hold constant.** Distance from the lamp, container size
  and colour, thermometer position and depth, starting temperature,
  mass or volume of material, room draughts, and how long the lamp has
  been on before you start — the bulb's own output changes as it warms.
- **How long, and how often.** A single reading at the end throws away
  the shape of the curve, and the shape is where the interesting part
  is.
- **The cooling half.** Turning the lamp off and continuing to read is
  half the evidence for the second question, and groups routinely
  forget to leave time for it.

## Safety

> [!danger] Lamps, water, and glass, on the same bench
> - **The bulb and its shade get hot enough to burn you** and stay hot
>   long after the switch goes off. Move the lamp by its base or stand,
>   never by the head, and never touch the bulb — not even to check.
> - Keep **plastic film, paper, and anything else that melts or burns
>   clear of the bulb.** Film goes over the container, not near the
>   lamp, and there is a minimum distance on the station card. Follow
>   it.
> - **Water and mains electricity share this bench, so keep them
>   apart.** Fill containers at the sink, carry them on a tray, keep
>   the lamp cord off the bench top and out of every puddle, and dry a
>   spill immediately before you switch anything on.
> - Use alcohol or digital thermometers only. If a glass thermometer
>   breaks, stop, keep everyone back, and tell me — you do not clean it
>   up.
> - Do not stand a thermometer in a container and then let go of it; a
>   toppling thermometer in a full container is how the glass breaks.
> - If you make carbon dioxide with baking soda and vinegar, do it in
>   an **open dish**. Never seal that reaction in a rigid container.
>   Eye protection on while you do it.
> - Look at the lamp no more than you have to. It is bright, and a
>   bright filament is unpleasant to stare into for a period.

## The prediction you write first

Before the lamp goes on, in your journal:

1. Which container you expect to be warmer, by roughly how much, and
   **through what mechanism** — name the physical process you think is
   responsible, not just the outcome.
2. Which material you expect to warm fastest, and which you expect to
   **cool** fastest, with a reason.
3. One result that would tell you your proposed mechanism was wrong,
   even if the container you named still came out warmer.

That last one separates a prediction from a bet. It is very easy to be
right about which container is warmer for a reason that has nothing to
do with the reason you gave.

## What to collect

| Time (min) | Container 1 (°C) | Container 2 (°C) | Difference (°C) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | | | |
| | | | |

| Time (min) | Water (°C) | Dry sand (°C) | Dark soil (°C) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | | | |
| | | | |

Plot both. Temperature on the vertical axis, time on the horizontal,
all series on the same pair of axes so the comparison is visible rather
than described. Mark clearly where the lamp went off. Then read the
**slopes**, not just the endpoints — how fast something warms and how
fast it cools are two different findings, and a material can lose on
one and win on the other.

Conventions for the graph, and the traps that come with axis choices:
[[Reading a Graph Honestly]].

## What to bring to the consolidation discussion

- Both graphs, with the lamp-off point marked.
- One sentence of the form: "The evidence supports / does not support
  the prediction that …, because …". A1.8 is asking for exactly that
  sentence, and it is worth writing before you arrive.
- Your estimate of the uncertainty in a single temperature reading, and
  whether your difference between containers is bigger than it.
- The answer to this, in writing: **what would you have to add to your
  model to make it a model of the atmosphere rather than a model of a
  box?**

## What you should not claim

This is the important section on this page, and the reason it is here
is that the model is genuinely a bit of a cheat.

| What your model actually showed | What the atmosphere actually does |
| --- | --- |
| A covered container warmed more than an open one | The air is not in a box, and nothing stops it circulating |
| Most of that difference comes from stopping warm air escaping upward — suppressed convection | The greenhouse effect is **radiative**: certain gases absorb outgoing infrared radiation and re-emit it in all directions, including downward |
| A lid is a physical barrier | Carbon dioxide is not a barrier, and adding it does not stop air moving |
| Minutes, one lamp, one box | Decades, a whole planet, oceans, ice, clouds, and winds |

So: a warmer covered container is **an illustration of the idea, not a
demonstration of the mechanism**. The honest sentence is "our model
warmed more when covered, but our model warms mainly by trapping air
rather than by trapping radiation, so it cannot by itself establish
that carbon dioxide warms the planet."

Also worth refusing to claim:

- **A difference smaller than your thermometer can resolve is not a
  difference.** Read the resolution off the instrument and compare.
- **One run is one run.** A draught from the door falls on one
  container and not the other, and you will never know.
- **Water warming slowly does not by itself explain a coastline.** It
  is a strong hint about heat capacity, and the real comparison
  involves depth, mixing, and evaporation that your beaker does not
  have.
