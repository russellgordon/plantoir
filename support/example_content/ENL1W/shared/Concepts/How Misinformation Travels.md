Most false things you will meet were not invented to deceive you. They
were shared by somebody who believed them, because the thing that makes
information travel is not accuracy — it is feeling.

## Three words worth keeping apart

| Word | Means | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Misinformation** | Wrong, shared without intent to deceive | A friend passes on a rumour they believed |
| **Disinformation** | Wrong, and spread on purpose | A coordinated campaign built to mislead |
| **Malinformation** | True, but released to harm | Private information leaked to damage somebody |

Most of what you will see is the first. That matters, because the person
who shared it is usually not an enemy — they are a person who got caught
by the same design you are learning to see.

## Why the false thing moves faster

- **It is built to be shared.** Outrage, fear, and delight all travel
  further than "it is complicated".
- **Feeds reward engagement, not accuracy.** A post that makes people
  argue is shown to more people, by design.
- **Corrections travel worse than claims.** The follow-up is boring, and
  it arrives after everyone has moved on.
- **Repetition feels like evidence.** Seeing something four times makes
  it feel true. That effect works on everybody, including on people who
  know about it.
- **It flatters us.** The story that confirms what we already believe
  gets the least scrutiny from us, which is exactly backwards.

## What actually helps

1. **Pause before sharing.** Two seconds is enough to break the reflex.
2. **Check laterally**: leave the post and search for the source
   elsewhere, per [[Finding and Judging Sources]].
3. **Look for the original.** A screenshot of a headline is not a
   headline.
4. **Check the date**, always.
5. **Ask what would change your mind.** If nothing would, you are not
   evaluating, you are defending.

## Images, video, and generated content

Photographs get reused from other years and other countries. Video gets
clipped so the sentence before is gone. And generated images and voices
are now good enough to fool a careful person on a small screen.

The defence is not "look for glitches" — that stops working every few
months. It is provenance: where did this come from, who published it
first, and does a source with something to lose stand behind it?

> [!warning] The uncomfortable part
> Everyone believes they are the exception. Studies on this are
> consistent and unflattering: confidence in your ability to spot fakes
> correlates with being fooled more often, not less, because confidence
> skips the check. The habit is what protects you, not the instinct.
