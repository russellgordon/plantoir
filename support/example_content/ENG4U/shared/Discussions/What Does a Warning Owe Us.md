## The question

*The Handmaid's Tale* was written as a warning. What does a warning owe
its reader — accuracy, plausibility, hope, or nothing at all?

## Positions to consider

| Position | The argument | The objection |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plausibility | A warning that cannot happen is entertainment | Atwood's claim that everything in it has precedent is itself contestable |
| Hope | A text that offers no way out produces despair, not action | Consolation can be its own kind of lie |
| Accuracy about mechanism | The value is in showing *how* it would happen — the frozen accounts, the sudden law | Mechanism without politics can be read as inevitability |
| Nothing | A novel is not a policy document | Then why did Atwood keep saying what she said about it? |

## The Historical Notes complication

The novel ends with a scholar treating Offred's testimony as material and
warning against judging Gilead too harshly. Ask what that ending does to
each position above — particularly the one about hope.
