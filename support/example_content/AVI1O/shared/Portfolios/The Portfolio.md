The portfolio is the container for your year. Not a folder of your best
work — that is a different thing, and you will build one for
[[The Exhibition]] — but the whole record: plans, experiments, preliminary
versions, the finished pieces, the photographs of anything too large or too
three-dimensional to keep, and the sketchbook that ran alongside all of it.

## What lives in it

Physically, a portfolio is a large flat folder that protects work from
being creased, plus a digital folder for photographs and files. Both need
to exist, because a print bends and a hard drive fails, and neither of them
warns you first.

Keep preliminary work with the final piece it led to. A finished painting
on its own says what you can do; the same painting with three thumbnails, a
colour test, and a first version that failed says how you got there — and
how you got there is what this course is actually teaching.

> [!info] What the final evaluation looks for
> The portfolio is submitted complete at the end of the course, and the
> reflection written with it asks you to point at evidence: where the
> exploration stage changed the original idea, where feedback altered a
> work, where you would do it differently now. You cannot point at what
> you threw away early on. [[The Portfolio and Reflection]] has the
> full requirements.

## Naming things so you can find them

Digital files need a system by about week three or they become a hundred
items called `IMG_4471`. Use the same pattern every time — your name, the
title of the work, and the date — and avoid punctuation the computer treats
as special. Keep the largest version of every photograph;
[[Photographing Your Work]] explains why the small copy is not enough.

## What it is for after this course

A portfolio is the only honest answer to the question *what can you do?*
A mark compresses a year into a number, and the portfolio is the version
with the reasons still attached. Programs that lead anywhere in the visual
arts ask to see one, and so do a surprising number of employers who have
nothing to do with art — a folder that shows a problem being worked at over
time is evidence about how you think.

That is not only about pathways, and [[Where Visual Arts Leads]] takes that
part further. It is that you now have a record of getting better at
something difficult, which is worth keeping for its own sake —
[[What You Take With You]] is the argument in full.
