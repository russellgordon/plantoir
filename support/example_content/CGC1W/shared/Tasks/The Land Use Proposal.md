**Due: before the culminating inquiry begins.** Groups of three — with
one section written and marked on your own.

One real, empty or under-used site in your community, and a defensible
proposal for what should happen to it.

## The site

A parking lot, a vacant lot, a closed building, a piece of land at the
edge of town, a stretch of waterfront. It must be real, you must be able
to stand on the sidewalk beside it, and you must map it.

## What you produce

| Part | What it must contain |
| --- | --- |
| The site as it is | A land-use map of the site and what surrounds it, from your own survey |
| The constraints | Physical, legal, and practical — what the ground, the zoning, and the services allow |
| The proposal | What you would build or keep, and at what density |
| The case | Who benefits, who loses, what it costs, and what happens if nothing is done |
| The alternative | The strongest proposal that is not yours, and why yours is better |
| Who wrote what | The constraints, the case and the alternative are one each: agreed on launch day, written alone in the working periods, and signed |

## How it is marked

- **Grounded** — your survey, not a satellite image and an opinion.
- **Constrained** — a proposal that ignores flooding, servicing, or
  zoning is a wish.
- **Argued** — the case for it survives the alternative you wrote.
- **Fair** — the people who would lose something are named, not skipped.
- **Your own section** — the constraints, the case, or the alternative,
  whichever you took. Decide who takes which on launch day, before
  anybody knows which will turn out to be the easy one. Nothing here is
  a common group mark: the survey and the site map are built together,
  and every row above is judged for each of you from your own section,
  from what I watched you do, and from what you can account for at the
  conference. Three people on one proposal can finish with three
  different marks.

> [!warning] Density is the argument, not a detail
> Almost every land-use fight in Canada is about how many people should
> live on a piece of ground and who gets to decide. Take a position on it
> and defend it — see [[Sprawl and Its Alternatives]].

Your survey comes from [[The Land Use Walk]], the constraints from
[[Building Where Nature Pushes Back]], and the liveability standard you
argue against is in [[Sustainable Communities]].

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

Groups of three, one section each, so record by student and not by
group.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 6, the constraints clinic
  Watch the sequence, not the content. Does the group find out what the
  ground and the rules allow before deciding what to put on the site,
  or after? The finished proposal presents constraints first whichever
  way round it was actually done, because that is the order the page
  asks for.
  Going well: nobody has said what should be built yet, and somebody is
  reading a zoning schedule or a floodplain mapping layer.
  Stuck: a sketch of a building already exists and the group is hunting
  for constraints that permit it.
  Record: group name, one column, and who was reading what — you will
  want the individual detail later.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 7, at the conferences on the alternative already on
that agenda
  Do not ask what their alternative is; the page asks for it and they
  will read it to you.
  Ask: "What would have to be true about this site for the alternative
  to be the better answer?"
  A strong answer names a condition you could go and check that would
  flip the density argument — how many households already sit within
  four hundred metres, whether the servicing has capacity, what the
  transit headway is — rather than "if people wanted it". That is E1.4:
  one kind of urban growth assessed against another, on this ground,
  rather than in the abstract.
  Then: "Your site map draws a line around the site. What is on the
  other side of that line that your proposal depends on?"
  A strong answer names a neighbouring use or a service the proposal
  cannot exist without — the sewer, the arterial, the school
  catchment, the loading bay next door. That is E1.1, spatial
  distribution affecting a community, and a proposal can be internally
  perfect and stop at its own boundary.
  Record: one line per STUDENT, with the section they own beside it.

The product evidence is the proposal presented and handed in on Day 14.
