Rights get the attention. Responsibilities are the half of citizenship
that nobody campaigns on, partly because the list is short and unglamorous
and partly because most of it is not enforced by anyone.

It helps to separate two kinds. Some responsibilities are legal
obligations — a court can make you comply, and there is a penalty if you
do not. Others are civic expectations: real, widely held, and carrying no
penalty at all beyond what your neighbours think.

## The legal ones, in Canada

- **Obey the law**, including laws you campaigned against and laws you
  think are wrong. Changing them is a separate activity, described in
  [[How Change Actually Happens]].
- **Pay taxes** owed under federal and provincial law. What they buy is
  the subject of [[Budgets as Statements of Priority]].
- **Serve on a jury** when summoned, if you are qualified. In Ontario
  this is a legal duty, not an invitation.
- **Attend school** to the age the *Education Act* requires.
- **Answer the census** when it comes, which is why census data can be
  used as evidence at all.

Note what is not on that list. Voting is a right in Canada, not a legal
duty — some democracies make it compulsory and this one does not. Military
service is not compulsory. Speaking either official language is not a
condition of citizenship for people born here.

## The civic ones, which are the interesting half

Staying informed enough to vote on something other than a slogan.
Serving on juries willingly rather than avoiding the summons. Speaking up
when a decision is being made badly. Respecting the rights of people you
disagree with, which costs nothing when you agree and everything when you
do not. Contributing something — time, work, attention — to a community
that has no way to require it of you. Those are covered further in
[[Service and Contribution]].

## Responsibilities that do not stop at the border

Canada has signed international conventions that bind it as a country:
the Universal Declaration of Human Rights was adopted by the United
Nations General Assembly in 1948 with Canada voting in favour, and Canada
is party to treaties on refugees, on the rights of the child, on torture,
and on genocide. Ratifying a convention creates obligations at
international law, but most of them do not become enforceable in a
Canadian court until Parliament passes legislation implementing them.
That gap between signing and enforcing is where a great deal of argument
about Canada's record lives.

Where human rights are violated abroad, the responses available are
limited and their record is mixed: reporting by international bodies,
sanctions, refugee resettlement, prosecutions at international courts,
and diplomatic pressure. Assessing whether a response worked means asking
what changed for the people affected, not how strongly it was worded. Any
answer you write should be able to name a response that failed.
