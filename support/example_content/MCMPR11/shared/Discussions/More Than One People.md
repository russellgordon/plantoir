Most of the historical and cultural material on this site so far has
centred Indigenous experience — FirstVoices, OCAP®, the Guardian
Watchmen. That is not the whole picture of whose history in BC has been
erased, contested, or locked away in a filing cabinet somewhere. Other
communities have fought the same fight, with the same question
underneath it: whose history gets digitized, by whom, and who controls
access to the record afterward?

### Landscapes of Injustice

Between 2014 and 2021, a University of Victoria-led research project
called Landscapes of Injustice reconstructed what happened to the
property of Japanese Canadians who were forcibly interned during and
after the Second World War — homes, farms, and businesses seized and
sold, mostly without their owners' consent. Led by historian Jordan
Stanger-Ross and a large team of partner organizations, the project built
a searchable digital archive of roughly 32,000 documents drawn from more
than twenty archives across Canada, the US, and the UK, launched in March
2021. Records that had sat scattered and effectively inaccessible for
seventy years became something a descendant, or a student, could
actually search.

### The Chinese Canadian Museum

The Chinese Canadian Museum opened in Vancouver's Chinatown, in the
historic Wing Sang Building, on July 1, 2023 — one hundred years to the
day after the 1923 Chinese Immigration Act took effect, a law that
halted almost all Chinese immigration to Canada until it was repealed in
1947. Its inaugural exhibit, *The Paper Trail to the 1923 Chinese
Exclusion Act*, displayed more than 400 crowdsourced Chinese Immigration
certificates, including "head tax" certificates families had kept for a
century — the largest public display of these documents ever assembled.
That crowdsourcing effort, run in partnership with the University of
British Columbia, is building a public digital archive from documents
that had previously existed only as single paper originals in family
homes. Separately, Library and Archives Canada maintains its own official
digitized collection of Chinese immigration registers and certificates
from the same period — two related, but organizationally distinct,
digitization efforts of the same history.

### The Komagata Maru

In May 1914, the ship *Komagata Maru* arrived in Vancouver's Burrard
Inlet carrying 376 passengers from Punjab, almost all Sikh, who were
British subjects with a legal right to enter Canada. Canada used a
deliberately race-targeted rule — the "continuous journey" regulation,
requiring immigrants to travel directly from their country of origin
without any stop, a route that was effectively impossible from India —
to keep all but 24 of them out. The ship was forced to leave after a
two-month standoff. A monument now stands at Harbour Green Park in
Vancouver, and the federal government has formally apologized twice:
Prime Minister Stephen Harper in Surrey in 2008, and Prime Minister
Justin Trudeau in the House of Commons in 2016. What is harder to point
to is a single, comprehensive public digital archive of the incident's
own records — a reminder that a monument and an apology are not the same
thing as a documented, searchable record.

### Discussion Prompts

1. **Who paid for it, who built it, who benefits.** Landscapes of
   Injustice was university-led, the Chinese Canadian Museum's project
   was community-and-university crowdsourced, and Komagata Maru's
   memorialization so far is mostly a monument and an apology, not an
   archive. Does it matter who does the digitizing? What is different
   about a project a community starts itself, versus one a university or
   government undertakes on a community's behalf?
2. **What a monument doesn't do.** A monument tells you an event
   happened. It doesn't let you search a document, look up a name, or
   verify a claim the way a digital archive does. Why might a community
   push for a searchable archive even after receiving a formal apology?
3. **The missing archive.** Of the three cases here, Komagata Maru is
   the one without a comprehensive public digital archive attached to
   it. If you were pitching a project to build one, what would you need
   from the descendant community before writing any code — and who
   should end up controlling it once it exists?

### How we run this

Run this one as a gallery walk.

- Set up three stations around the room, one per case above (Landscapes
  of Injustice, the Chinese Canadian Museum, the Komagata Maru), each
  with a short summary printed out and one of the Discussion Prompts
  pinned above it.
- Groups of three or four rotate through all three stations, about eight
  minutes each. At every station, read the summary, discuss the prompt,
  and leave a written response on a sticky note or directly on the paper
  before moving on.
- Don't repeat what a previous group already wrote — build on it,
  disagree with it, or ask it a question instead.
- After all three rotations, walk the room together as a class and read
  what accumulated at each station out loud.
- Close with prompt 3, as a whole-class conversation: what would a
  fourth case — one this page didn't cover — need to look like?
