Heat a metal, drop it into water, and let the two settle. The energy the
metal loses is the energy the water gains, and from that you can find the
metal's specific heat capacity — a number you can look up afterwards to
see how you did.

## Safety

> [!danger] Hot metal looks exactly like cold metal
> Tongs, always. Goggles on from the moment the kettle goes on. Dry the
> metal before it enters the water or you will splash yourself.

## Method

1. Mass the dry metal sample. Heat it in boiling water for at least five
   minutes — long enough to be at $100^\circ\text{C}$ throughout.
2. Mass a calorimeter of cool water, and record its starting temperature.
3. Transfer the metal quickly, stir gently, and record the highest
   temperature the water reaches.

## The physics

The water gains what the metal loses:

$$m_w c_w \Delta T_w = m_m c_m \Delta T_m$$

Everything except $c_m$ is measured, so rearrange and solve. Watch the
sign convention: both $\Delta T$ values are taken as positive sizes here,
because the equation already says one gained and one lost.

## Analysis

1. Compare with the accepted value for your metal. Percent difference?
2. Your value will probably be LOW. Why does heat lost to the room and to
   the calorimeter itself push it that way rather than the other?
3. Water's specific heat is about ten times most metals'. What does that
   mean for a cooling system, and for a coastline?
