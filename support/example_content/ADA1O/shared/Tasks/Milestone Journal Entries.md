## What you are making

Four pieces of writing, spread across the semester, in which you look back
at what you have just made and say honestly what it taught you. They live
in your [[Drama Journal]] with everything else — same book, same pen — but
these four are the ones I mark.

Everything else in the journal is practice: the entry after a workshop day,
the letter [[Writing in Role]], the response to something you watched.
Practice is not marked, and that is what makes it usable — you can write
that a rehearsal fell apart and it costs you nothing. But a milestone entry
is built out of that practice. It asks you to quote your earlier self, and
there is nothing to quote if the earliest entries say nothing.

## The four days

| When | Written after | What it looks back at |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unit 1, Day 16 | The tableau performance | Frozen pictures, and your first two weeks in an ensemble |
| Unit 2, Day 18 | The improvisation showcase | Offers, status, and playing without a plan |
| Unit 3, Day 22 | The scene study and the teaching presentations | Character, text, and forms that are older than this school |
| Unit 4, Days 21–23 | The culminating performance | The whole course — this one is [[Final Reflection]], and it is longer |

All four are written **in class, in the period**, with
[[Journal Checklist]] open beside you. That is deliberate and it is not
about trust: work that carries a mark is done here, where I am in the room,
where you can ask me something, and where nobody is judged on how much time
they had at home.

## How to work

1. Read backwards before you write forwards. Open the journal at the start
   of the unit and read to today in one go. Expect to be surprised.
2. Flag two entries as you read: one you had forgotten, and one that
   embarrasses you slightly. The second is usually where the growth is.
3. Make a claim about yourself, then prove it the way [[Showing Growth]]
   describes — date it, quote your own words, then show the now.
4. Name your own part. Not "we got better at transitions" — what *you*
   suggested, tried, avoided, or changed, and what your group needed from
   you that you had to learn to give.
5. Write in the vocabulary of the course. [[Writing About Drama]] is the
   standard: describe, then interpret, then judge.

## Success criteria

The same five rows, all four times. You will not be given a new table in the
end of the course — you will be given the same one, and by then you will
know it.

| Quality | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Evidence, not claims | Every claim about yourself is paired with a dated entry, quoted in your own words from the time |
| Your own part named | What you suggested, tried, avoided, or changed — not what "we" did |
| Working with others | You name one thing this unit taught you about collaborating, negotiating, or listening, and the moment it happened |
| What travels | One skill or characteristic you have strengthened that would be useful outside this room, and where you would use it |
| Said in drama | The vocabulary is used correctly and where it belongs — tableau, status, focus, subtext — not sprinkled for decoration |

> [!success]- If you cannot find anything to say (click to expand)
> That usually means you are looking for a big change. Look for a small
> one instead: a thing you now notice that was invisible at the start of the course —
> where the [[Focus and Emphasis|focus]] of a scene sits, who has the
> [[Status|status]] in a corridor conversation. New noticing is growth,
> and your earlier entries prove it was not always there.

## Why this is worth a quarter of the seventy

Because a third of this course is not making drama at all. Strand B —
reflecting, responding, and analysing — is one of the three, and almost
everything in it happens in writing rather than on stage: assessing your
own role in the work, explaining what the ensemble taught you, naming what
you will carry out of this room. A performance cannot show me most of that.
These four entries are where it lives, so they are weighted like a strand
rather than like a diary.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 3, Day 22, the last fifteen minutes, while they write
  Watch which journals are open at an early page. The entry itself
  will carry dated quotations, because the criteria table asks for them —
  what it cannot show you is whether those quotations were FOUND or
  remembered and rounded off. Unit 1, Day 16 is no good for this: that
  day's agenda tells them to read their Day 2 entry aloud, so everybody
  rereads and you learn nothing. Nothing on Day 22 tells them to.
  Going well: pages turning backwards before anything is written, and a
  finger holding a place.
  Stuck: a journal open only at today's blank page — or no journal on the
  desk at all and the entry being drafted on loose paper "to copy in
  later", which is the tell that there is nothing to copy it into.
  Record: a tick against each name on your class list, reread or cold.
  Two minutes walking the room, once, halfway through the writing.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 23, at the portfolio conferences already on that agenda
  Five minutes each, the journal open between you. This is the last
  conversation of the course, so it confirms rather than redirects — if
  you want a version that can still change something, the same two
  questions work at the Unit 2, Day 20 journal workshop.
  Ask: "Name somebody in this room who changed the way you work, and tell
  me what they actually did."
  Then: "Which of your own habits did this course cost you?"
  A strong first answer names a person and a behaviour you could stage —
  how somebody counted to three before moving, how somebody said the thing
  nobody else would say — which is B2.3 heard rather than read: what
  working in an ensemble taught them about listening and negotiating. The
  second gets B3.2 from the far side, as a thing given up rather than a
  quality acquired: interrupting, hiding at the back, needing to be the
  funny one. A quiet student who wrote thinly can answer both of these
  better than anyone in the room, which is exactly why they are worth
  asking.
  Record: two words per student — the person named and the habit. You will
  want both when you write report card comments, and you will not
  reconstruct them later.

The product evidence is the four entries themselves, handed in with the
journal at the end of each unit.
