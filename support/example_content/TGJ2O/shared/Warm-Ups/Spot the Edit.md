Two versions of the same thing go up side by side — a photo and its
edit, a quote and its trim — and the room answers two questions in
order. First: what changed? Second, and harder: does the change lie?
Some edits serve the truth; some manufacture a better story than the
one that happened. Telling them apart is a newsroom survival skill.

## How to run it

1. Show both versions. One minute of silent hunting — no calling out.
2. List every difference the room found before judging any of them.
3. For each change, vote: honest edit, or a lie in work clothes?
4. Close on the hardest call — the change the room split over.

> [!example]- One image, one quote (click to expand)
> **Image.** Version A: the final game, play sharp, bleachers half
> empty behind it. Version B: the same frame cropped to the players.
> Honest? Mostly — the crop serves the play. It lies the moment the
> story claims a packed house.
> **Quote.** Said: "I think we can win if everyone stays healthy."
> Printed: "We can win." Every word is real; the meaning is not. An
> ellipsis can lie with a straight face.

## One variation

Hand the scalpel over: students produce the pairs. Making an edit
that ALMOST lies teaches the boundary better than spotting one.

> [!tip] Judge the claim, not the tool
> Cropping, trimming, and brightening are not sins — every published
> photo and quote is edited. The test is what the edit makes a
> reader believe. That test returns, full size, in
> [[Misinformation and Verification]] and [[When Is a Photo True]].
