## What it is for

Most of this semester was worked in groups, at the boards, with time to
look things up and somebody beside you. This is three hours with nothing
to look up and nobody to ask. It is also the closest thing in this course
to the assessments Grade 12 and first-year mathematics will use
constantly, met here where I am the one marking it.

## What is on it

| Part | Roughly | What it asks you to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A. Functions and algebra | 30% | Function notation, domain and range, inverses, transformations; simplify polynomial, radical, and rational expressions and state restrictions; one quadratic set in a real situation and given in function notation; find where a line meets a curve, graphically and algebraically |
| B. Exponential functions | 20% | Rational exponents and exponent laws; graph and transform exponential functions and state the domain, range, intercept and asymptote of each; find an equation from a graph; solve growth and decay problems |
| C. Sequences, series, and finance | 25% | Recursive and explicit forms; one binomial expanded from Pascal's triangle; arithmetic and geometric sequences and series, including problems set in the world; simple and compound interest, annuities and loans |
| D. Trigonometry | 25% | Exact ratios and the reciprocal ratios; prove identities; the sine and cosine laws; sinusoidal graphs, equations from data, and problems |

Every part asks you to *show the route*. A bare answer earns almost
nothing; a clearly wrong number reached by correct reasoning earns most
of the marks.

## What to expect, precisely

- **Restrictions are marked.** Simplifying a rational expression without
  stating what $x$ cannot be is an incomplete answer, every time —
  [[Equivalent Algebraic Expressions]] is where that habit was built.
- **Radicals come apart at their square factors.** $\sqrt{24}$ is not
  finished until it is $2\sqrt{6}$, and the same move survives inside a
  product of binomials — [[Equivalent Algebraic Expressions]] has both.
- **One quadratic arrives as a situation**, handed to you in function
  notation: a profit, a height, an area. It asks for a maximum, and for
  the inputs that keep the output above a stated value.
  [[Quadratic Functions Revisited]] is where completing the square
  became a transformation story you already knew.
- **An exponential graph is not finished until its properties are
  stated.** Domain, range, intercept, the asymptote it never touches, and
  whether it climbs or falls — for the parent and for every transformed
  version of it. [[The Exponential Function]] and
  [[Transforming Exponential Functions]] between them list the lot.
- **Identity proofs** are marked on layout as much as on algebra: one
  side only, each line following from the one above.
  [[The Other Three Ratios]] has the method.
- **One question gives you data** — a table of measurements — and asks
  for a sinusoidal model, a prediction, and an honest sentence about how
  far ahead you would trust it. That is [[Catching a Wave]] under exam
  conditions.
- **Pascal's triangle is on it, and you rebuild it rather than recall
  it.** One binomial raised to a whole-number power, expanded from the
  row that counts it — the relationship [[Sequences as Functions]] sets
  out, and four rows of arithmetic is faster than remembering row six.
- **A line meets a curve twice, once, or never**, and you are asked for
  both routes: the picture and the algebra. The discriminant is how the
  algebra knows which case it is in — [[Where a Line Meets a Parabola]]
  has it.
- **Financial questions may need a TVM solver.** You will be asked to
  state what you entered as well as what came out, because the entries
  are where the thinking is. [[Interest as a Sequence]] covers the two
  conventions that cause most errors.
- **Exact values** where exact values exist. $\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ is an
  answer; 0.866 is a rounding of one.

## How to prepare

1. **Redo problems, do not reread them.** Five from each unit's practice
   set, cold, on paper, with only the formula sheet.
2. **Rebuild the connections.** Arithmetic sequence, linear function,
   simple interest are one idea; geometric, exponential, compound
   interest are another. Draw that map from memory — the gaps are your
   study list.
3. **Re-derive rather than memorise.** The period of
   $y = a\sin(k(x-d)) + c$ is $\tfrac{360^\circ}{|k|}$ because $k$
   compresses horizontally. Knowing why survives pressure; knowing the
   formula alone does not.
4. **Practise the proofs out loud.** If you cannot say the first move
   before you write it, you are hoping rather than working.
5. **Bring questions to the review classes.** This page is what is on
   the examination.

> [!tip] In the three hours
> Budget by the weightings and hold to them — twenty minutes over on
> Part A costs more than it gains. If a question stalls, write down what
> you know about it and what you intended to do; a described method
> earns marks and a blank space earns none.

## How this is assessed

Against the same expectations as everything else. Per [[How Marks Work]],
this examination is part of the final 30% of the course mark alongside
[[The Functions Symposium]], so that neither one afternoon nor one
project decides your grade alone.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

There is no watching and no talking in an examination room, and trying would
change the conditions for everybody in it. So none of this is about the three
hours. It is about the review classes, where the same expectations are still
being worked and where what you learn can still change somebody's preparation
while that is worth something.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 23, review part one, on the simplification questions

  Watch WHERE the restrictions come from. A page listing the right excluded
  values looks the same whether they were read off the original expression or
  worked backwards out of the simplified one, because on most questions the
  two lists agree — and the questions where they differ are exactly the ones
  that cost marks. The review room lets you see the habit instead of the
  outcome.

  Going well: the denominator factored and the excluded values written down
  before a single factor is cancelled.

  Stuck: restrictions added at the end, from the answer, in different ink.

  Record: three columns on your review plan — from the original, from the
  answer, not stated. That third column is who to reach in the clinic on the
  last day, before anybody sits down to three hours of it.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 26, at the individual conferences that last agenda
already sets aside

  Ask: "Two, six, twelve, twenty. Describe a procedure out loud that would
  give me the next one — in words, precise enough that I could follow it
  without seeing anything you have written down."

  Then: "A classmate writes that the square root of fifty plus the square
  root of two is the square root of fifty-two. Where does that go wrong, and
  what is the right answer?"

  The first is C1.2 word for word — determine and describe, in words, a
  recursive procedure for generating a sequence from its opening terms. What
  you are listening for is whether the description would survive being
  followed by somebody who cannot see the sequence: add four, then six, then
  eight, each step adding two more than the one before. A script shows you a
  formula; only this shows you whether they can build one from four numbers
  and say it aloud.

  The second is A3.2. The relationship that splits a radical is about
  multiplying, not adding, and the fastest refutation is arithmetic: about
  7.07 plus about 1.41 is over eight, while the square root of fifty-two is
  a bit above seven. The finished answer is six root two, which needs them to
  see fifty as twenty-five twos first. Listen for the
  test as much as the answer — a student who checks a suspicious identity with
  approximate values has a habit worth more than this one question.

  Record: one line each. You already have the Day 23 columns beside them.

The product evidence is the examination itself, and it arrives on its own.
