Today you read a piece of flash fiction — barely a page long — and by the
end of it you already knew things about the person at its centre that the
story never stated outright. Nobody wrote "she was impatient" or "he was
ashamed." You worked that out from what the character said, what they
did, and how the people around them reacted. That gap between what a
story tells you and what you infer is where characterization actually
happens, and naming it cleanly is the whole point of this page.

## Direct versus indirect characterization

A writer can simply tell you what someone is like — "he was proud" — and
that is direct characterization. It is fast, but it asks you to take the
narrator's word for it. Most strong short fiction, including the piece
you read today, prefers to show you and let you draw the conclusion
yourself. That is indirect characterization, and it almost always arrives
through one of five channels. English teachers have used the acronym
STEAL for decades because it holds up:

| Channel | What to look for |
|---|---|
| **S**peech | What a character says, and just as importantly, how |
| **T**houghts | Whatever the narration lets you see of their inner life |
| **E**ffect on others | How other characters respond to them |
| **A**ctions | What they actually do, separate from what they claim |
| **L**ooks | Physical description, dress, posture, expression |

Go back to the piece you read today. Which of these five did the writer
lean on hardest to build the person at its centre? Usually it is not all
five equally — a writer working in under a page has to choose, and the
choice itself tells you something about what mattered most to them.

## Round, flat, static, dynamic

Two more distinctions are worth having ready. A character can be
**round** (complex enough to surprise you) or **flat** (built around one
dominant trait, which is not automatically a flaw — a flat character can
do exactly the job a story needs). Separately, a character can be
**static** (ends the story the way they started it) or **dynamic**
(changes because of what happens to them). A short story often has room
for only one dynamic character, and figuring out who that is — and what
changed — is usually the fastest way into the story's meaning.

## Where this goes next

Character rarely stands alone. It is usually defined by how a person
meets conflict, which is the subject of [[Conflict and Plot Structure]],
the page you are reading alongside this one. Watch for characterization
all term: in how [[Boys and Girls]] reveals its narrator through what she
does rather than what she says, in how Wagamese lets Saul's silences
speak for him in *Indian Horse*, in how a soliloquy characterises Hamlet
from the inside out. Once you can name the tool, you keep noticing it
being used.
