Every angle in this set is measured **from the normal**. If an answer
of yours disagrees with mine by exactly the amount that would fix it,
that is almost certainly why.

**1.** Define, precisely: the **normal**, the **angle of incidence**,
the **angle of reflection**, and a **virtual image**.

> [!success]- Answer 1
> **Normal** — the line drawn perpendicular to the surface at the exact
> point where the ray meets it. It is a construction line, not
> something you can see, and on a curved mirror it points in a
> different direction at every point.
>
> **Angle of incidence** — the angle between the incoming ray and the
> normal.
>
> **Angle of reflection** — the angle between the outgoing ray and the
> normal, on the other side of it.
>
> **Virtual image** — an image formed where the light only *appears* to
> come from, because the outgoing rays have to be extended backwards to
> meet. No light actually arrives there, which is why you cannot catch
> a virtual image on a screen no matter where you put it.
>
> The law itself: the angle of reflection equals the angle of
> incidence, and the incident ray, the reflected ray, and the normal
> all lie in the same plane.

**2.** A ray strikes a plane mirror at an angle of incidence of 35°.
Give the angle of reflection, the angle between the incident ray and
the mirror **surface**, and the angle between the incident and
reflected rays.

> [!success]- Answer 2
> **Angle of reflection: 35°**, because $\theta_r = \theta_i$.
>
> **Angle to the surface:** the normal is perpendicular to the surface,
> so the two angles add to 90°:
> $90^\circ - 35^\circ = 55^\circ$.
>
> **Between the incident and reflected rays:** they sit 35° either side
> of the normal, so
> $35^\circ + 35^\circ = 70^\circ$.
>
> Three different angles, all correct, all describing the same picture.
> This is exactly why every optics answer must say *which* angle it is
> reporting — "the angle was 55°" is not wrong so much as unusable.

**3.** You stand 40 cm in front of a plane mirror. Describe the image
completely — location, size, orientation, and type — and give the
distance from you to your image.

> [!success]- Answer 3
> **Location:** 40 cm behind the mirror, along the perpendicular from
> you to the mirror. The image distance always equals the object
> distance for a plane mirror.
>
> **Size:** the same as you. Magnification is 1.
>
> **Orientation:** upright, but reversed front to back — which is what
> makes writing look mirrored and makes your raised right hand appear
> to be the image's left.
>
> **Type:** virtual. The rays leaving the mirror diverge, and they only
> meet when extended backwards, so nothing can be caught on a screen
> placed there.
>
> **Distance from you to the image:**
> $40\ \text{cm} + 40\ \text{cm} = 80\ \text{cm}$.
>
> That last one matters in practice. A camera focusing on your
> reflection has to focus for 80 cm, not 40 cm, because as far as the
> light is concerned it travelled the whole way.

**4.** What is the shortest plane mirror in which you can see yourself
from head to toe, and does it depend on how far away you stand? Justify
with the law of reflection.

> [!success]- Answer 4
> **Half your height**, and **no**, it does not depend on your
> distance from the mirror.
>
> The reasoning: consider the ray from the top of your head that
> reaches your eye. Because the angle of incidence equals the angle of
> reflection, it must strike the mirror at the point exactly **halfway
> in height** between the top of your head and your eye. Now do the
> same for the ray from your feet: it must strike the mirror halfway in
> height between your eye and the floor.
>
> Everything you can see lies between those two strike points, so the
> mirror needs to cover
>
> $$\tfrac{1}{2}(\text{head to eye}) + \tfrac{1}{2}(\text{eye to feet}) = \tfrac{1}{2}(\text{your height})$$
>
> Nothing in that argument mentions distance from the mirror. Step
> back and both strike points stay at the same **heights**; your image
> gets further away and shrinks in apparent size by exactly the amount
> needed to keep fitting. The mirror also has to be hung correctly —
> its top edge at the midpoint between your eyes and the top of your
> head — which is why a mirror of the right size can still be useless.

**5.** Two plane mirrors meet at 90°. A ray strikes the first mirror at
an angle of incidence of 30°. Find the angle of incidence on the second
mirror, and describe the final direction of the ray.

> [!success]- Answer 5
> At the first mirror, $\theta_i = \theta_r = 30^\circ$.
>
> The two mirrors are perpendicular, so their normals are perpendicular
> to each other. An angle of 30° from the first normal is therefore
> $90^\circ - 30^\circ = 60^\circ$ from the second normal.
>
> **Angle of incidence on the second mirror: 60°**, and the angle of
> reflection there is 60° as well.
>
> **Final direction:** the ray leaves travelling exactly **antiparallel
> to how it arrived** — back the way it came, offset sideways. The two
> turns add to $2(30^\circ) + 2(60^\circ) = 180^\circ$, and that total
> comes out the same for any starting angle, which you can check by
> repeating the arithmetic with 20° or 45°.
>
> That is the whole principle behind a retro-reflector: whatever
> direction the light comes from, it goes back to the source. It is why
> a cyclist's reflector lights up for the driver whose headlights hit
> it and for nobody else.

**6.** An object is placed **beyond the centre of curvature** of a
concave mirror. Describe the image: type, orientation, size, and
location. Then say what happens as the object is moved toward the focal
point.

> [!success]- Answer 6
> **Beyond C:** the image is **real** (the rays genuinely cross, so it
> can be caught on a screen), **inverted**, **smaller than the object**,
> and located **between F and C**.
>
> **Moving the object toward F**, the image moves outward, away from
> the mirror, and grows. At C the image is at C, the same size as the
> object, real and inverted. Between C and F the image is beyond C,
> real, inverted, and now **larger** than the object.
>
> **At F** the reflected rays come off parallel, so they never cross
> and never diverge from a common point — **no image is formed at all**.
> That is the case people forget, and it is the one that makes a
> spotlight work in reverse.
>
> **Inside F**, everything changes character: the image becomes
> **virtual, upright, and enlarged**, and it sits behind the mirror.
> That is a shaving or make-up mirror, and it is the only concave
> arrangement that gives you an upright image.

**7.** Why is a convex mirror used at the exit of a parking garage, and
why is there a warning printed on the passenger-side mirror of a car?

> [!success]- Answer 7
> A convex mirror curves outward, so the reflected rays **diverge**
> more than they would from a flat mirror. That has one consequence
> with two faces.
>
> **The advantage:** a much wider field of view. Light from a large
> spread of directions is gathered into your eye, so one small mirror
> shows the whole approach to the garage exit rather than a narrow
> strip of it. The image is always **virtual, upright, and smaller than
> the object**, whatever the distance — which is a convenient
> reliability.
>
> **The cost:** because everything is shrunk, a car looks smaller than
> it would in a flat mirror, and your brain reads "small" as "far
> away". The warning is there because the image is reduced, not because
> the mirror lies about position. The object really is closer than it
> appears.

**8.** A student writes: *"Light reflecting off a rough wall doesn't
follow the law of reflection — that's why you don't see your face in
it."* What is right, what is wrong, and what is the correct account?

> [!success]- Answer 8
> **What is right:** you genuinely cannot see your face in a rough
> wall, and roughness genuinely is the reason.
>
> **What is wrong:** the law of reflection is not suspended. It holds
> at **every single point** on that wall, exactly as it holds on a
> mirror.
>
> **The correct account:** the law is stated with respect to the
> **normal**, and on a rough surface the normal points in a slightly
> different direction at every point, because the surface tilts this
> way and that on a scale far too small to see. Parallel rays arriving
> together therefore each obey $\theta_r = \theta_i$ about *their own*
> normal and leave in scattered directions. That is **diffuse
> reflection**, and it is what makes the wall visible from everywhere
> in the room rather than from one special place.
>
> A mirror is not a surface that obeys a different law. It is a surface
> flat enough that all the normals point the same way, so the rays that
> arrived together also leave together — **specular reflection** — and
> arrive at your eye still carrying an image.
>
> The general habit worth taking from this: when something behaves
> unexpectedly, check whether the law has failed or whether one of its
> **inputs** has changed. Nine times out of ten it is the input.
