In this course the investigation comes **first**. You go to the bench
before anybody explains what you are about to see, because a question
you have felt is worth ten questions you have been handed. The concept
page arrives afterwards, and its job is to account for what your class
actually measured.

| Investigation | The question it opens |
| --- | --- |
| [[Looking at Tissue]] | If every cell came from one dividing cell, why do they not all look alike? |
| [[Modelling Digestion]] | Why does food have to be taken apart before a body can use it? |
| [[Evidence of a Chemical Change]] | How do you know something *new* was made, and not merely moved? |
| [[Balancing by Counting]] | Does anything actually disappear when a reaction happens? |
| [[Reaction Types Lab]] | Do reactions fall into families you could sort before anyone names them? |
| [[Modelling the Greenhouse Effect]] | Why is a planet warmer than the sunlight arriving at it would suggest? |
| [[The Law of Reflection]] | Is there a rule that decides where a reflected ray goes? |
| [[Finding the Focal Length]] | Can you say where the image will be *before* you go and look? |

Every page has the same seven parts: what you are trying to find out,
what you have to work with, safety, the prediction you write down
first, what to collect, what to bring to the consolidation discussion,
and what you should not claim from your data. Read the whole page
before the class it belongs to — especially the safety section, which
is not optional reading and is not the same list twice.

Three things are different from Grade 9, and they are the whole point.

- **You design.** Most pages hand you a question and a bench of
  equipment rather than numbered steps. What to change, what to
  measure, and what to hold still are yours to decide and yours to
  defend.
- **You predict in writing, before you measure.** Not roughly, not
  afterwards. With a *because*. See [[Mistakes Are Data]] for why being
  wrong on the record is the useful part.
- **You state limits.** Every page ends by asking what you would refuse
  to claim from the data you got. That paragraph is where the marks
  live — see [[How Marks Work]].

**Four of the eight are written up, and those write-ups are marked.**
[[Modelling Digestion]], [[Evidence of a Chemical Change]],
[[Modelling the Greenhouse Effect]], and [[Finding the Focal Length]] each
get the period after the bench work to be written up in class, one per
unit. The criteria are the same eight every time and they are published on
[[Lab Reports]], which you read on the day before the first write-up. The
other four carry no mark at all: what you owe them is a journal entry.

The bench work is done in pairs or threes and the raw readings belong to
the bench. From the analysis section onward, a write-up is yours alone.

> [!danger] Three things, every single lab day
> Eye protection on from the first move to the last of the cleanup.
> Long hair tied back, loose sleeves secured, closed-toe shoes. And any
> procedure **you** designed checked by me before you run it, including
> what could go wrong and what you would do about it. Missing any of
> these means the written alternative instead, for everyone's sake.

Write-up format: [[Writing a Lab Report]]. Drawings and ray diagrams:
[[Drawing Scientific Diagrams]]. Labels, symbols, and disposal:
[[Lab Safety and WHMIS]] and [[Safety in the Lab]]. Stuck at the
bench with ten minutes gone? [[Getting Unstuck]].
