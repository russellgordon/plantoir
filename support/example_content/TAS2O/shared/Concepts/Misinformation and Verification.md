Every round of [[News or Not]] ends the same way: the item half the
room believed turns out to be false, and it was always the *shareable*
one. That is not an accident, and it is not stupidity. It is design —
and understanding the design is the first defence.

## How false things spread

False stories travel faster than true ones because they are optimised
for sharing rather than accuracy: they trigger outrage or delight, they
confirm what a group already suspects, and they demand to be passed on
*right now*. Each reshare strips a little more context — the date falls
off, then the source, then the place — until an old photo with a new
caption reads as this morning's news. The platforms are accelerants:
they reward speed and emotion, not verification, a bargain with real
consequences for what a society ends up believing. Almost nobody in the
chain meant to lie. That is exactly why it spreads.

## Verification habits

Verification is not a talent; it is three questions asked in order,
every time, before you believe — and long before you share.

> [!question] The three questions
> **Who said this first?** Trace past the reshares to the original
> source, and judge that source, not the friend who forwarded it.
> **When is it from?** An old image under a new caption is the most
> common trick on the Internet. Check the date before the claim.
> **What surrounded it?** Find the full photo behind the crop, the
> full sentence behind the clip. Context is where truth lives.

These habits are drilled in [[Verification Practice]], and they change
how you read everything — including the confident claims that surface
in [[The Comment Section]].

A newsroom is the antidote to all of this, by construction: here,
nothing publishes until someone else has checked it. Every fact
verified before [[Filing a Story]] is a small repair to the shared
record — which is why accuracy sits first in
[[Our Newsroom Standards]], ahead of speed, and always will.
