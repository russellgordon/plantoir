A photograph feels like proof because it looks like looking. It is not.
Someone stood in a particular place, pointed a heavy machine at a chosen
subject, waited for a chosen moment, and later somebody else cropped it and
wrote a caption underneath. Every one of those steps is an argument, and
none of them is visible in the image.

## Four kinds, four different problems

**Official photographs** were commissioned — by a government, a department,
a company, a mission. They exist to show a programme working. Look for the
tidy row, the new equipment, the posed group.

**Press photographs** were chosen for a page and cut to fit a column. The
negative usually held more than the printed frame did.

**Family photographs** were made for people who already knew the story, so
they record what mattered privately and label nothing.

**Photographs of people who did not consent** are their own category, and
this course meets many: residential school photographs, images made of
Inuit communities by visiting expeditions, film footage sold to audiences
who would never meet the people in it. *Nanook of the North* is the famous
case, and the perspective it flattered was the audience's.

> [!question]- Five questions to put to any photograph (click to expand)
> **Who is behind the camera, and how did they get in the room?** Access is
> permission, and permission came from somebody.
>
> **What is the frame excluding?** Photographs are edges as much as content.
>
> **Is anyone posed, and who did the posing?**
>
> **Who wrote the caption, and when?** Captions are often added by an
> archivist decades later. The caption is a separate source with a separate
> date, and it can be wrong about the picture it sits under.
>
> **What did this image do after it was taken?** Published, filed, sold,
> exhibited, or never seen — that history is part of the evidence.

## A film to practise on

Film is photographs at speed, and the same five questions apply. This one
is four silent minutes from the City of Vancouver Archives. It records
Queen Elizabeth's visit to Vancouver in 1959: children are arranged into
the shape of a dogwood flower in Queen Elizabeth Park, and the Queen is
shown watching them.

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Bx5wri9LHpc)

Watch it once straight through. Then watch it again with these in hand:

- Which of the four kinds above is this, and what in the film tells you?
- Who is posed, and who did the posing? Find the moment the arranging
  shows.
- What is outside the frame? Name two things that must have been there
  and that the camera never turns to.
- The archive's title is *Visit of Queen Elizabeth - dogwood -
  playground*. It was probably written by an archivist, later. What does
  it leave out?

The film plays from YouTube, so it needs an internet connection. The
archive published it under a Creative Commons Attribution licence.

## Where to find them, described honestly

The Archives of Ontario has put more than 8,700 high-resolution images on
Wikimedia Commons under an open licence, with curated sets on Flickr and
film on YouTube — start from
[archives.gov.on.ca](https://www.archives.gov.on.ca/online-exhibits/).
Toronto Public Library's Digital Archive Ontario holds over 100,000
historical photographs, maps, postcards and ephemera, free to view
(accessed August 2026). It is often miscalled a newspaper archive; it isn't.

Handle what you find with the care set out in
[[Oral History and Testimony]], and use it as evidence, not decoration —
[[Using Evidence]] shows what that costs.
