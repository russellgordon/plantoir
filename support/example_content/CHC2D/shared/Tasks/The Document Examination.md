**The final evaluation. Three hours, in the examination period.**
Individual.

Unseen documents, and the whole century.

## What it looks like

You are given a dossier of five or six primary sources on a theme you
have studied — but sources you have not seen — spanning more than one
period of the course. They will disagree with each other.

You answer in three parts:

1. **Read the documents** (about 45 minutes). For each: what it is, who
   made it, for whom, and what it is evidence of. Say where two sources
   conflict and what could explain the conflict.
2. **Explain** (about 75 minutes). Using the documents and your
   knowledge of the course, account for what was happening and why. A
   strong answer crosses periods, because the dossier will.
3. **Judge** (about 45 minutes). A question is posed that the documents
   do not settle. Answer it, with your reasoning, and say what further
   evidence you would want.

The remaining time is for reading and planning. Use it.

## What is being examined

| Criterion | What it means here |
| --- | --- |
| Source criticism | Provenance and purpose established quickly and accurately |
| Corroboration | You use the documents against each other rather than in a list |
| Knowledge | Course knowledge deployed where the documents are silent |
| Concepts | Significance, cause and consequence, continuity and change, and perspective used to reason |
| Judgement | A defensible answer, with its uncertainty stated |

## Preparing for it

The three review periods work old dossiers against the clock. Bring your
[[Your Inquiry Notebook]] — the source work you did in September is the
best preparation there is, and rereading your own early attempts is the
fastest way to see how much sharper you have become.

> [!tip] Establish provenance before you read for content
> Who made this, when, and why — thirty seconds per document, before you
> read a word for meaning. Every year the strongest papers do this and
> the weakest ones do not.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

There is nothing to observe in a three-hour examination and nobody to talk
to in it, and attempting either would be unfair to the room. So this block
points at the two review periods instead — the last place in the course
where what you find out can still change what a student does.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 23, the unseen dossier worked against the clock
  Watch the first three minutes and then stop watching. A student who turns
  all six documents face up and reads the attributions before reading any
  of them for content has the habit. A student who begins at document one
  and reads to the end has not — and on an easy dossier the two papers can
  finish equally well.
  They have all been told to do this: it is printed on this page and you
  went through the paper on Day 22. That is the point. Under a clock, the students
  who know the rule and the students who own the habit come apart, and this
  is the last period where you can see which is which while there is still
  time to say so.
  Going well: pages turned quickly, notes in the margins of the header
  lines, no highlighter yet.
  Stuck: document one read closely while five documents wait.
  Record: two columns on your day plan — surveyed, read straight through.
  Give the finding back to the room afterwards, without names.
  That is A1.4 — interpreting and analysing evidence with strategies
  appropriate to historical inquiry — and a strategy is exactly the thing a
  finished paper cannot report on itself.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 24, while the class works the dossier that spans two
units
  You will not get another conversation with these students about history.
  Spend it on the ones you have heard least from, one of these questions
  each, with the documents in front of them.
  Ask: "Which of these would you drop, and what would you lose?"
  Then: "What is the one thing none of these thinks it has to explain?"
  Take the first unless a student has already dropped one, in which case
  take the second. Do not use the phrase "takes for granted" — that is the
  course's own stock question, set as homework in the first fortnight, and
  they will answer the habit rather than the dossier.
  The first forces a judgement about evidentiary weight instead of a
  description of conflict, which is what the paper asks for; a strong
  answer names what the dropped document was the only witness to. The
  second is the harder one, and it is historical perspective — one of the
  four concepts A1.5 names — heard directly: a student who can say that
  every one of these documents assumes a particular authority was the one
  that mattered, or assumes a word meant then what it means now, is
  reasoning with the concept rather than labelling with it. Some will say this far more
  clearly than they will ever write it, and that is the evidence you came
  for.
  Record: initials and one word — dropped one, named the assumption, did
  not get there. Ten students in ten minutes.

The product evidence is the examination itself. What these two periods buy
you is the reason a quiet student's mark does not have to rest on one
afternoon.
