You have spent three units learning how a novel, a story, or a play tries
to move you. Media texts — ads, newscasts, sponsored content, social
media posts — are doing exactly the same work, usually faster and with
less patience for subtlety. This page opens the unit by naming what to
watch for.

## Techniques that shape meaning

Media producers reach for a recognisable toolkit of conventions and
techniques, chosen because they reliably work on an audience:

- **Pacing and editing** — quick cuts build urgency or excitement; slow,
  held shots build seriousness or intimacy.
- **Music and sound** — a soundtrack tells you how to feel before the
  content has finished making its point.
- **Framing and camera choice** — a close-up creates intimacy or
  vulnerability; a wide shot can create distance or scale.
- **Repetition** — a slogan, an image, or a phrase repeated until it
  becomes familiar, and familiarity gets mistaken for trust.

None of these techniques is inherently dishonest. A documentary and an
advertisement can both use a slow, held shot — the technique is the same;
what differs is what it is being used *for*.

## Why a text is trying to persuade you at all

It is worth asking, before you even get to technique, why a given piece
of media exists in the first place. Almost nothing you encounter is
neutral: it was produced, marketed, and distributed by someone with a
reason for putting it in front of you — a network selling advertising
space, a platform whose recommendation algorithm rewards content that
keeps you watching, a regulatory environment (Canadian content rules, for
instance) that shapes what gets made and funded in the first place. Those
industry pressures are not visible on the screen, but they shape which
techniques a producer reaches for and why. A text funded by advertising
has different incentives than one that is not, and it is worth asking
which kind you are looking at.

## Reading actively

Use the following as a working checklist while you watch or read a media
text — a way to slow down your own attention, not something to check off
on the page itself:

- [ ] Ask who made this, and what they gain if it works on you.
- [ ] Name the technique doing the most work — pacing, music, framing,
      repetition, something else.
- [ ] Separate the claim being made from the feeling being produced.
- [ ] Ask what the text leaves out, and whether that omission is doing
      work too.
