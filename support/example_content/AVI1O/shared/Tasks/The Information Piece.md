**Due: mid-Unit 2.** One finished piece each.

You may work alongside a partner: share the message, the research into
who needs it, and the testing. The piece you hand in is your own, and
your mark comes from it and from what you can say about your own
choices — nothing your partner makes moves your mark in either
direction.

Art that has to communicate something specific to someone who did not
make it — a poster, an infographic, a set of signs, an illustrated
instruction, a public notice.

## The brief

Choose a real message someone in your school or community actually needs
communicated: where the recycling goes, how to sign up for something,
what to do in an emergency, why a club is worth joining.

Then design a piece that does it — using the elements and principles
deliberately, and at least one convention that carries meaning beyond
the literal.

## What is marked

| Criterion | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Communication | A stranger understands the message without you there |
| Design | Elements and principles used to direct the eye, not decorate |
| Convention | A metaphor, symbol, or visual reference used and explained |
| Craft | Finished properly, ready to be seen in public |
| Audience | You can say who it is for and how that shaped the choices |

> [!success]- The test, before you hand it in (click to expand)
> Give it to someone who does not know the brief. Ask them what it is
> telling them to do. If they hesitate, the design is doing the work you
> intended the words to do, or the reverse — and you have learned the
> most useful thing this task can teach.

[[Art That Tells You Something]] and [[Conventions in Art]] are the
ideas; [[Composition]] is how the eye gets moved around it.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 2, Day 15, the revision that follows the small-group
critique
  Watch for: whether the answer to a note is a change or an addition.
  The commonest move in this task is to patch a layout failure with
  another line of type — the picture is not doing its job, so words are
  sent in to explain it. A finished piece shows how much text is on it;
  it cannot tell you that the text arrived as a repair.
  Going well: something comes OFF the piece, or moves, before anything
  is added.
  Stuck: every note produces a new caption, arrow or heading, and the
  hierarchy the student built on Day 13 quietly disappears under them.
  Record: a plus or a minus against each name — did anything leave the
  piece this period?

TALK — Unit 2, Day 16, the conferences already on that agenda
  Ask: "Somebody actually needs this message. If they walked in now and
  looked at it, what would they ask you to change?"
  Then: "The convention you used — what does it stop you from saying?"
  A strong answer to the first predicts a specific objection from a
  specific person, and it usually surfaces what the piece leaves out:
  people who cannot read English quickly, people seeing it from six
  metres, people already past the door. That is A2.2 heard in
  conversation — applying the elements and principles to communicate is
  a choice about who is served, and a poster on a wall shows the choice
  without the reasoning. Weak answers say "nothing", which means the
  audience is still an idea rather than a person.

  The second question is C2.2: understanding a convention includes
  knowing what it forecloses — a red circle and a bar cannot
  say "not yet", an arrow cannot say "either" — and a student who chose
  a metaphor rather than inherited one can name the thing it will not
  carry. Do NOT ask what a stranger reads: they tested that on somebody
  as homework the night before and the answer is printed on the task
  page.
  Record: one line per student on the day plan, in the period.

The product evidence is the piece itself, handed in and posted on Unit
2, Day 17, and what came back from the wild.
