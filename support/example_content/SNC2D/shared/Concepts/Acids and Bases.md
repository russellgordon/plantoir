One of the reactions you sorted in [[Reaction Types Lab]] mixed two clear
liquids and produced nothing you could see, while the thermometer climbed and
the indicator changed colour. That is neutralisation, and it is a double
displacement reaction whose driving force is invisible: the product being
formed is water.

This page says what acids and bases actually are, what the pH scale measures,
and why the safety habit you were taught in [[Lab Safety and WHMIS]] applies
to both ends of the scale.

## What makes a solution acidic or basic

The definition used in this course is the one that works for the substances
you will handle: an **acid** is a substance that releases hydrogen ions,
$\ce{H+}$, when dissolved in water, and a **base** is one that releases
hydroxide ions, $\ce{OH-}$. That is why $\ce{HCl(aq)}$ is an acid and
$\ce{NaOH(aq)}$ is a base, and why the $\text{(aq)}$ matters so much — dry
hydrogen chloride gas releases nothing.

A lone hydrogen ion is a bare proton and does not drift around by itself; it
attaches to a water molecule to form the hydronium ion,
$\ce{H3O+}$. You will see both written, and they mean the same
thing about the solution. Using $\ce{H+}$ is a shorthand, not a
disagreement.

Two words that are constantly mixed up and are not synonyms:

- **Strong or weak** is about what fraction of the dissolved substance
  releases its ions. Hydrochloric acid is strong because essentially all of it
  does; acetic acid is weak because only a small fraction does at any moment.
  This is a property of the substance and cannot be changed.
- **Concentrated or dilute** is about how much of it is in a given volume of
  solution. This is a property of the mixture and you change it by adding
  water.

So a dilute solution of a strong acid can be far gentler than a concentrated
solution of a weak one, and neither phrase alone tells you whether something
is dangerous.

## pH is a scale of concentration, and it is not linear

pH measures the concentration of hydrogen ions in the solution. The scale is
logarithmic, which is the single most important thing about it: **each whole
number is a factor of ten.**

A solution at pH 3 has ten times the hydrogen ion concentration of one at
pH 4, and a hundred times that of one at pH 5. Lemon juice near pH 2 is not
"about twice as acidic" as black coffee near pH 5 — it is roughly a thousand
times as concentrated in hydrogen ions. Lower pH means more $\ce{H+}$; pH 7
is neutral at room temperature; above 7 the hydroxide ions are in the
majority and the solution is basic.

The familiar range from 0 to 14 covers the solutions you will meet, but it is
a convenience rather than a limit — concentrated laboratory solutions can sit
outside it at either end.

This is why a small pH change in a large body of water is a large chemical
change. As carbon dioxide dissolves in seawater it forms a weak acid, and the
measured fall in ocean pH over the industrial era is a substantial increase in
hydrogen ion concentration affecting organisms that build shells from
carbonate. That is a separate consequence of carbon dioxide emissions from the
warming described in [[The Greenhouse Effect]], caused by the same molecule
through completely different chemistry.

## Neutralisation, and what the product actually is

An acid reacts with a base to produce a **salt** and, usually, **water**:

$$\ce{HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) -> NaCl(aq) + H2O(l)}$$

Swap the partners and you can see the double displacement. The interesting
part is what drives it. Sodium and chloride ions were dissolved before and are
dissolved after — they change nothing. The real reaction is

$$\ce{H+(aq) + OH-(aq) -> H2O(l)}$$

and it goes because it takes ions out of the solution and locks them into a
molecule that stays intact. The energy released as those bonds form is the
temperature rise you measured.

"Salt" here means any ionic compound formed this way, not just table salt.
And the word **usually** in the definition is doing real work: an acid
reacting with a carbonate gives a salt, water, *and* carbon dioxide — the fizz
when vinegar meets baking soda — while ammonia reacting with hydrogen chloride
gives a salt and no water at all.

> [!warning] Bases are not the safe end of the scale
> A common and dangerous belief is that acids burn and bases are the mild
> ones that neutralise them. Concentrated sodium hydroxide — the active
> ingredient in drain cleaner — dissolves fats and proteins, which is exactly
> what your skin and the surface of your eye are made of. Strong base burns
> penetrate more deeply than acid burns and often hurt less at first, so the
> damage is worse by the time anyone reacts. Treat both ends of the scale with
> the same respect: goggles on, dilute by flooding with water, and report a
> spill or splash immediately rather than trying to neutralise it on yourself.

Neutralisation used well is everywhere: an antacid raising the pH of stomach
contents, lime spread on acidic farmland, treatment plants adjusting water
before it is released. Practise identifying and predicting these reactions in
[[Reaction Types Practice]], and then design one of your own in
[[The Reaction Investigation]].
