> [!abstract] At a glance
> Four write-ups, one in each of the first four units, each written **in
> class** and handed in
> at the end of that period · individually, from data your bench
> collected together · the same seven criteria every time

Four of this course's eight investigations are written up in full, and
those four write-ups are marked. They are the part of your seventy per
cent that arrives steadily rather than in five large pieces, which is
what lets a report card say something about your most recent and most
consistent work instead of about five afternoons.

The criteria below do not change between the first report and the
fourth. That is deliberate: improvement is only visible if the ruler
stays the same length all semester.

## The four, and when they happen

| Report | The investigation behind it | At the bench | Written in class |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| One | [[Boiling Points and Structure]] | Unit 1, Day 1 | Unit 1, Day 7 |
| Two | [[Identifying an Unknown Organic Compound]] | Unit 2, Day 4 | Unit 2, Day 6 |
| Three | [[Testing Hess's Law]] | Unit 3, Day 5 | Unit 3, Day 7 |
| Four | [[Disturbing an Equilibrium]] | Unit 4, Day 5 | Unit 4, Day 6 |

The writing period is never the bench period, and the gap is not an
oversight. Report one waits longest, because the idea its discussion
turns on — a name for the force doing the work — does not arrive until
Day 6. The other three sit one or two classes after the bench: long
enough for the consolidation to have happened, short enough that your
own table is still the thing you were arguing about.

Each investigation page carries its own question, its own safety notes,
what it wants you to collect, and — the section to reread before you
write — **what you should not claim**. The shape of the report itself,
section by section, is on [[Writing a Lab Report]].

**What each report's analysis has to answer**, beyond the shared
criteria:

| Report | The question the discussion has to settle |
| --- | --- |
| One | Which structural feature accounts for the ranking you measured, and why the pair with matched molar masses rules out the simple explanation |
| Two | Which test eliminated what, in what order, what a negative result bought you that a positive one would not have — and, for every test that gave a positive, the reaction that produced it, written out with states |
| Three | Hess's law stated in your own words, your two routes shown to be an application of it, and then whether they agreed **at the precision you could achieve**, judged against the threshold you set before you started |
| Four | Which one of your disturbances changed the constant rather than the position, and why that one is different |

The other four investigations are not written up formally. They are read
back in the consolidation, they turn up in your [[Chemistry Journal]],
and two of them are rehearsals for something larger:
[[Factors That Change a Rate]] before [[The Rate Investigation]], and
[[Building a Galvanic Cell]] before [[The Cell Report]].

## Written in class, and handed in before you leave

Every one of these is written in the period set aside for it, with your
data in front of you, your bench partner beside you, and me in the room.
It goes in at the end of that period. Nothing is finished at home.

That is deliberate three times over. A report carried home is a report
about what you can still remember a week later, and the thing worth
marking — what you made of the numbers while they were still strange —
has gone by then. The help is available at the moment you need it, which
is while you are staring at a difference of 4 kJ/mol wondering whether
it is a result or a rounding. And what is marked here is work I watched
you do, which is not possible for everything in a semester but is
entirely possible for these four.

## Individually, from data the bench shared

The bench work is done in pairs or threes and the raw readings belong to
the bench. **From the analysis onward the report is yours alone.**

Two people may hand in the same observation table. Two people who hand
in the same discussion have handed in one report between them, and I
will treat it that way.

## The bench counts, not only the page

Working safely and accurately at the bench is not a background
expectation in this course — it is in the curriculum, so it is part of
what these four reports are marked on. The investigation pages spell out
what each procedure needs; what carries across all four is this:

- The approved procedure, run as approved, with any change told to me
  **before** it happens rather than explained afterwards.
- Eye protection from the first move to the last of the cleanup, and
  everything else in the standing rules on the
  [[Investigations/index|Investigations]] page.
- **One thing changed at a time**, and whatever comparison or control
  that investigation asks for actually run rather than assumed.
- A reading you doubt **repeated**, not quietly replaced by the tidier
  one, and the doubtful reading still visible in your table.
- Instruments used the way they are meant to be used: the balance
  closed and zeroed, the probe rinsed between solutions, the pellet
  massed and moved rather than left on the pan while the argument
  finishes.

## How these are judged

| Quality | What it looks like at the bench, and in your write-up |
| --- | --- |
| Safe and effective at the bench | The procedure run as approved, with the comparison or control that investigation asks for, eye protection throughout, and a doubtful reading repeated rather than tidied away |
| Observations kept whole | Every trial recorded, including the excluded one and the reason it was excluded, each reading carrying the resolution of the instrument that produced it — and the table, graph or diagram the investigation asks for, laid out so a reader can find a number in it |
| Numbers you could defend | Units on every quantity and every column, and a final answer to the figures your **worst** measurement entitles you to — not to whatever the calculator offered |
| The prediction answered in those words | Supported or refuted, said plainly, against the threshold or hit rate you committed to before the data existed |
| The chemistry explained, not named | The mechanism attempted — which particles, which attraction, which rate changed — rather than the principle's name used as though naming were explaining |
| Limitations sized and directed | Each one named, which way it pushed your number, and roughly how much — plus one specific change you would make next time, and which limitation it is aimed at. "There may have been experimental error" is a sentence, not a finding |
| Written to be checked | Somebody who was not at your bench can follow every step and arrive at your numbers |

**The first row is not judged from the page.** Safe and effective at the
bench is judged at the bench, from what I see while you work — a
write-up cannot show it and is not asked to. Everything below it is
judged from what you hand in.

The last three rows are the Grade 12 rows, and they are the three that
vanish under time pressure. Run them against your draft with
[[Judging Your Own Work]] before the period ends — twenty minutes before
the bell is worth more than an hour afterwards, because afterwards there
is nothing you can do about what you find.

> [!important] The report that says where the model gave out
> Every one of these four investigations was designed so that a model
> gets you most of the way and then stops. Evaporation rate is not
> boiling point. A functional-group test tells you what a molecule has
> and not what it is. Hess's law is exact and your cups are not. Le
> Châtelier's principle gives a direction and never a size.
>
> A report that reaches that boundary and says so is doing the thing
> this course is for. A report that reaches it and writes around it is
> the more common outcome, and it is the difference between the two
> right-hand columns in [[How Marks Work]].

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

These four are the steadiest evidence in the course and the cheapest
place to gather the other two kinds: each one gives you a bench period
where the numbers are being made and a writing period where they are
lying on the desk in front of the person who made them. Two of the eight
periods are written out below, and one more thing is worth looking for.

OBSERVE — Unit 3, Day 5, while the three calorimetry runs are set up
  Watch for: whether the sodium hydroxide is weighed by difference, and
  how long it stands open while that happens. Every bench has written a
  plan for massing a hygroscopic solid quickly, and the plan and the
  afternoon are two different things. Solid left sitting in an open boat
  takes up water and carbon dioxide the whole time, so part of what is
  weighed is not sodium hydroxide, the moles come out high, and the two
  enthalpies measured from the solid come out too small while the third,
  measured from a solution, does not move at all — which makes the two
  routes disagree for a reason that has nothing to do with Hess's law.
  The report cannot show you any of it: 2.03 g reads the same whether
  the boat stood open for eight seconds or ninety.
  Going well: the boat weighed, filled and covered in one movement; the
  stock container shut before anything else happens; the boat reweighed
  after the transfer, so what is recorded is what actually left it.
  Stuck: an open boat on the bench while the cups are argued about; the
  mass taken only after the solid has been standing; the transfer
  assumed complete and the intended mass written down instead.
  Record: on your seating plan, a tick for the benches that weighed by
  difference with the container shut, a circle for the ones to go back
  to. That is A1.5, and it is one lap of the room.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 6, moving between benches while they write report four
  Ask: "You added calcium chloride to the limewater and something
  appeared. If I had handed you sodium chloride instead, what would you
  have predicted, and would you have been right?"
  Then: "Your control tube sat on the rack all period. Suppose it had
  gone slightly yellow by the end. Which of your rows would you still
  trust, and which would you throw out?"
  A strong first answer says that sodium chloride shares no ion with
  that equilibrium, so nothing should happen — the shift belongs to the
  ion the two solutions have in common, not to having added something.
  That is E3.3 spoken, and a report can name Le Châtelier's principle
  correctly on every line without it. A strong second answer keeps the
  rows judged against the control at the same moment and throws out the
  ones judged against a memory of how the tube looked earlier — and
  notices that a drifting control drifts in a direction, since the
  room's own carbon dioxide acidifies an open tube, so it would inflate
  every acidifying disturbance and hide the one going the other way.
  That is the bias-and-error clause of A1.8, and it is the clause a tidy
  limitations paragraph most often skips.
  Record: three names in your day plan — the ones who reached the
  direction of the drift unaided. Everybody else gets the question again
  on Day 7.

ALSO — Unit 2, Day 4, the identification bench
  One thing, and it needs no recording: watch who runs a test their own
  decision tree has already made pointless, and who stops to re-read the
  tree. The report shows the order the tests came in; it cannot show
  whether the order was still being thought about.

The product evidence is the four reports themselves, each handed in at
the end of its own writing period.
