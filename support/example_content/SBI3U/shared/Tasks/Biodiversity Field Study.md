> [!abstract] At a glance
> **Format:** field data plus a four-page report. **Groups of three**, and
> two sections written by you alone. **Due:** end of Unit 1.
> **Assessed on:** Thinking and Investigation, Communication.

Sample one small area properly and say what lives there — with numbers
that another group could check.

## The brief

1. **Choose a site** on or near school property, and a second contrasting
   site (mown versus unmown, sunny versus shaded, edge versus interior).
2. **Sample systematically** using quadrats. Decide the number and
   placement BEFORE you start, and justify it. Random placement is not
   the same as convenient placement.
3. **Identify** what you find to the finest level you can support, using
   the key from [[Using a Dichotomous Key]]. "Unknown grass B" recorded
   consistently is honest and usable; a wrong species name is not. For
   three of your organisms, give the full taxonomic rank as far as you
   can defend it, and say which characteristic put it in each group —
   that is what a key is actually doing.
4. **Calculate** species richness and a diversity index for each site.
5. **Compare**, and account for the difference with one abiotic factor you
   measured.

## What your group does, and what you do alone

The site choice, the sampling design and the field data are your group's,
and one set of them is handed in. **Two sections are written by you
alone, and your mark rests on them:** the taxonomic ranks for three
organisms with the characteristic that placed each one (step 3), and the
comparison in step 5 — the difference between the sites, accounted for by
one abiotic factor you measured. Put your name on each. Three people
sharing a quadrat is how field biology works; three people sharing one
explanation is not.

## What is marked

The sampling design and the honesty of the identification, far more than
the number of species. A study that found six species and defends its
method beats one that claims twenty without saying how it looked.

## Success criteria

Read these on launch day, and again with [[Judging Your Own Work]] on the
working period before you hand it in.

| Quality | What it looks like in your study |
| --- | --- |
| Designed before it was done | The number and placement of quadrats is written down, and justified, before any quadrat is placed |
| The rule survived the field | Where you departed from your own design, you say so and say what it cost |
| Honest identification | Every organism is named to a level you can defend; "unknown grass B" appears where it should, used consistently |
| Ranks with reasons | For three organisms, each rank names the characteristic that put it there |
| Numbers another group could check | Raw counts, then richness, then the index, with the arithmetic shown once |
| A difference accounted for | The comparison names one abiotic factor you measured, not one you assumed |
| Readable by a stranger | Tables titled, graph chosen for the comparison, no term used before it is defined |

> [!note] For teachers
> Two 40-minute periods outdoors is enough if the quadrats and keys are
> prepared. The contrast between mown and unmown ground is reliably
> dramatic and needs no travel.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 1, Day 11, the sampling period outdoors
  The criteria ask them to declare where they departed from their own
  design. This period is the only way you will ever know whether that
  declaration is complete, because the report can only contain the
  departures they noticed and chose to write down.
  Watch for: quadrats lifted and moved off bare ground; a placement rule
  quietly loosened at the second site because the first one was slow.
  Going well: a quadrat left where the rule put it, and the empty result
  recorded as data.
  Stuck: every quadrat somehow on the interesting patch, and nobody
  saying so.
  Record: your class list with two columns — followed it, moved it. Then
  read your column against their method section when the study comes in;
  a group that declared what you saw has earned the row, and a group that
  declared something you did not see has earned it twice.

TALK — Unit 1, Day 13, the working period on the taxonomic ranks
  Ask: "Your unknown grass B — what would you have to see to move it from
  family down to genus?"
  Then: "Which of your three ranks would you defend hardest, and which
  one is a guess you are recording honestly?"
  A strong answer names the characteristic doing the work at each level
  and says where the evidence runs out. That is B3.1 heard rather than
  read, and a table of ranks in the finished report looks identical
  whether it was reasoned or copied off the key. This is also the
  individual evidence for a task done in threes.
  Record: one line per student, and the rank they could not defend.

The product evidence is the report, handed in at the start of Unit 2.
