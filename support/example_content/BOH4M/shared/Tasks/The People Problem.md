**Launched near the end of Unit 2. Finished in the working period on
the first day of Unit 3, and handed in the class after that.**
Individual. About 900 words.

You draft it from the data pack before that period — drafting is
preparation, and preparation is never marked. The finishing, and the
check with me that goes with it, happen in class.

A diagnosis. You are handed a workplace where something is wrong with the
people, and asked what is actually causing it.

## The situation

Your teacher will give you one of three scenarios — a team with rising
turnover, a department where two groups have stopped cooperating, or a
high-performing employee whose work has fallen off. Each comes with data:
attendance, output, a survey extract, and two short interviews.

## What to produce

1. **The symptoms**, separated from the causes. List what you can
   actually observe.
2. **Three candidate explanations**, drawn from different parts of the
   course — motivation, conflict, job satisfaction, communication,
   workload.
3. **The evidence for and against each.** This is the marked centre of
   the task: a diagnosis that only argues for its favourite is not a
   diagnosis.
4. **Your conclusion**, with the one piece of evidence you would gather
   next if you could.
5. **What you would do on Monday**, and what you would measure in six
   weeks.

## How it is marked

| Criterion | What earns the marks |
| --- | --- |
| Diagnosis | Symptoms and causes kept apart |
| Use of theory | The right tools chosen from the whole unit, not the most recent one |
| Evidence | Claims tied to the data you were given |
| Judgement | A realistic first action, with a way of knowing whether it worked |

> [!warning] Do not reach for the incentive
> The most common answer to any people problem is "offer a bonus". Before
> you write that, apply the can't/won't test from
> [[Motivation in Practice]] and the conflict styles in
> [[Conflict Styles]] — most of these scenarios are not motivation
> problems at all.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 3, Day 1, the twenty-five-minute working period at the
  end of that class
  Watch for: which part of the data pack a student keeps going back to.
  The attendance and output numbers are the symptoms and they are easy to
  work from; the two interviews are where the attitudes and perceptions
  are, and they take longer to read. A diagnosis written only from the
  numbers can still quote one interview line and look complete. Whether
  the transcripts were reopened after the explanations were chosen is
  visible here and nowhere else. That is B1.2 (explain the relationship
  between job satisfaction and an individual's personality, attitudes,
  and perceptions) — attitudes live in what people said, not in the
  attendance column.
  Going well: an interview page marked up, with something circled that
  contradicts the student's favourite explanation.
  Stuck: the spreadsheet is the only thing that has been open all period.
  Record: a tick or a dash beside each name on the seating plan, on the
  same pass of the room as the notes.

TALK — Unit 3, Day 1, while you go round leaving notes on the drafts in
  that same period
  Everybody gets the written note; that is the checkpoint the class page
  promises and it takes most of the twenty-five minutes. For eight or ten
  of them, ask the question while you are already at the desk. Choose the
  ones whose written work has told you least so far — the rest are
  handing you a paper tomorrow.
  Ask: "Name the reward this workplace is actually giving — not the one
  in the pay policy — and say what behaviour it is buying." A strong
  answer finds something nobody designed: the shift that always gets the
  easy route, the person whose complaints get answered fastest. That is
  C3.3 (explain the relationship between motivation, rewards, and job
  performance), and it is the question that separates a diagnosis from a
  summary.
  Then: "Of the two people in the interviews, which one is avoiding the
  conflict — and what is the avoidance buying them?" A strong answer
  names the style and says who pays for it. That is C2.4 (evaluate
  conflict-management styles and their impact on a situation). Avoid the
  can't/won't question — the task page prints it and Day 21 of Unit 2
  taught it.
  Record: one line per student you reach, on the day plan, right then —
  a letter and three words. Circle the names you did not get to; the
  seminar on Day 22 of Unit 2 and the retrieval clinic on Day 22 of Unit
  3 are both places to catch them.

The product evidence is the 900-word diagnosis, handed in on Day 2 of
Unit 3, after fifteen minutes of that period are spent making the change
your note named.
