**Due: mid-Unit 3.** Individual. About 600 words.

One work of art, looked at properly and written about using the critical
analysis process.

## The work

A work your teacher approves — historical or contemporary, in a gallery,
in a book, or online — that you can look at repeatedly and at reasonable
size. Not a thumbnail, and not something you already know the answer
about.

## The four stages, labelled

The same four as [[The Critical Analysis Process]], with the same names —
label each section of your writing with the stage it is, because naming
the stages correctly is part of what is marked.

1. **Initial reaction.** What you felt or noticed first, before
   analysing. Honest, including boredom or confusion.
2. **Analysis and interpretation.** Description first: what is actually
   there — subject, elements, principles, materials, scale — and no
   judgement yet. Then how the parts work together, what the work seems
   to be about, and what in the work supports that.
3. **Cultural context.** Who made this, when, for whom, and under what
   conditions — and what that changes about the reading you just made.
4. **Aesthetic judgement.** Does it succeed — first on its own terms,
   then on yours? "It did what it was after and I still dislike it" is a
   conclusion; "I liked it" is not.

## What is marked

| Criterion | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Description first | Evidence before opinion; the commonest failure is the reverse |
| The elements | Identified accurately and connected to their effect |
| Interpretation | An argument supported by what is visibly there |
| Context | You find out when, where and for whom the work was made, and say what knowing it changed |
| Judgement | You say whether the work succeeds on its own terms before you say whether you like it |
| Terminology | Correct, and used because it is precise — including the names of the four stages |
| Change | You say how your reading shifted between first and second look |

> [!tip] Write down your first reaction before you know anything
> It is the only chance you get — once you have read about the work you
> cannot recover it, and the difference between reaction and
> interpretation is half of what [[Looking Twice]] teaches.

[[The Critical Analysis Process]], [[Describing What You See]], and
[[Interpreting a Work]] are the method.

OBSERVE — Unit 3, Day 6, the studio time before the peer reading of
drafts
  Watch for: whether the work is in front of the student while they
  write. The agenda asks for exactly that, which is the point — B1.2
  says the elements are to be identified ON THE BASIS OF EXAMINATION,
  and this is the one period the schedule asks for it out loud, so it is
  the one where not doing it is visible. A finished 600 words full of
  accurate detail could have come from looking or from a catalogue
  paragraph, and by Day 8 you cannot tell which.
  Going well: the student goes back to the image between sentences, and
  points at it rather than describing it when you stop by.
  Stuck: the reproduction is closed, or shrunk into a corner of the
  screen. The context stage is drafted this week too, so a paragraph
  about who the work was made for is on task; a biography of the artist
  that never comes back to the picture is not.
  Record: one column on the class list — looking, or not — on a single
  circuit of the room.

TALK — Unit 3, Day 5, the conferences already on that agenda
  Ask: "What would have to be different in the picture for the reading
  you are heading towards to fall apart?"
  Then: "What is in that picture that you cannot explain yet?"
  A strong answer to the first names something specific and small — the
  direction of one figure's gaze, the fact that the light comes from
  outside the frame — and understands that a reading which nothing could
  break is not a reading. That is B1.3 in conversation: interpretation
  tied to what is actually in the work. The second question suits this
  day, which is early — the description is drafted for homework
  afterwards — and it is the more useful of the two, because the part a
  student cannot explain is where B1.2 is really tested. An answer of
  "nothing" means they have stopped looking; a good answer names a
  specific thing and is annoyed by it.
  Record: two words per student on the class list — the feature they
  named, and the thing they could not explain.

The product evidence is the 600 words, handed in on Unit 3, Day 8.
