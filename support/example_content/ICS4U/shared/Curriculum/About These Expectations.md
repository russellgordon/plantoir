Three notes about this course's status and this reproduction:

- **This curriculum is current.** A newer Grade 10 computer studies
  course exists — *Digital Technology and Innovations in the Changing
  World* (ICD2O, 2023) — but it replaced only the Grade 10 material.
  The Ministry's own ICS4U course page states that the 2008 computer
  studies curriculum remains in effect and that Grades 11 and 12
  courses continue to be based on it.
- **The document prints no teacher prompts.** What you see on each
  page is the whole of what the Ministry prints beneath that
  expectation.
- **Its typography and its slips are reproduced, not tidied.** The
  document sets `(e.g., …)` examples in italics but `(i.e., …)`
  clarifications in roman, and that distinction is kept. So are three
  things a proofreader would have changed: `D1.1` prints
  "(e.g. ergonomic standards)" with no comma after "e.g." — the only
  such instance in the course; `C1.1` prints "UML [Unified Modeling
  Language]" with the American spelling; and the hyphens in phrases
  like "character-to-integer" in `A1.2` are the document's own, not
  artifacts of a line break.

Each page keeps its code, and every concept, exercise, and task in
this course, and every stage of the large software project that
[[B2. Software Project Contribution|B2]] asks for, links back to the
codes it addresses. The codes are also tags, so every page tagged `#C2`
can be gathered in one place.

## Why the codes matter more than they look

A code is a shared address. When a student asks "why are we doing
this?", the answer is a link. When a department writes a course
outline, or a teacher takes over a class partway through, the codes
are what make one person's planning legible to another.
