Research notes arrive in the order you found them, not the order your
essay needs them in. An outline is what turns a pile of notes into a plan
— and building one is a separate step from researching, not something you
do automatically as you go.

## Three moves

1. **Cluster.** Spread your notes out — physically, or in a document —
   and group anything that's really making the same point, even if you
   found those notes on different days from different sources.
2. **Order the clusters.** Decide which idea has to come first for the
   next one to make sense, not which idea you happen to like best. A
   cluster that depends on a term or fact from another cluster has to come
   after it.
3. **Give each cluster a job.** Write, above each cluster, the one
   sentence it needs to prove. If you can't write that sentence, the
   cluster probably isn't one idea yet — it's two, and they need to
   separate.

## A worked example

Say your notes for [[Your Canada, Your Story]] are about media coverage
of a Canadian news event, gathered across several sources in no
particular order: two notes about which outlets covered the story first,
one about a misleading headline, three about how coverage changed once
the story went viral, one definition of "confirmation bias" you copied
from a source.

Clustered, that becomes three groups: *how the story broke*, *how the
headline shaped the first impression*, *how coverage shifted under
attention*. The definition doesn't belong to any of them on its own — it's
a tool you'll use inside whichever cluster needs it, most likely the
headline one.

Ordered, "how the story broke" comes first because the other two clusters
assume the reader already knows what happened. Each cluster becomes a
paragraph, and each paragraph's job is the sentence you wrote above its
notes.

## What this saves you

Drafting straight from a pile of notes usually means restructuring the
whole essay halfway through, once you realize paragraph four actually
needed to come before paragraph two. An outline moves that problem
earlier, where it's cheap to fix — crossing out a line on a page — instead
of later, where it's expensive — rewriting three paragraphs the night
before something is due.[^1]

[^1]: This is also why the outline day sits on the schedule *before* the
    first drafting day, not folded into it — the two are genuinely
    different kinds of thinking, and doing them at the same time usually
    means doing neither one well.
