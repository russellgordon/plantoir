A student paper lives inside the institution it covers. The people
it reports on assign its marks, schedule its room, and approve its
budget — a working definition of conflict of interest, and we
publish anyway. Two exits tempt every school newsroom: timidity,
where nothing uncomfortable ever runs and the paper becomes a
newsletter; and recklessness, where anything runs and someone real
pays for our practice. The third road is harder — standards strong
enough that uncomfortable stories become POSSIBLE, because the room
can show exactly how carefully they were made.

Questions worth arguing about:

1. Mould in the change rooms, documented and confirmed. The story
   embarrasses the school that funds the paper. Whose interest is
   a school paper actually for — and who gets to answer that?
2. An uncomfortable story and an unfair one can look identical from
   the hallway. Write the room's test for telling them apart, in
   one sentence, before the week you need it.
3. Review before publication: a second set of eyes is how every
   real newsroom works. When does review become veto? Name the
   exact moment it crosses.
4. A student did something genuinely newsworthy and genuinely
   wrong. What would this room require before printing the name —
   and does the answer change at fourteen versus eighteen?
5. "We'd get in trouble" and "we can show our work" can describe
   the same story. What, concretely, moves a story from the first
   sentence to the second — sources, records, right of reply?

Carry it back to practice: [[Our Newsroom Standards]] is not a
fence around the newsroom; it is the licence to leave the yard. A
story built on named sources, documented facts, and a real chance
for the other side to speak — per [[Releases and Consent]] and the
checks that carry [[The Investigation]] — is a story the room can
defend in any office in the building. That is what a free school
press looks like: not permission, preparation.
