Unseen passages, answered under time. This is the examination's largest
single section and the one that improves fastest with practice.

**1.** Read this and answer in one sentence each: what has happened, and
how do you know?

> He counted the tins twice. Then he moved four of them to the back of
> the cupboard, where the light did not reach, and closed the door
> quietly, the way you close a door when somebody is asleep.

> [!success]- Answer 1
> What: he is hiding food. How: the second count, the deliberate move out
> of sight, and the simile about somebody sleeping — which tells you
> there is another person in the house he does not want to wake or to
> know.

**2.** From the same passage: what does the last clause tell you about
the relationship?

> [!success]- Answer 2
> That care and concealment are happening at once. He is quiet *for*
> somebody — the same person he is hiding the tins from. A weaker answer
> says "he is sneaky"; the passage will not support only that.

**3.** Identify the technique and its effect: *the light did not reach*.

> [!success]- Answer 3
> Understatement doing the work of a metaphor: the cupboard's dark is
> literal, and it also names what he is doing to the household — keeping
> something out of the light. Naming the device is worth nothing without
> the second half of that sentence.

**4.** Under time: read an unseen paragraph for four minutes and write
one analytical paragraph in eight. Then check it against
[[The Essay]].

> [!success]- Answer 4
> No fixed answer. The check: claim in sentence one, evidence quoted by
> sentence three, and at least two sentences of analysis. If the
> paragraph ends where the quotation ends, it is not finished.

## The poem

Twenty per cent of the examination is an unseen poem. Prose questions
practised on prose do not carry over on their own — the moves are the
same, but the evidence is smaller and the line break is a technique that
prose does not have. The next three questions use one short poem,
written for this page.

> The house sold in a week. The new people
> painted the door a colour we would not have chosen
> and took out the hedge.
>
> Every February I drive past
> slowly, the way you read a letter
> you have already answered.

**5.** What has happened, and what is the speaker's position on it? One
sentence each.

> [!success]- Answer 5
> What: a family home was sold and changed by strangers. Position: not
> anger — something closer to a habit the speaker has not broken. "We
> would not have chosen" is the only judgement in the poem, and it is
> about a colour.

**6.** Choose the line break doing the most work in this poem and say
what it does.

> [!success]- Answer 6
> "I drive past / slowly" is the strongest: the break holds the car still
> for a beat before the adverb arrives, so the reader slows at the moment
> the speaker does. "The new people" is the other good answer — the line
> ends on strangers, and the next line hands them the door.

**7.** Identify the simile and its effect. Then say what the poem would
lose without the word *February*.

> [!success]- Answer 7
> "The way you read a letter you have already answered" — rereading
> something already settled, which is what the drive is. Without
> *February* the habit has no calendar: one named month turns a feeling
> into an anniversary, and "every" turns it into years of them.

**8.** Under time: eight minutes on an unseen poem, then one analytical
paragraph in ten. The extra two minutes are for reading it twice, which
is not optional with a poem.

> [!success]- Answer 8
> No fixed answer. The check is the same as question 4's, plus one: at
> least one piece of your evidence should be something only the poem's
> form does — a break, a stanza gap, a repetition, a line that will not
> end where the sentence does.

**9.** The examination asks "how does the writer create X?" Rewrite that
question in your own words before answering it.

> [!success]- Answer 9
> "What did the writer do, in the words on this page, that produced X in
> me?" That rewrite is the whole method: it forces you back to the text
> rather than to your impression of it.
