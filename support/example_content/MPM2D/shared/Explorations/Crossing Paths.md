Two stories, one grid. Jo has \$90 saved and adds \$5 every week. Sam
has \$30 and adds \$10 every week. Somewhere ahead of them is a week
when the two bank balances say the same number — and everything about
that moment is readable from a picture, before any algebra exists.

## The task

Put both stories on one graph and find where they cross. Then answer
the questions the picture raises: *when* does Sam catch Jo? *how
much* money does each have at that moment? who is ahead before, and
after? Finally, write the crossing as a sentence about the story, not
about the graph. When your group is satisfied, invent a second pair
of stories — two walkers, two phone plans, two fundraisers — whose
crossing means something different, and trade with a neighbouring
board.

> [!question]- Getting started (click to expand)
> - A table, week by week, costs nothing and lies about nothing.
>   Where do the two columns agree?
> - One grid, shared axes, agreed scales — two graphs on two grids
>   can never cross.
> - "Meeting" needs *both* coordinates to match. Same balance in
>   different weeks is a coincidence, not a crossing.
> - Could two stories cross *before* week zero? What would that even
>   mean?

## What mathematics tends to surface

Each story is a line: its steepness is dollars per week, its starting
height is the opening balance — and the crossing is the one point
that satisfies both stories at once. That is the central object of
[[Linear Systems]], met here as a picture first. Groups usually
notice the graph's weakness too: when the crossing lands between
gridlines, "about week 12" stops being good enough, and the appetite
for an exact method is precisely what
[[Solving Systems Algebraically]] feeds. [[Using Desmos]] will draw
both lines in seconds — but *you* say what the intersection means.

## An extension

Design a pair of stories that *never* meet, and a pair that meet
everywhere — then say what each situation looks like in the story, on
the graph, and in the equations. A business version of the same
crossing, costs against revenue, runs through [[Break-Even]], and
[[Linear Systems Practice]] is where the technique settles in.
