One current technology story, one minute of telling, and three
columns on the board. Same routine as Grade 10, with one addition
that changes its character: this year the middle column has to contain
a **number with a unit**, or an honest admission that no number was
published. Engineering claims live and die on quantities, and the
fastest way to spot an empty claim is to go looking for the units and
find nothing there.

## The three columns

| Claimed | Known | Who benefits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What the headline says, in its own words | What is actually measured, with units and test conditions | Who is better off if you believe the claim before it is proven |

## How to run it

1. One student from the rotating roster brings the story and tells it
   in about a minute. No screen required — if it cannot be told, it
   was not understood.
2. The class fills the three columns. The middle column is filled
   last and slowest, because it is the hard one.
3. The gap between column one and column two is the discussion.
4. Close with a single sentence: what measurement, under what
   conditions, would move this claim from *claimed* to *known*?

## The units test

Watch what happens to a claim when you ask for units. "Twice as fast"
— at what workload, measured how, against which part? "Charges in
five minutes" — to what percentage, from what temperature, over how
many cycles before the capacity drops? "Lasts a lifetime" — whose?
A specification that survives those questions is engineering. One
that dissolves was advertising all along, and now you can tell them
apart at a glance, which is a skill worth actual money.

Battery claims are the reigning champions of this genre.[^1]

> [!question] Who benefits is a question, not a verdict
> Sometimes the honest answer is "everyone" — genuine improvements do
> get announced, and cynicism is not the same as analysis. The habit
> is asking the question every single time, so that the one time it
> matters, it has already been asked. The same standard runs through
> [[Writing About Technology]]: a claim needs a measurement, and a
> measurement needs units and conditions.

## One variation

Bring two write-ups of the *same* announcement from different
outlets and fill the columns twice. Watching the *known* column sit
perfectly still while the *claimed* column swings from "breakthrough"
to "incremental" is the whole lesson, delivered without anyone having
to argue for it.

[^1]: Watch for the swap between energy and power. A cell that
    accepts charge quickly and a cell that holds a lot of charge are
    two different achievements, measured in different units — watts
    against watt-hours — and a headline that praises one while
    implying the other has not lied, exactly.
