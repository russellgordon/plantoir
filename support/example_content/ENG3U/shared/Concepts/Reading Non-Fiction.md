Non-fiction argues. Reading it well means locating the argument, testing
the evidence, and noticing the craft — because good non-fiction persuades
partly through style.

## Locate the argument

- **What is the claim?** State it in one sentence the writer would
  accept.
- **What kind of claim is it?** Fact, value, or policy — each needs a
  different kind of evidence.
- **What is the evidence?** Data, anecdote, authority, personal
  experience. Which is doing the persuading?
- **What is conceded?** A writer who concedes nothing is not arguing;
  they are declaring.

## Notice the craft

| Move | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| The anecdote that opens | Buys attention, and frames everything after it |
| The rhetorical question | Assumes agreement it has not earned |
| The list of three | Sounds complete whether or not it is |
| Irony and understatement | Lets a writer criticise without shouting |
| Direct address | Makes a reader complicit |

Thomas King's *The Inconvenient Indian* uses the last two constantly. Ask
what the humour is doing — it is not there to lighten the material.

## Test it

1. Who is the writer, and what is their standing to make this claim?
2. Would somebody informed disagree, and with which part?
3. What is left out?
4. What would change your mind — and does the text address it?

That fourth question is the one that separates reading from agreeing.
