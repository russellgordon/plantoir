> [!abstract] At a glance
> **Seven write-ups**, spread from September to January · each written **in
> class**, in the period set aside for it · **individually**, from data your
> group collected · **Format:** the six sections on [[Writing a Lab Report]]

Seven of this course's investigations get written up properly, and those
seven write-ups are marked. They are the part of your mark that arrives
steadily instead of in four large pieces — which is what lets a report card
say something about your most recent and most consistent work rather than
about four busy weeks.

Every investigation page carries its own purpose, safety notes, procedure and

Six of the seven are written in the period straight after the bench work.
[[Testing Conductivity]] is the exception — it is quick enough to run and
write in one period, which makes it the shortest report of the seven.

## Written in class, and why

Each of these is written here, with your data in front of you and me in the
room. That is deliberate, twice over.

A report written at home three days later is a report about what you can
remember, and the thing actually worth marking — what you made of the
numbers while they were still strange — has gone by then. And writing it
here means the help arrives at the moment you need it, which is never the
moment you are stuck at a kitchen table. Ask.

## Individually, from data you shared

The bench work is done in pairs or threes, and the raw data belongs to the
group. **From the analysis onward the report is yours alone.** Two people may
hand in the same table of readings; two people handing in the same analysis
have handed in one piece of work between them.

## Success criteria

The same six rows every time, so that by January you can run them from
memory. Use them with [[Judging Your Own Work]] before you hand a report in.
The criteria never change, which is exactly what makes improvement across
seven reports visible.

| Quality | What it looks like in your report |
| --- | --- |
| A purpose and a prediction | The question in one sentence, and a prediction with a **because** in it — the reason, not the guess |
| Observations kept clean | Units on every column, the odd result written down rather than quietly dropped, and no interpretation in this section |
| A graph that carries the argument | Axes labelled and scaled, data plotted rather than joined dot to dot, and a line of best fit where the data supports one |
| Analysis that reads the numbers | The calculation shown once in full, the trend read off the graph, and the result set beside what the science predicted |
| Limits named honestly | The step that could have misled you, roughly how much it could have moved the number, and one change that would actually reduce it |
| A conclusion the data allows | One paragraph answering the purpose, with the uncertainty left in — including "nothing I can claim from this" where that is the truth |

The fifth row is the one that separates a report from a recipe. "There may
have been experimental error" is a sentence, not a finding; naming the step
that could have gone wrong, and saying how far it could have moved your
number, is a finding.

## Safe practice is marked too, by watching

Working safely is not a favour you do me. It is
[[A1.5|an expectation of this course]] in its own right, so it is marked like
anything else in the curriculum — and it is the one part of a report mark
that never appears in the report.

What it looks like at the bench: goggles on before the first bottle is
opened, [[WHMIS and Lab Safety]] symbols read before a container is used, and
the disposal step carried out the way the investigation page writes it rather
than improvised at the sink. That is knowledge of safe practice, applied. The
rest of the [[Safety Contract]] is how this room behaves, and it belongs to
your learning skills, not to your percentage.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

These seven are the steadiest evidence in the course, and they are also the
cheapest place to collect the other two kinds, because each one gives you a
bench day and a writing day one period apart. Unit 4, Days 4 and 5 are the
pair to use: the physics is clean enough that what you hear is about method
rather than about the content.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 4, the Ohm's law investigation at the bench
  Watch for: what happens to the reading that does not fit. Somewhere between
  4.0 and 8.0 V one bench will get a number off the line, and what they do in
  the next thirty seconds is the whole of it. A reading that was taken again,
  a reading that was nudged towards the line, and a reading that was always
  correct all look exactly alike in a finished table of five rows.
  Going well: the supply reset, the reading repeated, and both numbers left in
  the notebook — which is what Your Lab Notebook asks for, so where they do it
  you are corroborating a record rather than replacing it.
  Stuck: the pen hovering, and a number that fits appearing without the
  apparatus being touched.
  Record: three columns on your seating plan — repeated it, kept it as it was,
  or nothing off the line yet. One circuit of the room. That is the
  observations half of A1.2 watched, and it is the half a clean table hides.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 5, while they write that report up
  You are in the room for the whole period and the graph is on the desk in
  front of them, so this costs you the walk you were making anyway. Do not
  ask about the slope or the intercept: the investigation page asks both, and
  the agenda spends an item on the gradient, so those are spent.
  Ask: "If I gave you this same resistor with the marking scratched off,
  which two of your five rows would you throw away before working out its
  value — and why those two?"
  Then: "Your percent difference — is that number telling you about the
  resistor, or about the meters?"
  A strong answer names a specific row and a reason that lives in the method:
  the 2.0 V row, where the last digit on the ammeter is a large fraction of
  the whole reading, or the 10.0 V row on a bench that stopped switching off
  between readings — which is exactly why the procedure says to.
  That is A1.2 heard rather than read — the experimentation process, and the
  judgement about which observations the findings can rest on. A tidy table
  of five rows shows you none of it.
  Record: three columns on the seating plan — named a row with a reason,
  named a row with no reason, or neither. Carry the third column into the
  Unit 4, Day 11 write-up and see who has moved.

The product evidence is the seven reports themselves, each handed in at the
end of the period it was written in.
