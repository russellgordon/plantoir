> [!abstract] At a glance
> Individual · **one period**, written, on Unit 3, Day 1 · no notes ·
> everything from the ecosystems unit, weighted the way we spent the time

This is the only test in the course, and it is here on purpose. The
[[Final Examination]] in January is three hours long and, for most of you, the
first examination you will ever have written. Meeting that format once, in
seventy-five minutes, on one unit you have just finished, is a much better
introduction than meeting it cold at the end.

## What is on it

| Part | Roughly | What it asks you to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A. Systems | 25% | Read a food web; say what happens upstream and downstream of a change |
| B. Cycles | 30% | Trace carbon, water, or nitrogen through a real place, with the step that returns it |
| C. Human impact | 25% | Explain what an activity does to a system, using a mechanism rather than a word like "pollution" |
| D. Climate evidence | 20% | Read a graph honestly: what it shows, what it does not, and what would change your reading |

## Success criteria

| Quality | What that looks like on the paper |
| --- | --- |
| Explaining rather than naming | "It is a chemical change" earns little; the evidence that makes it one earns the marks. The same is true of every part of this paper |
| Claims with their evidence attached | Every "because" points at something — a number off the graph, an arrow on the web, a step in the cycle |
| Diagrams that carry information | Arrows labelled with what moves along them, not just drawn. An unlabelled cycle is a picture of a circle |
| Honest limits | Where a graph or a food web does not support the claim you were asked about, saying so is the right answer, not a dodged one |

## How to prepare

1. **Draw all three cycles from memory**, then check them. The arrow you
   cannot draw is the one that will be on the paper.
2. **Redo the questions you got wrong** on the Unit 2 checkpoint. That
   revision list is what the class before this is for.
3. **Read one climate graph you have not seen** and write two sentences about
   what it does not show. [[Interpreting Climate Graphs]] has more of them.
4. The Concepts pages for this unit are the study guide. Nothing on this paper
   comes from anywhere else.

> [!tip] In the period
> Budget by the weightings above. Where a question asks you to explain, write
> the mechanism even if you are unsure of the name — a described process with
> the wrong label earns far more than a right label with nothing under it.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

A test is a product, and this one arrives as a stack of paper on the day the
next unit begins. Both of the other two kinds are available in the two classes
before it, and they are the two classes where finding out is still useful.

OBSERVE — Unit 2, Day 18, the retrieval warm-up at the start
  They are drawing a cycle from memory with no notes, which is as close to the
  paper as anything gets.
  Watch for: where the pen stops. Everybody can start the carbon cycle at a
  plant. The arrow people cannot draw is the one that comes back out of the
  deep store, or the one that returns nitrogen to the air, and the pause before
  it is visible from across the room. A marked script tells you the answer was
  wrong; it cannot tell you which arrows they were sure of and which they put
  down hoping.
  Going well: a gap left blank with a question mark beside it, and the rest of
  the diagram carried on around it.
  Stuck: a complete, confident-looking cycle with an arrow that goes nowhere
  real — usually straight from the atmosphere into the animal.
  Record: three columns down your day plan — left the gap, filled the gap
  wrongly, or drew it whole. The middle column is who to sit beside tomorrow,
  and it is exactly the group whose test score will surprise you otherwise.

TALK — Unit 2, Day 19, the first fifteen minutes, while they work their
revision lists
  You are circulating anyway and their own list of failures is open in front of
  them, which is the cheapest conversation of the unit. Do not ask where the
  carbon in this room is — that was Day 9's warm-up and they have said it once
  already.
  Ask: "Two ponds, same size, same town. One has fifteen kinds of living thing
  in it and the other has three. Which one comes through a hot summer, and
  why?"
  Then: "If we paved the field outside tomorrow, which of the three cycles
  notices first?"
  A strong first answer treats variety as insurance and can say what the
  fifteenth species is for — that is B2.4 heard, biodiversity attached to
  sustainability rather than recited as a definition. A strong second answer
  names the water cycle and reaches for infiltration and runoff rather than
  saying "pollution"; that is B2.5, an effect of a human activity explained by
  a mechanism. Both are questions a written answer can bluff and a spoken one
  cannot.
  Record: two ticks per student in the margin. The ones with neither tick are
  the list you take into tomorrow, when it is still worth something.

The product evidence is the paper itself, written on Unit 3, Day 1.
