> [!abstract] At a glance
> The last class of the semester · benches set up as stations · a
> five-minute demonstration of [[The Engineering Project]] and a defence
> that reaches back across the whole course, plus written critique of
> three other projects · journals in at the end

## What happens

The shop becomes a floor of stations. Every project is set up and
powered, its documentation open beside it, and the room moves in
rotation: you demonstrate, then you visit, then you demonstrate again to
somebody new. Guests come through — other classes, teachers, sometimes
families and people who work in the field.

You are not presenting a poster. You are standing beside a working thing
you built, answering questions from people who did not build it, which is
the situation every technologist eventually spends their career in.

## Your demonstration

Five minutes of demonstration, then the defence. Four parts, in this
order:

1. **The working run.** State the one-sentence job, then do it. Not a
   video of it — it, in front of them.
2. **The measured claim.** One specific, testable statement about your
   device, with the data on the bench: how many trials, what the spread
   was, what instrument you measured it with.
3. **The honest failure mode.** Show one way it fails or once failed,
   and walk the room through how you found the cause. A project with no
   failure to describe has either not been tested or is not being
   described honestly, and the room can tell which.
4. **The defence.** Then questions, and two of them are guaranteed.
   You will be asked to take one component value in your device and
   derive it out loud, from the law it came from, with the numbers you
   actually used. And you will be asked to follow your own schematic
   aloud in front of somebody who has never seen it — sensor to
   actuator, node by node, no notes.

The rest of the questions are not confined to your own project, and that
is the point of this being the final evaluation rather than one more
demonstration. Anything from the semester is fair: what your latch would
do at power-up, what happens on a machine between the power switch and
the login screen, which port on your client's switch served the printer.
That is what the last three classes review, and it is why they come
immediately before today.

"I don't know" is a perfectly good answer when it is followed by "here
is how I would find out".

## Your critique

For three projects that are not yours, you write:

- [ ] **One question** — something you genuinely want to know, that
      cannot be answered yes or no.
- [ ] **One commendation** — a specific decision that was well made, and
      why it was well made. Not "cool project".
- [ ] **One recommendation** — a next step you would take, with a
      reason. Kind, specific, and useful is the standard; all three at
      once, or it does not count.

Critique sheets go to the builders at the end, and they matter: several
of them will end up quoted in [[Final Reflection]]. What they are not is
a mark. Nothing a classmate writes about your project moves your
percentage — see [[How Marks Work]] — and what *is* marked here is the
quality of the three critiques you write, which is your own work and
nobody else's.

> [!info] This is the thirty per cent
> Today is the final evaluation for this course: thirty per cent of your
> mark, on one day, standing beside something you made. The other
> seventy is already in — five jobs across the semester. Bring the
> device, the documentation and the journal; everything else you need
> you have been assembling since September.

> [!example]- What a useful recommendation sounds like
> **Not useful:** "Maybe add more features." **Useful:** "Your sensor
> reading jumps around by about fifty counts between samples. Averaging
> ten readings before you compare against the threshold would settle
> it, and it would cost you almost nothing in response time." The
> second one names what was observed, proposes a specific change, and
> says what it would cost — which is the shape of every good
> engineering recommendation you will ever give or receive.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like at your station |
| --- | --- |
| A demonstration that lands | The job is stated, then done, in front of the visitor |
| A claim backed by data | Numbers, trials, and instrument all present on the bench |
| Honesty about limits | The failure mode is shown and its diagnosis explained |
| Answers under questioning | Any builder can trace the whole chain, unprompted |
| Values you can still defend | One component value derived on the spot, from the law and the numbers that chose it |
| A drawing you can read aloud | You follow your own schematic for a stranger, sensor to actuator, without notes |
| Documentation in reach | Schematic, code, and build log open and readable |
| A reflection that reads the whole record | The final reflection is in the journal, and each claim in it points at a dated entry |
| Critique that is useful | Specific, reasoned, and kind — all three, three times |
| A professional station | Bench tidy, cables managed, nothing energised unattended |

## Closing the semester

Journals come in today. [[Journal Checklist]] is your last look before
they leave your hands, [[Showing Growth]] is the entry that puts
September beside January, and [[Final Reflection]] is the one that
answers what you would build next. The last thing that happens in this
room is the same thing that happened on the first day: tools away,
benches clear, one final log in your [[Tech Journal]].

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

This one is awkward and it is worth saying why. On showcase day you are
running a room with guests in it, so the observation you would most like
to make is the one you have least chance of making. Take it the period
before instead — the evidence is better there anyway.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 27, during the rehearsal on the next bench
  The agenda tells everybody to rehearse, so doing it proves nothing.
  What it does not tell them is what a rehearsal is FOR, and that is the
  whole measurement: a rehearsal used as a test, or performed as a
  recital.
  Watch for: what happens to the listener's confusion. Does the builder
  notice the moment their neighbour stops following, ask about it, write
  it down, and change something before the period ends — or talk
  through, collect a nod, and pack up unchanged?
  Going well: a rehearsal gets interrupted by its own builder; something
  in the demonstration is reordered afterwards; the failure mode is
  chosen and practised rather than left to happen on the day.
  Stuck: the rehearsal runs once, straight through, and nothing moves
  after it.
  Record: class list, three columns — tested it, performed it, skipped
  it. That column is D3.5's reliability and initiative caught doing
  something, and on showcase day a polished station tells you nothing
  about how it got polished.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 23, the conference on that agenda, taken bench by
bench while the class searches for routes and programmes
  Ask: "If the person running that programme asked to see your work on
  Monday, what would you put in front of them — and where is it right
  now?"
  Then: "Hand it to me and let me open it anywhere. Before I look —
  what is on that page, and will it make sense to somebody who was not
  there?"
  And the one that gets the rest of D3.6: "Why keep it ready in
  advance, rather than putting it together the week somebody asks?"
  A strong answer to the first names specific pieces — a schematic, a
  photograph of a board, the trial data — rather than gesturing at the
  whole journal. The second is a prediction they can be wrong about in
  front of you, which is the useful part: a student who keeps the thing
  knows roughly what any page holds, and a student who does not finds
  out at the same moment you do. The third wants an opportunity that
  will not wait, not "so it is not rushed". D3.6 has two
  clauses, maintaining and explaining why it matters for getting on in a
  career, and a collected journal evidences only the first.
  If the student names a non-traditional route, the follow-up worth
  asking is who they would approach and what support exists for them,
  which is D3.3.
  Record: one line per student in your day plan; the useful mark is
  whether they could name pieces or only the folder.

The product evidence is the demonstration, the three critique sheets,
and the journal handed in at the end of the last class.
