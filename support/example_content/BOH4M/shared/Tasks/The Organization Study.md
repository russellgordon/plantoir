**Presented in the third week of Unit 1.** Worked in pairs, marked
individually.

You will study one real organization closely enough to say how it is put
together and how it is managed — and then say it out loud in eight
minutes.

## Choose an organization you can actually reach

A local business, a not-for-profit, a hospital department, a municipal
service, a farm co-operative, a school board department. It must be one
where you can get past the marketing: an interview, a site visit, an
annual report, or someone who works there and will talk to you.

Global brands are the tempting choice and usually the worse one. You will
find plenty written and nothing you can verify.

## What to produce

| Part | What it must contain |
| --- | --- |
| The structure | Purpose, division of labour, hierarchy of authority, with evidence |
| The levels | Who works at each level, and what their time horizon looks like |
| The theory | Which schools of management thought this organization's practices actually reflect |
| The evidence | Where each claim came from, named on the slide |
| The presentation | Eight minutes, both partners speaking, one clear recommendation at the end |

## What is marked as yours

You research in pairs, because two people can reach twice as many
sources. There is no shared mark. Yours is made from three things:

- **The slides you built.** Put your initials in the corner of each one
  you made, and deliver those slides in the eight minutes. Four minutes
  each, roughly, on the part you actually did the work on.
- **What you say at the checkpoint conference** on the working period in
  the second week — you answer for your own half of the evidence.
- **The questions from the class**, which come to whoever built the
  slide being asked about.

Split the organization, not the labour: one of you on structure and
levels, the other on theory and evidence, works far better than one of
you researching and the other making slides.

## How it is marked

- **Accuracy** — the structure you describe is the one that exists, not
  the one on the website.
- **Analysis** — you explain *why* it is arranged this way, and what it
  costs.
- **Evidence** — every claim traceable; an interview quoted properly.
- **Communication** — the eight minutes work as a presentation, using
  what is on [[Presenting to a Room]].

> [!warning] The org chart is not the organization
> Ask who actually decides. In most organizations at least one important
> decision is made by someone the chart does not put in charge of it, and
> finding that is worth more than a tidy diagram.

Build the deck with [[Building a Deck That Works]], and use
[[The Tools of the Trade]] to keep your evidence somewhere you can find
it again.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 1, Day 10, the working period on the study's evidence
  Watch for: what a pair does when two sources disagree — when the
  website's chart and the person they interviewed describe different
  reporting lines. Do they go back and ask a third person, or does one
  version quietly become the slide? The deck shows you which version they
  landed on, and your Accuracy criterion judges that; what only this
  period shows is whether they knew there were two. A pair that never
  noticed and a pair that chose well produce the same slide. That is A1.1
  (identify the characteristics of an organization — e.g., purpose,
  division of labour, hierarchy of authority), where the identifying is
  the whole difficulty, because two sources are describing two different
  hierarchies and only one of them is the one people work in.
  Going well: a pair with two accounts open beside each other, arguing
  about which one describes what happens on a Tuesday.
  Stuck: the annual report is the only thing on the screen and the
  interview notes have not been opened since they were taken.
  Record: initials on the seating plan, one column, tick or dash. The
  conferences are running in the same period, so do this on the way
  round.

TALK — Unit 1, Day 10, the five-minute conferences already on that
  agenda, one per pair
  Ask each partner separately, not the pair together: "Take the last
  decision there that changed somebody's ordinary working day. What would
  have had to be different for it to have been made a level higher?" Lead
  with that clause, not with which level made it — sorting decisions by
  level was the whole of Day 3 and is the drill the course runs
  constantly, so they will place it without thinking. A strong answer
  says what a level up does not have: the detail, the time, the
  relationship. That is A1.2 (evaluate the different levels of management
  with respect to roles, responsibilities, activities, skills, and
  competencies) heard in conversation, and it is the part the slide
  reduces to a box. Not "who actually decides" either — the task page's
  closing warning prints that one.
  Then: "Which part of this organization does NOT fit the school of
  thought you have named for it?" A strong answer finds one — a
  department run on trust inside a place otherwise run on measurement —
  and says what that costs them. That is A1.3 (evaluate major management
  theories and practices). Do not ask which school it reflects; the task
  page prints that, and they will have the sentence ready.
  Record: one line per student, right then, on the day plan.

The product evidence is the eight-minute presentation on Day 13 and the
slides behind it. Do not try to observe or converse on Day 13 — you are
running the room and taking questions from the class.
