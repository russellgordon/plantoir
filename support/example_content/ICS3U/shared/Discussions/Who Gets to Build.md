Look around this room and then look at who works in software. The two
pictures do not match, and they have not matched for forty years. That
is not a fact of nature — it is the result of a long series of small
moments, and some of them happen in classrooms like this one.

## Before we talk

Read [[Pathways After This Course]], and come with one honest answer to
this: when you pictured "a programmer" at the start of the course, who did you
picture? Nobody is asked to share that answer.

## What we are discussing

1. A student is the only person like them in a computing class. Nothing
   hostile happens. Why might they still leave the subject, and what
   would have to be true for them to stay?
2. Whose problems get solved first when a team all share the same
   background — and what goes unnoticed rather than unsolved?
3. Does "anyone can learn to code" help or hurt? It is true. Is it the
   whole truth?
4. What would you change about how this course runs, if the goal were
   that everybody who is curious at the start of the course is still here at the end of the course?

## Groups that exist for exactly this

Several organisations across Canada work to open the field to students
who are under-represented in it — girls and young women, Black and
Indigenous youth, students with disabilities, and students in rural,
northern, and remote communities. Among the national ones:

- **Actua**, which runs programs through a network of university- and
  college-based members across the country, with dedicated national
  programs for Indigenous youth, Black youth, and girls.
- **Canada Learning Code**, a national non-profit focused on digital
  skills for people currently under-represented in technology.
- **Hackergal**, a Canadian charity running coding events for girls in
  Grades 6 to 9, with a focus on rural, remote, and BIPOC communities.
- **Black Boys Code**, which runs technology workshops for Black youth.

Many of these have been supported through the federal **CanCode**
program. Most run free or low-cost events, several run them online, and
all of them are easier to join than students assume. If one of them
fits you, the request in this discussion is simple: look up when the
next one runs.

> [!note] Why this is on the schedule rather than a poster
> A field's composition is decided by thousands of ordinary decisions —
> who gets the encouragement, whose question gets a real answer, who is
> assumed to already know. Those decisions are made by people in rooms
> like this, which means you are already making them.

Bring your thinking to your [[Code Journal]] afterwards: one thing you
heard that you had not considered, and one thing you would do
differently in a group project because of it.
