Split every one of these in two before you calculate anything: what is
happening horizontally, what is happening vertically, and which quantity
the two halves share. The method is on [[Projectile Motion]]; take
$g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ throughout.

**1.** A ball rolls off a bench $1.2\ \text{m}$ high at
$3.0\ \text{m/s}$. How long is it in the air, and how far from the bench
does it land?

> [!success]- Answer 1
> Vertically: $1.2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(9.8)t^2$, so
> $t = \sqrt{2(1.2)/9.8} = 0.49\ \text{s}$.
> Horizontally: $d_x = (3.0)(0.49) = 1.5\ \text{m}$.

**2.** The same bench, the same ball, launched at $6.0\ \text{m/s}$
instead. Predict the new range before you calculate it.

> [!success]- Answer 2
> The time of flight is unchanged — the vertical motion knows nothing
> about the horizontal speed — so doubling $v_x$ doubles the range:
> $2.9\ \text{m}$.

**3.** A stunt cyclist leaves a ramp horizontally at $12\ \text{m/s}$ and
lands $2.4\ \text{s}$ later. How high was the ramp, and how far did the
cyclist travel horizontally?

> [!success]- Answer 3
> $\Delta d_y = \tfrac{1}{2}(9.8)(2.4)^2 = 28\ \text{m}$;
> $\Delta d_x = (12)(2.4) = 29\ \text{m}$.

**4.** A soccer ball is kicked at $18\ \text{m/s}$ at $35°$ above the
horizontal, from and onto level ground. Find its time of flight and its
range.

> [!success]- Answer 4
> $v_x = 18\cos 35° = 14.7\ \text{m/s}$;
> $v_y = 18\sin 35° = 10.3\ \text{m/s}$.
> Up and back down: $t = 2v_y/g = 2.1\ \text{s}$.
> Range $= (14.7)(2.1) = 31\ \text{m}$.

**5.** Same kick, same speed, at $55°$ instead. What is the range, and
why is that not a coincidence?

> [!success]- Answer 5
> $31\ \text{m}$ again. Angles that add to $90°$ give the same range on
> level ground, because the gain in flight time exactly offsets the loss
> in horizontal speed.

**6.** At the highest point of the $35°$ kick, what are the ball's
velocity and its acceleration?

> [!success]- Answer 6
> Velocity: $14.7\ \text{m/s}$ horizontally — the vertical component is
> zero, the horizontal component never changed.
> Acceleration: $9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ downward, exactly as it was the whole
> flight. A zero velocity component does not mean zero acceleration.

**7.** A rescue aircraft flying level at $60\ \text{m/s}$ releases a
package from $180\ \text{m}$. How far back from the target should the
release happen?

> [!success]- Answer 7
> $t = \sqrt{2(180)/9.8} = 6.1\ \text{s}$, so the release must be
> $(60)(6.1) = 370\ \text{m}$ short of the target. The package keeps the
> aircraft's horizontal velocity, which is why it lands ahead of the
> release point and directly below the aircraft.

**8.** Your measured range for a horizontal launch is 12% shorter than
this model predicts, and your uncertainty is 3%. Name two explanations
worth investigating, and say how you would test one.

> [!success]- Answer 8
> Air resistance (test: repeat with a denser ball of the same diameter —
> the range should get closer to the prediction) and a mis-measured
> launch height or speed (test: re-measure the height to the centre of
> the ball, and time the launch speed directly with a photogate).
