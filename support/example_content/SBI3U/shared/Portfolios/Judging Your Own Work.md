Every task page carries a table of success criteria, published on the day
the task launches. Most people read it once, at launch, and never again —
and then hand in a report that misses two rows they could have fixed in
twenty minutes. This page is the twenty minutes.

We do this together the first time, on somebody else's field study, so
that the move is not new to you when it is your own work being judged.

## The routine

1. Open the task page and put the criteria table beside your work.
2. Go row by row. For each one write **yes**, **not yet**, or **partly** —
   and one sentence of evidence: the page, the figure, the paragraph, the
   thing a reader would actually see. "Yes" with no evidence is a wish.
3. Find your weakest row. Not the easiest to fix — the weakest.
4. Write one sentence saying what you will change, and when.
5. Fix it — in the rest of this period, or in the next one. This is why
   the self-check is never scheduled on the day something is due: a
   judgement you have no time to act on is just a worry.

Steps 2 and 3 are the whole exercise. Naming your weakest row honestly is
harder than any of the fixing, because it means saying the thing you have
been hoping nobody would notice.

> [!example]- One row, judged honestly
> From [[Biodiversity Field Study]], the row called *the rule survived
> the field*: "Where you departed from your own design, you say so and
> say what it cost."
>
> **Not yet.** We wrote the placement rule down on Day 10 and followed it
> at the mown site. At the unmown site we moved two quadrats off bare
> ground because they looked boring, and I have not said so anywhere.
> Weakest row. **Change:** say it in the method, and say what it does to
> the richness figure — next period, before the graph.
>
> That entry is worth more than "looks good to me" on all seven rows, and
> it took less time to write. It is also the kind of honesty a marker
> rewards rather than punishes: an acknowledged flaw, with its cost
> named, is a finding.

## In a lab report

The criteria on [[Writing a Lab Report]] are the ones to use for the
bench write-ups, and two rows are worth checking every single time,
because they are the two that go missing under time pressure:

- **Does the analysis say what the data allows you to claim**, rather
  than what you expected to find?
- **Is every drawing to the standard on [[Biological Drawing]]** — title,
  magnification, ruled label lines that do not cross?

## Where this fits

Your judgement of your own work is not part of your mark, and neither is
a classmate's — see [[How Marks Work]]. That is deliberate, and it cuts
both ways: nothing you write here can cost you anything, which is exactly
what makes it worth writing honestly.

What it does change is the work. A self-check that leads to a fix is the
cheapest mark improvement available in this course. Keep the entries in
your [[Biology Journal]]; by January they are also the clearest record
you have of what you got better at, which is what
[[Portfolio Checklist]] is asking for.

One last thing worth doing: read your judgement out loud to the person
next to you. It catches the rows where you were being generous, and you
will usually hear it yourself before they say anything.
