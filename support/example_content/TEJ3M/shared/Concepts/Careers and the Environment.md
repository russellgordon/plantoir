[[Tech Headlines]] has been running all semester, and by now the pattern
in what people bring in is hard to miss: a new device is announced, and
somewhere further down the same page is a story about what the last one
cost. This page puts those two halves together, because the people who
will decide how the next generation of hardware is made and unmade are
the ones currently learning to solder.

## What the hardware costs

A circuit board is a concentrated pile of materials that were expensive
to obtain and are difficult to separate again.

**Getting the materials.** Copper, tin, gold, tantalum, cobalt, and the
rare-earth elements in magnets and displays are mined, and mining carries
environmental and human costs that are paid a long way from the shop
where the board is assembled. Nothing on your bench is exempt from that
supply chain — it is the substance of the argument in
[[Who Pays for the Hardware]].

**Making the thing.** Manufacturing semiconductors uses very large
volumes of ultrapure water and energy, and board assembly historically
used solvents to wash flux from boards, some of which damaged the ozone
layer and have since been regulated out of use. That change is worth
noticing: it is a case where the industry's environmental cost went down
because rules and chemistry changed together.

**Running it.** Energy consumed doing nothing is the quiet one. A device
left in standby draws a small amount continuously, and small multiplied
by every device in every building is not small — put a number on it
yourself in [[Power Calculations Practice]]. This cuts both ways,
though, and honestly: computerised traffic control, route optimisation,
and building management systems reduce fuel burn and emissions by more
than the computers running them consume. Technology is not uniformly a
cost to the environment, and pretending otherwise makes the real
accounting harder.

**Throwing it away.** Electronic waste is the fastest-growing part of the
municipal waste stream in most developed countries, and it is the part
you least want in landfill: lead in older solder and CRT glass, mercury
in older backlights, cadmium, and brominated flame retardants in the
plastics. Informal recycling — burning insulation off cable to recover
copper, acid-leaching boards for gold — releases exactly those materials
into the people doing the work.

## Reduce, reuse, recycle, applied to hardware

The order of those three words is a ranking, and hardware is where the
ranking bites hardest.

- **Reduce** starts at specification. A machine chosen for the job it
  actually does, rather than for headroom nobody will use, is the largest
  single saving available and the one nobody gets credit for.
- **Reuse** beats recycling by a wide margin, because it keeps the
  embodied energy of manufacture intact. A four-year-old workstation with
  more RAM and a fresh drive is a perfectly good machine for most work,
  and refurbishment charities and school programs move a great many of
  them. This is the ground [[Repair or Replace]] fights over.
- **Recycle** last, and properly. In Ontario the responsibility for
  collecting and recycling electronics sits with the producers who put
  them on the market, which is why retailers and municipal depots accept
  old equipment at no charge to you. A certified recycler documents where
  material goes; an uncertified one may simply export the problem.

> [!question] Where does the machine on your bench go?
> Trace one real device from the drawer: what is it made of, who would
> take it if the school retired it tomorrow, and what would happen to it
> next? The answer is rarely on a poster and is usually findable in
> twenty minutes. Bring what you find to [[Tech Headlines]] — a specific
> answer about a specific machine is worth more than a general opinion
> about e-waste.

## Where this work leads

Computer technology is unusual among trades in that several genuinely
different routes lead into it, and none of them is the "real" one.

| Route | Typical length | Leads to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Industry certification | Weeks to months, often alongside work | Help desk, IT support, network technician roles |
| Apprenticeship | Years, paid, with in-school terms | Electrician, instrumentation and control technician |
| College diploma or advanced diploma | Two to three years | Electronics technician and technologist, network administrator, embedded systems technologist |
| University degree | Four years, often with co-op | Computer engineer, software engineer, systems analyst |
| Degree plus graduate study | Longer | Research, design of the parts everyone else uses |

The honest guidance is this. The roles differ less in prestige than in
what your day contains: how much of it is hands on hardware, how much is
design on paper, how much is with people. A technologist who commissions
control systems and an engineer who designs them both need what you are
learning now, and they need it to different depths.

What every route asks for is the same short list, and you are building it
this semester: reading technical documents accurately, writing so someone
else can act on it, working safely, showing up, and being able to say
what you did and why. Those are the ==Essential Skills and work habits==
the Ontario Skills Passport names, and they are assessed in this course
because employers assess them first.

Some paths are still much less travelled by some groups than others, and
that is a fact about the industry rather than about the work. Mentoring
programs, trade and industry associations, and specialised postsecondary
programs exist specifically to change that; [[The Trades Are Tech]] is
where we talk about who ends up in this room and who does not.

## Career portfolios and technical evidence

Start collecting the evidence now. Your [[Tech Journal]] and the
portfolio it feeds are the artefact every one of these routes asks to
see — circuit diagrams, photographs of builds, a video of something
working, and your own account of what went wrong first. See
[[Final Reflection]] for what a strong one looks like by June.
