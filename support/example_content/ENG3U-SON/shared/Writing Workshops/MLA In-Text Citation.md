Every quotation and every borrowed idea needs to point back to exactly
where it came from. In-text citation is how you do that without breaking
your sentence to explain yourself — the details live in parentheses, right
after the evidence.

## The basic pattern

Author's last name, then the location — no comma between them, and the
period goes *after* the closing parenthesis, not before it:

> When her father dismisses her competence with "just a girl" (Munro 15),
> the narrator hears, for the first time, what she is being sorted into.

If you've already named the author in your sentence, drop the name from
the parentheses and keep only the location:

> Munro writes that the narrator hears, for the first time, what she is
> being sorted into when her father calls her "just a girl" (15).

## What goes inside the parentheses, by source

The "location" isn't always a page number — it depends on what kind of
text you're citing.

| Source type | What goes in the parentheses | Example |
|---|---|---|
| A novel or short story | Author's last name + page number | `(Wagamese 112)` |
| A play, verse-numbered | Author's last name + act, scene, line | `(Shakespeare 3.1.56)` |
| A source with no author named | A short version of the title | `("Boys and Girls" 12)` |
| A source with no page numbers (most media texts) | Author's last name alone, or the title if there's no author | `(Taylor)` |
| A source you're quoting for a second time in the same paragraph | Page or location alone, if the author is still unambiguous | `(15)` |

## Long quotations

A quotation longer than four lines of your own prose gets set off as a
block: no quotation marks, indented from the left margin, with the
citation *after* the final punctuation instead of before it — the one
place the usual "period after the parenthesis" rule flips.
