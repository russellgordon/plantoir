**The final evaluation. Three hours, in the examination period.**
Individual.

Unseen maps, unseen data, and questions that reach across the whole
course.

## What it looks like

You are given a small dossier about a place you have not studied: a
topographic extract, a thematic map, a table of census data, a climate
or hazard record for the place, and two short written sources that
disagree with each other.

You answer in three parts:

1. **Read the evidence** (about 45 minutes). What do the map and the
   data actually show? Describe patterns and trends precisely, with
   figures, and say what the sources leave out.
2. **Explain it** (about 70 minutes). Why is this place like this? Use
   physical geography, resources and industry, population, and land use
   as the evidence requires — a strong answer crosses strands, because
   real places do.
3. **Judge it** (about 45 minutes). A decision is proposed for this
   place. Assess it from more than one perspective — including whose
   quality of life it changes and whose it does not — and recommend.

The remaining time is for reading. Use it — the strongest papers spend
twenty minutes before writing anything.

## What is being examined

| Criterion | What it means here |
| --- | --- |
| Reading evidence | Map and data read accurately, including scale and units |
| Credibility | You notice what a source is for, and who made it |
| Explanation | Physical and human geography connected, not listed |
| Judgement | A recommendation that names its cost, whose well-being it changes, and its uncertainty |
| Communication | Structured and plain under time pressure |

## Preparing for it

The three review periods are built for this: unseen dossiers worked
against the clock, then compared. Bring your fieldwork notes — the
skills you built at [[The Shoreline Study]] and on [[The Land Use Walk]]
are exactly what part 1 asks for, and [[Judging a Source]] is the whole
of the credibility mark.

> [!tip] Answer the question that was asked
> Every year, the most common lost mark is a good essay about the wrong
> thing. Underline the verb — *describe*, *explain*, *assess* — and give
> it what it asked for.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

Say the awkward part first: an examination is product evidence and
nothing else. You cannot watch or talk in an examination room and you
should not try. What follows is where the observation and conversation
evidence for THESE expectations is still available — in the review
days, while it can still change what somebody does on the paper.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 23, the unseen dossier worked against the clock
  Do not watch for whether they read before writing; the page tells them
  to and Day 24 rehearses it, so you would be watching a rehearsed
  behaviour. Watch the table instead. Before two figures are compared,
  do the units and the years get checked — a rate against a count, a
  2016 figure against a 2021 one? On a script an answer that was checked
  and an answer that was lucky are indistinguishable, and only one of
  them will survive a dossier where the trap is set differently.
  Going well: units and years circled or written at the top of the
  answer before any comparison appears.
  Stuck: two figures subtracted that turn out to be from different
  years, or a rate set against a count.
  Record: class list, one column. Keep it — the students in it are the
  ones to reach before Day 24 ends.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 23, while readings are being compared in pairs,
before you take it whole-class
  Ask: "These two sources disagree. Which would you go and check first,
  and what would you be looking for?"
  A strong answer names the thing that would settle it — the date, who
  paid for it, what was actually counted — rather than which source
  sounds more official. That is A1.3, credibility and bias assessed as
  an action rather than as an adjective.
  Then: "Part 3 proposes a decision for this place. What in the physical
  evidence would make that decision harder in twenty years than it is
  today?"
  A strong answer reaches into the climate or hazard record and names
  something moving — a shoreline, a frequency, a growing season — and
  says what it does to the ground the decision sits on. That is E1.2,
  physical processes and climate change bearing on land-use planning,
  and it is the connection a timed script makes last if at all.
  Record: one line per student on the list you already have out.

The product evidence is the examination script itself. Nothing you hear
on Day 23 belongs in the examination mark — the paper is the paper. It
belongs to the seventy, as recent evidence like any other, and to the
twenty minutes you spend with a student before the end of the day.
