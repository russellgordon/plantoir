> [!abstract] At a glance
> Groups of three · scouted through the final unit, presented on the
> last day · invited guests · one exhibit, one model, one panel each

## What you are making

The whole course has been one idea wearing different clothes: find the
function hiding in the world, name its family, and make it speak. The
symposium is where you do that in public.

Your group chooses one real-world relationship worth modelling — a
cooling drink, a bouncing ball, daylight hours across the year, a
savings balance, a growing pattern — and builds an **exhibit**: the
data or situation, the model, and the story of how you chose it. The
catch that makes it a finale: you must audition **two candidate
families** from different units before defending your winner. A
quadratic that lost to an exponential is evidence, not waste — that
judgement call is exactly [[What Makes a Model Good]].

Guests circulate on the day. Every group answers the same two
questions: *why is this the right model?* and *where does it stop
being one?*

## Milestones

- [ ] Phenomenon scouted and approved — real, measurable, and yours
- [ ] Two candidate families auditioned; the comparison recorded
- [ ] Winning model built, with every parameter tied to the situation
- [ ] Panels divided — the audition, the parameter story, the boundary:
      one each, and the author's name on it
- [ ] One question answered with the model — something the raw data
      cannot answer — worked in function notation, with the number it
      gives written down
- [ ] The failure boundary found: where the model stops deserving trust
- [ ] Exhibit assembled; both symposium questions rehearsed out loud

The folded callout below stays folded until you are stuck — that is
what folding is for.

> [!question]- If your group cannot choose a phenomenon
> Pick the one you can measure by tomorrow. A modest phenomenon with
> real data beats a spectacular one with none, and the audition of two
> families matters more than the fame of the winner.

## How it is assessed

The conversation is the assessment. Per [[How Marks Work]], this and
the [[Final Examination]] together are the final thirty per cent of the
course, in two deliberately different rooms. I listen for reasoning:
the audition, the parameter story, and the failure boundary. Speed,
polish, and poster quality are not criteria. Guests ask whatever they
like, and **nothing a guest says is part of anybody's mark** — the
judgement is mine, from evidence.

You build the exhibit in threes and you are marked one at a time. The
phenomenon, the audition and the winning model belong to the group.
**Yours alone** is one of the three panels — the audition, the
parameter story, or the boundary — written by you and carrying your
name, decided at the end of the audition period and written on the last
working period; and the answer you give me when I reach your exhibit
with one question about that panel.
Afterwards, [[Final Reflection]] asks what the symposium showed you
about your own growth, and your [[Math Journal]] carries the record.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like at your exhibit |
| --- | --- |
| A real phenomenon | Data or a situation your group can vouch for |
| A genuine audition | Two families tried; the loser's failure shown |
| Parameters that speak | Every letter tied to the world, not the page |
| A model that earns its keep | One question the raw data cannot answer, worked through your model in function notation, and the number it gives |
| A known boundary | You name where the model fails before guests ask |
| Threads across units | The exhibit cites ideas from more than one unit |
| A panel with your name on it | One of the three panels written by you, and defended when I ask about it on the day |

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

Day 21 is not a gathering day. Guests are circulating, you are hosting, and the
single question you put to each student about their own panel is as much as
that room will carry. Everything below belongs to the two working periods
before it.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 19, the audition working period

  Watch whether the second family gets the same care as the first. A recorded
  comparison satisfies "A genuine audition" whether the loser was fitted
  properly and then beaten, or nominated in four minutes so that a comparison
  would exist at all. Only the period tells you which, and the difference is
  the whole argument of the task.

  Going well: two fits attempted at comparable effort, and a group that looks
  genuinely unsure for a while about which one is winning.

  Stuck: the winner declared inside five minutes, and the loser drawn
  afterwards in order to lose.

  Record: two ticks or one against each group on your day plan, plus the name
  of the family they reached for first.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 20, while the panels are written and groups question each
other

  Take each student aside for a minute. Ask: "If the family that lost had won
  instead, which of your parameters would have meant nothing at all?"

  Then, pointing at their boundary: "Where did that number come from — did you
  compute it, read it off the graph, or decide it?"

  The first is the audition run backwards, and it cannot be rehearsed the way
  the two symposium questions can, because it is about the model they did not
  build. A strong answer names a parameter and says what the losing family had
  no slot for — a common ratio has nowhere to put a phase shift; a vertex has
  nothing to say about a doubling time. Where the group's winner or loser is a
  sequence or a series you are hearing C2.4, and where it is a quadratic you
  are hearing A2.3, in both cases the "arising from real-world applications"
  half that a wall display never shows: whether the letters were chosen or
  inherited. Where the group's two families are neither, this is still the
  best evidence of the modelling you will get, and it is the criteria table it
  belongs to rather than a code.

  On the second, the answer worth having is an admission with a method
  attached: "we decided it, and here is what we would measure to check" beats
  a confident figure nobody in the group can source. A panel states a
  boundary. It never says which of the three it was.

  Record: down your class list, student by student. A note against the group's
  name would defeat the purpose of asking them separately.

The product evidence is the exhibit on Day 21 and the panel carrying that
student's name.
