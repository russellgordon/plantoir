Pour an acid into a base and the mixture gets warm. You have known that
since Grade 11 and it has always been a footnote — a thing that happens
while the reaction you cared about goes on.

Today the warmth **is** the measurement. You are going to put a number
on it, in kilojoules per mole, and then spend the rest of the unit
finding out what that number is a statement about.

## What you are trying to find out

Energy is not created in the beaker. If the solution got hotter, the
energy came out of the chemical system, and if you can measure how much
went into the water you have measured how much came out of the
reaction.

That is the whole idea, and it needs one relationship:

$$Q = mc\Delta T$$

where $m$ is the mass of the **solution** being warmed, $c$ is its
specific heat capacity, and $\Delta T$ is the temperature change. Take
$c = 4.18\ \text{J/(g}\cdot^\circ\text{C)}$ for dilute aqueous
solutions, which is the value for water and is close enough for today.
Where that relationship comes from, and why the mass and the heat
capacity both belong in it, is next class's job.

Two things you are really testing:

1. **Can you measure a heat of reaction at all**, with a polystyrene
   cup, a thermometer, and a balance?
2. **Does it depend on how much you use?** Run the reaction at two
   different scales. If the energy released doubles when the amount
   doubles, but the energy **per mole** stays put, then you have found a
   quantity that belongs to the reaction rather than to your afternoon.

That second one is the real question of the day, and it is the reason
the design asks you to change the scale rather than repeat the same
thing three times.

## What you have to work with

- **Hydrochloric acid, 1.0 mol/L**, and **sodium hydroxide solution,
  1.0 mol/L**. Both dilute, both at the concentrations on the
  containers, and neither of them is to be made stronger.
- **Two nested polystyrene cups** in a beaker for support, with a lid
  that has a hole for the thermometer.
- **A thermometer or temperature probe.** A probe with a logger is much
  better, and the reason is in the analysis section.
- Graduated cylinders, a stirring rod, a balance, a stopwatch.

The reaction is:

$$\ce{HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) -> NaCl(aq) + H2O(l)}$$

Your design decisions, written and justified **before** the lab:

- **The volumes you will use, at two different scales.** Choose them so
  that the acid and the base contain equal numbers of moles — otherwise
  one is in excess, and the excess is sitting there absorbing heat
  without having reacted. Show the calculation that makes them equal.
- **Which scale you run first**, and why. There is a reason to run the
  small one first and a reason to run the large one first. Pick one and
  defend it.
- **What you will use for $m$.** The mass of the acid? The base? Both
  together? This decision changes your answer and most people get it
  wrong by not noticing they made a decision at all. State it and
  justify it.
- **How you will read the maximum temperature.** The mixture warms fast
  and then cools slowly, so the highest reading you catch depends
  entirely on how often you look. Decide your reading interval before
  you start, and use the same interval for every trial.
- **How many trials at each scale**, and what agreement you will accept.
- **What you will do about the cup itself.** The polystyrene, the lid,
  and the thermometer all warm up, and none of that energy went into the
  water you are counting. Decide now whether you will ignore it, and be
  ready to say which way ignoring it pushes your result.

> [!danger] Sodium hydroxide, dilution heat, and one thing you will not do today
> - **Sodium hydroxide is the reagent that does not warn you.** Dilute
>   acid stings, so a splash announces itself and you wash it off.
>   Sodium hydroxide feels soapy or slippery instead — that sensation is
>   the solution attacking the fats in your skin — and it keeps working
>   while it does not hurt. A base burn of the same strength goes
>   **deeper** than an acid burn, precisely because nothing made you pull
>   away. **Rinse any suspected contact for at least 15 minutes under
>   running water**, whether or not it hurts, and tell me. In an eye it
>   is an emergency: eyewash at once, hold the lid open, somebody else
>   comes for me while you stay at the eyewash.
> - **Add acid to water, never water to acid.** You are not diluting
>   anything today if you use the solutions as supplied — and if you
>   decide you want a lower concentration, this rule is why you talk to
>   me first. Dilution releases heat. With the water already there the
>   heat spreads through a large volume; the other way round it
>   concentrates in a few drops that can boil and spit acid back out at
>   you.
> - **Eye protection on from the first move to the last of the cleanup**,
>   including while a partner finishes and including while you wash up.
> - **Add the second solution steadily, with the lid on and the
>   thermometer in place.** Pouring from height splashes, and a splash
>   from a cup you are leaning over goes upward.
> - **The thermometer is not a stirring rod.** A bulb cracked against
>   the bottom of a cup puts glass and, in older thermometers, its
>   filling into your reaction.
> - **Neither solution goes back into stock**, and the neutralised
>   mixture goes where I tell you — check its pH first, because a
>   mixture you thought was neutral often is not.
> - **You are not burning anything today.** Calorimetry with a flame —
>   fuel under a can of water — puts an open flame, a hot metal surface,
>   and a flammable liquid within centimetres of each other. When this
>   course does that measurement, **I do it as a demonstration** and you
>   record from your seat. Every calorimetry experiment you run with your
>   own hands in this course is in a polystyrene cup, with solutions, at
>   around room temperature.
> - **Report every incident immediately**, however small, including a
>   splash you dealt with yourself.

## The prediction you write first

Before you pour anything:

1. **The sign of $\Delta T$**, and your reason in one sentence.
2. **The magnitude of $\Delta T$** for your smaller scale, in degrees
   Celsius, to the nearest degree. Commit to a number.
3. **What you expect to happen to $\Delta T$ when you double the
   scale** — bigger, smaller, or the same — and separately, what you
   expect to happen to the **energy per mole**. These two answers are
   not the same and the difference between them is the point of the
   lab.
4. **How many significant figures your final answer will be entitled
   to**, and which measurement is the bottleneck. Work it out now. It
   is almost never the one people guess.

Both solutions start at the same temperature, and you should check that
rather than assume it — a bottle taken from a cupboard near a window is
not at the same temperature as one from the middle of the room. If they
differ, record both and use the mean as your initial temperature, and
say that you did.

Temperatures to the resolution your instrument actually has. A glass
thermometer graduated in 1 °C gives you readings to 0.5 °C at best, and
writing 21.37 °C from one is a claim you cannot support. See
[[Measuring Well]].

> [!example]- The shape of the calculation, with illustrative numbers
> These are not your results. They show the route, and the units carried
> at every step.
>
> Suppose 50.0 mL of 1.00 mol/L hydrochloric acid was mixed with
> 50.0 mL of 1.00 mol/L sodium hydroxide, both starting at 21.2 °C, and
> the highest temperature reached was 27.9 °C.
>
> Temperature change: $\Delta T = 27.9 - 21.2 = 6.7\ ^\circ\text{C}$
>
> Mass of solution warmed, taking the density of these dilute solutions
> as 1.00 g/mL: $m = 50.0 + 50.0 = 100.0\ \text{g}$ — **both** volumes,
> because the reaction warmed all of the liquid in the cup and not just
> the half you poured second.
>
> Heat absorbed by the solution:
> $Q = mc\Delta T = (100.0\ \text{g})(4.18\ \text{J/(g}\cdot^\circ\text{C)})(6.7\ ^\circ\text{C}) = 2801\ \text{J} = 2.80\ \text{kJ}$
>
> Moles of water formed. The acid supplies
> $n = cV = (1.00\ \text{mol/L})(0.0500\ \text{L}) = 0.0500\ \text{mol}$
> of $\ce{H+}$, the base supplies the same amount of
> $\ce{OH-}$, and the equation is one to one, so 0.0500 mol of water
> is formed.
>
> Energy per mole:
> $\frac{2.80\ \text{kJ}}{0.0500\ \text{mol}} = 56.0\ \text{kJ/mol}$
>
> The solution **gained** that energy, so the reaction **lost** it, and
> the sign flips: $\Delta H = -56\ \text{kJ/mol}$.
>
> **Two significant figures, and no more.** The volumes were given to
> three and the concentrations to three, but $\Delta T = 6.7\ ^\circ$C
> has only two, and a subtraction of two similar numbers is the usual
> place precision goes to die in calorimetry. Work out where your
> bottleneck is before you write the answer down, not after.

## What to bring to the consolidation

- Your prediction, dated before the data.
- Your table, both scales, every trial including any you would rather
  not show.
- **Your $\Delta H$ in kJ/mol, with a sign**, calculated separately for
  each scale.
- **The comparison between scales.** Did the total energy roughly
  double? Did the energy per mole stay put? State both as numbers, and
  state whether the difference between your two per-mole values is
  larger or smaller than the spread within each scale. Those are
  different questions.
- **The class spread**, plotted. Everybody's per-mole value on one
  number line. Look at whether the scatter is centred on something or
  piled up to one side of it — the second pattern means the whole class
  shares a systematic error, and you should be able to name the
  technique step responsible.
- **The comparison with the data booklet.** Look up the accepted value
  for the enthalpy of neutralisation of a strong acid with a strong
  base, and state the difference as a percentage. Then answer the
  harder question: is that difference larger than your uncertainty?

## What you should not claim

- **You did not measure the heat of the reaction. You measured the heat
  that reached the water.** Everything else the reaction warmed — the
  cups, the lid, the thermometer, the stirring rod, the air above the
  liquid — is energy that left the chemical system and never got
  counted. That missing energy makes your measured $\Delta T$ **too
  small**, which makes $|\Delta H|$ **too small**, which means your
  result is almost certainly **less negative** than the accepted value.
  If your class comes out low, this is why, and it is a limitation of
  the apparatus rather than a mistake in your technique.
- **Heat loss to the room pushes the same way.** From the moment mixing
  starts, the cup is losing energy to a cooler room, so the highest
  temperature you catch is already below the temperature the mixture
  would have reached if the reaction had been instant and the cup
  perfect. Same direction, same conclusion: an underestimate.
- **A larger $\Delta T$ is not a bigger reaction.** If your small-scale
  run gave a larger $\Delta T$ than your large-scale run, that is not
  evidence about the chemistry — it is evidence that the same energy per
  mole is warming a smaller mass. This is exactly the confusion the
  two-scale design exists to expose, so do not import it into your
  conclusion.
- **Using only one of the two volumes for $m$ is a real error with a
  known direction.** Halving the mass halves $Q$ and halves your
  $|\Delta H|$. If your value is close to half the class's, check this
  first.
- **You did not show that $c = 4.18$ for your solution.** You assumed
  it, and it is the value for pure water. A 1.0 mol/L salt solution has
  a slightly lower specific heat capacity and a slightly higher density,
  so both assumptions are approximations. State them as assumptions —
  they are small compared with your heat loss, and saying so is better
  than pretending you did not make them.
- **Two trials at each scale is not a spread.** It tells you that you
  can repeat yourself once. Do not quote a mean of two values to three
  figures.
