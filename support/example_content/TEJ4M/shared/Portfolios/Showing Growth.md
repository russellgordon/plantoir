By mid-course your [[Tech Journal]] stops being only a log and becomes
something rarer: **evidence**. The person who wrote your first entries
does not quite exist any more — and because that person wrote honestly,
and wrote down numbers and decisions, you can prove it. Put early-you
and current-you side by side and let the difference speak.

For some of you the record now runs three years. Use it. A quotation
from a Grade 10 entry beside one from last week is the most persuasive
thing you will ever write about yourself, and it costs you nothing but
the reading.

## Growth is a claim plus evidence, like any measurement

"I got better at electronics" is a claim, and on its own it is worth
about as much as "the voltage was fine". A service log never says
"fixed it somehow" — it records the symptom, the tests, the values, the
part replaced, and the verification. Growth writing meets the same
standard: every claim backed by two dated entries of your own, quoted
rather than summarised.

> In my first-week entry I wrote: "I am fast at building and slow at
> deciding. Give me a requirement and I freeze." Last week I wrote
> half a page working out whether to run the sensor rail from the same
> regulator as the motor, decided against it because the motor's
> current steps would move my reference, and then measured 180 mV of
> dip on the shared rail to check I was right. I did not freeze. I
> went and got a number.

The claim is *choosing under uncertainty stopped being frightening*.
The evidence is two moments, months apart, both on the record, one of
them containing a measurement. Nobody can argue with that — including
the part of you that suspects you have not changed.

## Where Grade 12 growth actually shows

Last year the visible growth was in your hands: instrument discipline,
technique, speed. Some of that is still happening and it still counts.
But the growth that matters this year lives in the parts of an entry
described in [[Tech Journal#The decision rule]], and it looks like
this:

- **The margin moved.** Early entries design to the rating. Later
  entries design to half of it and say why.
- **The alternative appeared.** Early decisions have one option in
  them. Later ones have two, and a sentence about what the winner
  cost.
- **The condition got attached.** Early measurements are numbers.
  Later ones are numbers with a temperature, a load state, and a
  supply setting.
- **The question changed.** Early entries ask "why does it not work?"
  Later ones ask "under what conditions would this stop working?" —
  which is the question the whole course is pointed at.

## How to cite yourself

Treat your earlier entries the way a report treats sources.

- **Date it.** "In my entry from the second week…" The distance is
  part of the evidence.
- **Quote a phrase, not a page.** Your words then carry more truth than
  your summary now. One sentence from [[Your First Entry]] says more
  than a paragraph of "I was new to designing things."
- **Then show the now.** A recent moment, just as specific, that the
  earlier version of you could not have managed. Include the numbers
  and the rejected alternative; they are what make it undeniable.

## Growth is not always a bigger build

Some of it is a new noticing. Hearing a supply drop into current limit
and knowing immediately what your circuit just did. Looking at a
schematic and seeing the missing flyback diode before you see the
parts. Reading a datasheet's derating curve and feeling the small
flinch that stops you designing at the fence. Catching, in
[[Spot the Hazard]], the hazard that consists of something being
*absent*. Being asked "why that part?" and having an answer that was
written down weeks earlier.

None of that photographs well and none of it looks like an achievement
from the outside. It is growth all the same, and your early entries
are the proof it was not always there — which is the entire reason we
bother writing them down.
