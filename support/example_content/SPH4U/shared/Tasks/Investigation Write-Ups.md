> [!abstract] At a glance
> **Format:** five write-ups, Unit 1 to Unit 4. One period each, written
> here, collected at the bell. **You write yours; the data behind it is
> your bench's.** **Assessed on:** Thinking and Investigation first,
> Communication throughout, and Knowledge wherever the physics has to be
> explained rather than computed.

The other four things on your seventy per cent are big, slow and far
apart: you start one, and the better part of a month later you start
another. These are
the opposite. Five times this semester you will walk out of a bench
period, sit down the next day with your own numbers, and have a marked
piece of work finished by the bell. Nothing else in the seventy per cent
measures what you can do in one sitting, and nothing else shows a report
card that the physicist you are at the end is not the one you were at
the start.

## Which five, and the day each is written

| Write-up | Investigation | What that write-up has to answer | Written in class on |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| One | [[The Flying Pig]] | Two independent routes to one speed — the cone's geometry, and the radius with the period — reconciled, and what the agreement or the gap says about how speed, radius and period are tied together | Unit 1, Day 11 |
| Two | [[Collisions in One Dimension]] | Total momentum and total kinetic energy before and after all four interactions, each one placed as elastic or inelastic from those numbers rather than from how it looked, the explosion shown to start and finish at zero, and the impulse on each cart worked out as its own change in momentum | Unit 2, Day 9 |
| Three | [[Collisions in Two Dimensions (Lab)]] | Momentum conserved component by component, the right-angle result for equal masses derived rather than asserted, and the energy checked to decide whether the collision was elastic | Unit 2, Day 11 |
| Four | [[Mapping an Electric Field]] | Equipotential spacing read as field strength, field lines drawn from the map rather than remembered, and the parallel-plate pattern set against the two-point one | Unit 3, Day 6 |
| Five | [[Double Slit with a Laser]] | A wavelength extracted from fringe spacing, slit separation and screen distance, and the predicted effect of halving the slit separation tested against what the screen did | Unit 4, Day 4 |

The third column is not decoration. It is the question each write-up is
marked on answering, and it is why one set of rows can judge five
different investigations: the rows say how an argument has to be built,
and that column says what this one has to be about.

The first one is a **full report** in the shape set out on
[[Writing a Lab Report]] — purpose through conclusion, because you should
write one complete one before you are allowed to skip the parts that are
obvious. The other four are the **analysis and conclusion only**: your
observations, what you made of them, and what you can claim.

Go to each investigation's own page for the question, the method, the
hazards and the analysis prompts. None of that is repeated here. This
page is only the standard the five are held to.

> [!note] The one that is not marked
> [[Projectile Range]] is written up on Unit 1, Day 4 as the practice
> run, using these same rows, and it carries no mark. That is on
> purpose: the first time you meet a standard you should be allowed to
> miss it.

## Written in class, and why

Each of these gets its own period, here, with the numbers still on the
bench beside you. That is a choice, not a scheduling accident. Three
nights later a write-up is about what survived in your memory; the part
that is worth a mark — the argument you build while the result is still
strange to you — has already gone flat. Doing it here also means I am
standing in the room while you need me, which is the point. Ask.

Every one of the five is written in the period immediately after the
bench work, while the apparatus is still a memory rather than a note.
The five are not spread evenly across the units, and there is no
principle hiding in that. Unit 2 gets two because its collision labs
alternate with their writing days — bench on Day 8, write on Day 9,
bench on Day 10, write on Day 11. The other five investigations are
analysed in class too. They are simply not the ones carrying a mark, and
each of their pages says so at the top.

## Whose work is whose

Two or three people at one bench take better readings than one person
alone, and arguing about a number is half of learning to trust one. So
the measurements belong to the bench, and it is perfectly fine for three
of you to hand in the same table.

**Everything after the table is yours.** What you graph, what you decide
the uncertainty permits you to claim, and how you say it. Three identical
analyses are one analysis that three people watched being written.

## Success criteria

Seven rows, the same seven each time, until you stop needing to look them
up. Six describe how a physics argument has to be built and are identical
for all five; the seventh points back at the third column above, and is
the only one that changes.

Hold your own draft against them before it goes up —
[[Judging Your Own Work]] is the routine. Because the rows stand still,
the five write-ups end up measuring you rather than five different tasks,
which is the whole reason there are five of them.

| Quality | What it looks like in your write-up |
| --- | --- |
| The data is left alone | A unit on every column, the readings you took sitting beside anything you worked out from them, and the note that the string slipped on trial 3. No interpretation in this part, and no trial missing because it was inconvenient |
| A graph that does some work | Axes labelled with units, a line or curve whose slope or intercept means something physical, and a sentence saying what |
| Symbols before numbers | The algebra carried to an expression, substituted once, and the answer given to a number of significant figures you could defend |
| A diagram drawn to convention | Free-body diagram, resolved components, field lines, ray path — labelled, and with the positive direction stated and then kept |
| Uncertainty that came from somewhere | The dominant source named, with a number, what that number does to the conclusion, and one change to the method that would actually shrink it. "Human error" names nothing and fixes nothing |
| The conclusion claims no more than it may | A paragraph that answers the question the investigation asked, holds the result against what the physics said should happen, and is willing to end with "this apparatus cannot tell the two apart" when that is the truthful ending |
| This investigation's own question is answered | Whatever the third column above asks of this one, done and shown — the quantity that survived, the field read off the map, the wavelength pulled out of three lengths. A write-up can satisfy every row above it and still never answer the question the bench period asked |

The fifth row is the one that separates this course from last year's.
"Experimental error may have affected our results" is a sentence somebody
wrote to fill a heading. A finding names the one step that mattered in
this room on this day, puts a size on it, says which way it pushed the
number, and offers something you would do instead. That last part is not
decoration: if you cannot propose a fix, you have not yet worked out what
went wrong.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

These five produce more product evidence than anything else in the
course, and they hand you the other two kinds for free, because each one
is a bench day followed by a writing day. Watch on the day the numbers
are made. Talk on the day they are argued from, with the table open on
the desk between you.

OBSERVE — Unit 3, Day 5, the conductive-paper bench
  Watch for: whether the probe ever leaves the space between the
  electrodes. Out at the edges of the sheet the potential barely changes
  and the readings wander, and that is precisely where a pair either
  takes the awkward readings or continues the pattern by eye. The
  finished map is a set of smooth curves and looks much the same either
  way, unless a pair happened to record numbers out there — so this
  corroborates the first criteria row rather than being hidden by it,
  and corroboration is worth the two minutes.
  Going well: someone says the reading is 1.8 to 2.1 all the way across
  here, and marks a band rather than a confident line.
  Stuck: four tidy equipotentials in the middle, nothing beyond them,
  and the outer curves drawn last.
  Record: a tick per bench on a sketch of the sheet, probed or not
  probed. One walk around the room.

TALK — Unit 2, Day 9, while they write up the collisions
  Sit down where the momentum table is already filled in.
  Ask: "All four of your momentum totals came out low, and none came out
  high. Does a pattern like that let you rule anything out?"
  Then: "What number would have made you say kinetic energy WAS
  conserved in the sticky collision — and how close is close enough?"
  A strong answer separates an offset that always leans one way from
  scatter that leans both, and can name something that would produce the
  first: a track that is not level, a photogate catching the flag
  off-centre, a mass taken from the label rather than the balance. On
  the second it sets a threshold against its own uncertainty instead of
  answering "it just wasn't". A1.8 asks them to weigh the evidence and
  name where the error came from, and this is that happening in front of
  you. It is also the one thing a paragraph headed "Sources of Error" can
  imitate perfectly without any of it ever having occurred.
  Record: three columns — leaned one way, scattered both ways, did not
  see a difference. Take the third column to Unit 3, Day 6 and open with
  those people.

Product evidence takes care of itself here: five pieces of writing, each
collected before its own bell, on five days you already know.
