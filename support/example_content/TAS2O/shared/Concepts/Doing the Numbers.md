Communications work runs on a surprising amount of arithmetic, and all
of it is arithmetic you already have. The trick is knowing which
calculation answers which question before deadline, not after. The last
section, on measuring, is the one the shop runs on.

## Exposure: the three settings that trade against each other

Exposure is measured in **stops**, and one stop means twice or half the
light. That single idea makes the three settings interchangeable:

| Change | One stop more light | One stop less |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shutter | Twice the time: 1/125 → 1/60 | Half: 1/125 → 1/250 |
| Aperture | One f-stop wider: f/4 → f/2.8 | f/4 → f/5.6 |
| ISO | Double: 400 → 800 | Halve: 400 → 200 |

So a photograph at 1/250, f/4, ISO 800 is exposed identically to one at
1/125, f/5.6, ISO 800 — the same total light, different look. That
substitution is the whole craft of exposure, and it is one calculation.

## Lighting ratios

A **lighting ratio** compares the key light to the fill: a 2:1 ratio is
one stop of difference and reads as gentle; 4:1 is two stops and reads
as dramatic; 8:1 is three stops and much of the face is in shadow.
Portraits for [[Portraits with Purpose]] are usually 2:1 or 3:1 —
enough shape to be interesting, not so much that the subject looks
menacing in a school newspaper.

## Aspect ratios, resolution, and cropping

An **aspect ratio** is width to height: 3:2 out of most cameras, 16:9
for video, 1:1 and 4:5 for social, 9:16 for vertical. Cropping between
them always throws pixels away, so shoot with the final ratio in mind
and leave room.

Resolution arithmetic you will actually use:

- A 4000 × 3000 image is 12 megapixels.
- Print quality wants about 300 pixels per inch: 4000 px ÷ 300 ≈ 13
  inches wide at full quality.
- Web wants pixels, not inches: a 1200 px wide image is plenty for a
  story page and loads in a fraction of the time a full-size file does.

## File sizes and time, which is the constraint nobody plans for

| Medium | Rough size | What that means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| JPEG photo | 3–8 MB | A game shoot of 400 frames is a few gigabytes |
| Raw photo | 20–30 MB | Same shoot, ten times the card |
| 1080p video | ~100 MB per minute | A 20-minute interview fills 2 GB |
| 4K video | ~400 MB per minute | The same interview fills 8 GB |
| Audio, WAV | ~10 MB per minute | Small enough that you should always record it separately |

Multiply before the shoot, not after. Cards, drive space, and the time
to copy files are all things that have ended a deadline in this
newsroom, and all three are predictable with one multiplication.

> [!example] The deadline calculation
> Three reporters, four hours of 1080p footage, one editing machine.
> That is roughly 24 GB to copy, and a copy runs at maybe 80 MB per
> second from a card — about five minutes per hour of footage, so
> twenty minutes before anyone can start editing. Plan for it in
> [[The Production Pipeline]] and it is a coffee break; discover it at
> 3 p.m. and it is a missed publication.

## Millimetres and inches, which the shop mixes

The shop measures in millimetres, and then hands you things sized in
inches. The bridge is one exact number: **1 inch = 25.4 mm**.

| You are holding | Its size | In the other system |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The screw on top of a camera tripod | ¼″-20: a quarter inch across, 20 threads per inch | 6.35 mm across |
| Plywood sold as "⅛ inch" | Nominally 3.175 mm | Measure it — sheets often come in near 3 mm, and the difference matters |
| A phone | Its maker lists it in millimetres | Divide by 25.4 for inches |
| A print at 300 pixels per inch | 4000 px ÷ 300 ≈ 13.3 inches | ≈ 339 mm |

Three habits make the numbers trustworthy:

- **Measure the real thing, not the label.** "⅛ inch" is a name, not a
  measurement. The calipers settle it.
- **Pick the unit, then stay in it.** Convert once, at the start, and
  write the unit beside every number. Mixed units in one sketch are
  how a 6.35 mm hole becomes a 6.35-inch one.
- **Read calipers to the second decimal, and trust the first.** Digital
  calipers show hundredths of a millimetre; your grip, the plastic,
  and the edge you chose are worth a few of those hundredths. Measure
  three times and write down all three.

Machines add their own small numbers. A laser burns away a sliver of
material along every line — the **kerf**, a fraction of a millimetre
you measure on your own test card — and a 3D printer tends to print
holes a little small. The allowance you design in for that is a
**tolerance**, and finding it is what the class tolerance coupons in
[[The Tolerance Coupon]] is for.
