**Four entries, one per site, written in class on the day we visit.**
Individual, even where the looking is done in pairs.

It is the one piece of assessed work here that is written where the
evidence is, and it is the one everything else borrows from.

## The four entries

| Entry | Written on | The question that entry closes on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The shoreline | The shoreline day, Unit 1, in the room afterwards | Something here was built or placed to change what the water does. Is it working, and what on this stretch tells you so? |
| The woodlot | The woodlot day, Unit 1, in the room afterwards | What does this piece of ground allow, and what does it prevent — and which use does that leave possible? |
| The farm | The farm visit, Unit 2, back at school | Why is a farm on this ground and not five kilometres away? Land, water, inputs, labour, markets — which one is doing the most work? |
| The neighbourhood | The neighbourhood walk, Unit 3, in the room afterwards | What do the housing types and densities on this block say about how many people live here, and what put them there? |

Each site page — [[The Shoreline Study]], [[The Woodlot Study]],
[[The Farm Visit]], [[The Land Use Walk]] — tells you what to record
while you are there. This page tells you what the entry itself has to
do afterwards, and it is the same every time.

## Written in class, and why

You write the entry in the last part of the period, in the room, on the
day. Not at home that night, and not the following week.

Three reasons, and only one of them is about marks. Field evidence
decays: within a day you remember the interesting bits and lose the
inconvenient ones, and the inconvenient ones are usually the finding.
Second, I am in the room while you write, which is the point at which a
question about your own page is worth something. Third — the one about
marks — work that carries a mark in Ontario is done under a teacher's
supervision wherever it can be. What you do with the notebook at home
is yours, and worth doing, and not something I judge.

The notebook stays with you — you will want it at the next site. Before
you leave, photograph the entry and send it to me, or hand me your phone
at the door and I will. My comments come back **before the next field
day**, so the place you act on them is the next site.

## How it is marked

The same five things, every entry.

| Criterion | What earns the marks |
| --- | --- |
| Recorded on site | Date, time, location and weather at the head of the page, and every figure written where you took it |
| Observation and inference kept apart | What you saw on the left, what you concluded on the right — and nothing crossing over |
| Traceable numbers | Every figure says how you got it: paced, taped, counted, the notebook in the photograph for scale |
| A sketch that works | Labelled, north arrow, rough scale, and showing what the question needs rather than everything in view |
| The day's question answered | Answered from your own page, with a plain sentence about what your evidence cannot settle |

The fifth row is where the marks concentrate, and it is the one nobody
can do for you: the first four are discipline, and the fifth is
geography.

> [!warning] An absence is a finding
> If the stretch you surveyed has no exposed roots, no debris line, and
> nothing built on it, write that down. "No evidence of retreat on this
> stretch" is a real observation and it will be worth more at the end of the course
> than an inference somebody expected you to make.

## Working in pairs, marked on your own

At the shoreline and on the walk you look in pairs, because one person
holding the tape and one writing is how it is done. The page is still
yours. Two partners who saw the same stretch and wrote the same four
sentences have handed in one entry between them, and I will say so.
Where your partner's page disagrees with yours, say so in your own —
that is evidence about the method, and it earns marks.

[[The Field Notebook]] sets up the notebook itself in the first week,
and [[Fieldwork Safety and Ethics]] governs every one of these days,
including whose ground you are standing on.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

The four entries are the product, and they arrive on their own. What
they cannot show you is how a number got onto the page, which is the
whole of this task's difficulty.

OBSERVE — Unit 1, Day 6, at the shoreline, while the profiles are being
drawn
  The page itself will tell you whether an absence got written down —
  the callout on this page asks for one, so treat the entry as the
  self-report and watch for the thing behind it. Watch what happens
  AFTER the absence: does the student walk fifty metres along to find
  out whether "no debris line here" is local or general, or write it
  once and stop? Two entries reading "no evidence of retreat on this
  stretch" are indistinguishable on paper, and only one of them was
  checked.
  Going well: somebody leaves their spot, looks at a second section,
  and comes back with either a boundary or a confirmation.
  Stuck: one observation, made once, standing in for the whole shore —
  or an inference in the observation column with no measurement near it.
  Record: pair names on the clipboard you are carrying anyway, one tick
  column. You are outside; keep it to a tick.

TALK — Unit 1, Day 7, during the warm-up where yesterday's sketches are
compared
  They are working in pairs and you are free to move, which is the only
  reason this fits.
  Ask: "Whatever has been put on that stretch to hold the shore — where
  is the material it stopped going instead?"
  A strong answer points along the shore, downdrift, and says what
  would be piling up or thinning out there. That is B2.2 — human
  activity altering a physical process — and it is a step past what the
  entry itself asks, which stops at whether the structure works.
  Then, to each partner separately: "Your sketch puts the change in
  material three metres from where your partner's does. What would
  settle it if we went back tomorrow?"
  A strong answer names a fixed thing to measure from and a repeat, not
  "mine is right". This is also where the individual evidence comes
  from on a day when two people surveyed one stretch.
  Record: one line per STUDENT, not per pair — the mark on this task is
  individual and this is the day you can defend that. You will not reach
  a whole class in one warm-up; take half the room and catch the rest at
  the woodlot, where the second question works unchanged with the soil
  pit in place of the shore.

The product evidence is the four entries, each collected on the day it
was written.
