Shine light on a metal and electrons come off — but the details refuse to
fit a wave model, and that refusal started quantum mechanics.

## What the wave model predicts, and what happens

| Wave model says | Experiment says |
| --- | --- |
| Any colour works if bright enough | Below a threshold frequency, nothing happens at any brightness |
| Brighter light gives faster electrons | Brighter light gives MORE electrons, at the same speed |
| Faint light needs time to build up | Emission is immediate, even for very faint light |

## Einstein's explanation

Light arrives in quanta of energy $E = hf$. One photon gives its whole
energy to one electron. If that is less than the work function $W$ — the
energy binding the electron to the metal — nothing happens, however many
photons arrive.

$$E_{k,\text{max}} = e V_{\text{stop}} = hf - W$$

The graph of maximum kinetic energy (or stopping potential $V_{\text{stop}}$)
against incident frequency $f$ is a straight line whose slope yields
Planck's constant $h$ and whose horizontal intercept gives the threshold
frequency $f_0 = W/h$. Analysing simulated or laboratory photocell data to
extract $h$ directly tests quantum theory against classical electrodynamics.

## Why it mattered

It won Einstein the Nobel Prize — not relativity — and it forced physics
to accept that light is granular as well as wavelike. See
[[Wave–Particle Duality]].
