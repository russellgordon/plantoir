You will meet dozens of economic claims this term, in news reports, party
platforms, industry submissions and your own drafts. Most are neither true
nor false as stated. They are underspecified, and your job is to find out
what is missing before you agree or disagree.

Six questions do most of the work. Copy them somewhere you will see them.

- [ ] What exactly is being measured, and in what units?
- [ ] Compared with what, and over what period?
- [ ] Who collected the number, and what do they gain if you believe it?
- [ ] Is this a level or a change — and is the change real or nominal?
- [ ] Is the claim causal? If so, what is the comparison group?
- [ ] What would have to be true for this claim to be wrong?

Those are questions to work through on paper, not boxes to tick — nothing
on this site records what you click.

## The two failures worth naming

**A stale figure presented as current.** This is the failure mode of
economics specifically. The Bank of Canada held its policy interest rate at
2.25% on 15 July 2026, with the next fixed announcement date of
2 September 2026. Any sentence in your work that gives a rate needs the
date of the decision attached, because the sentence will outlive the rate.
The fix is not to avoid numbers. It is to date them.

**A correlation wearing a causal verb.** "The minimum wage rose and
employment fell" is two facts. "The minimum wage increase cost jobs" is a
claim about what would have happened otherwise, and the honest version of
it names the comparison — a similar region that did not raise its wage, a
similar group of workers, a period before the change. See
[[Does a Minimum Wage Cost Jobs|Does a Minimum Wage Cost Jobs?]], where economists using different
comparison groups reach genuinely different answers from the same data.

## Whose interest, and does it disqualify them?

Not automatically. An industry association's submission on a tariff is
motivated, and it is also likely to contain the best available detail on
that industry's cost structure. A think tank has a position and a
methodology, and only one of those is a reason to discount it. Read
motivated sources for their evidence and check their method; discount them
for the conclusions they reached before they looked.

> [!warning] Distinguish the disagreement you are having
> Whether carbon pricing reduces emissions is a positive question, settled
> in principle by evidence. Whether the reduction is worth the cost to a
> household in Timmins is a normative one, and no dataset settles it. A
> paper that argues the second while pretending to argue the first is the
> most common weak paper in this course.
