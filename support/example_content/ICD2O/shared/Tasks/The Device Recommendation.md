## What you are making

A one-page recommendation for **somebody you actually know** who needs a
device — a phone, a laptop, a tablet, a console, or the machine a family
shares. Real person, real budget, real uses. You interview them, turn
what they do into requirements, and recommend one thing you can defend.

## What must be in it

- **Who they are and what they do with it**, in three or four sentences
  taken from an actual conversation. "It's slow" is not a requirement —
  ask when it is slow, and at what.
- **The budget**, and where it came from.
- **Anybody else who uses it.** Most machines are shared. Name the
  second person, say what they need that your main client does not, and
  which of your choices is there for them. If it genuinely is one
  person's machine, say how you know.
- **A note on your notes.** You are holding somebody's budget and their
  habits. Say what you asked permission to write down, what you decided
  to leave out, and where these notes live between the interview and
  the hand-in. "In a file called `interview.txt` on the school drive,
  which only I can open" is an answer; "on my phone somewhere" is the
  problem this line exists to catch.
- **A requirements table**: each thing they do, the hardware or software
  it leans on, and what that means concretely.

  | What they do | What it leans on | What that means |
  | --- | --- | --- |
  | Twelve tabs and a video call | Memory | 16 GB, not 8 |
  | Photos from a real camera | Storage, then screen | 1 TB; a screen worth looking at |

- **The device you recommend**, with its specifications written out
  properly — processor, memory, storage, screen, battery, ports — using
  the vocabulary from [[Hardware Inside the Box]] correctly.
- **The software they will need**, and what kind each piece is: an
  operating system, an application, a service that runs somewhere else.
  [[Software and Operating Systems]] has the distinction that matters.
- **One connected device** that belongs with it — headphones, a printer,
  a watch, a controller, a smart speaker — chosen on what it does rather
  than on brand. [[Connected Devices]] is where we work out what each
  one is actually for.
- **What you gave up**, and who would notice. Every budget forces a
  trade; naming it is what makes this advice rather than an
  advertisement.
- **A setup checklist for their first day**: the security settings you
  would turn on for them, where their files will live and how they are
  backed up, and the one habit you would ask them to keep. From
  [[Staying Secure Online]] and [[Files and the Cloud]] — and you have
  to be able to explain each item to them in one sentence.
- **Research sources and verification**: cite the credible sources used to
  verify component specifications, software compatibility, and benchmark
  performance (manufacturer spec sheets, independent test reviews,
  accessibility compatibility guides) as practised in
  [[Finding Answers Online]].

## The working periods

| Working period | What it is for |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Interview written up in their words, your note on the notes written, and the requirements table started |
| 2 | The software half: what they will need, what kind each piece is, and their first-day setup checklist |
| 3 | The row you ruled weakest, fixed first; then a classmate plays your client and you fix it while they are still sitting there |

The ruling itself is done two classes earlier, in the clinic period —
see [[Judging Your Own Work]].

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Grounded in a person | Real uses, in their words, not a product review |
| More than one head counted | The second user's needs are named, and one choice is traceable to them |
| Careful with their information | The brief says what was recorded with permission, what was left out, and where the notes lived |
| Correct vocabulary | Memory, storage, processor, application, operating system used precisely |
| Requirements drive the choice | Every number traces back to something they do |
| Research practices verified | Hardware specs, benchmarks, and compatibility are confirmed against credible sources |
| Connected device justified | Chosen for what it adds, not for the box it comes in |
| Honest about trade-offs | Says what was given up and who notices |
| Safe from day one | The checklist names the security settings, where their files will live, and how they are backed up |
| Written for the client | They could follow it without you in the room |

## Reflect

A [[Dev Journal]] entry: which of their answers changed your
recommendation most? And what would you have got wrong if you had
skipped the interview and gone straight to shopping?

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 1, Day 15, the working period where a classmate plays
the client and reads the draft
  Watch for: what happens in the two seconds after a question. The
  finished page shows which questions got answered; it cannot show
  which ones were waved away, and that is the interesting half. A
  writer who answers three questions out loud and opens the document
  for none of them has decided their client is wrong, without noticing
  that they decided it.
  Going well: the client's own phrasing goes into the page, mid-
  sentence, while they are still talking.
  Stuck: the answer is given confidently across the desk and the
  document is never touched; or the "client" is handed a script and
  asks only what the writer already covered.
  Record: a tick and one word beside each name on your seating plan —
  wrote, argued, or scripted.

TALK — Unit 1, Day 12, while they are working on the software half
  Ask: "Their cousin says forget all this and buy the machine they
  bought. What do you say back to them?"
  Then: "Which item on your setup checklist is the one they will
  resent, and why is it still on the list?"
  The first answer is B2.3 out loud — a recommendation assessed against
  real requirements rather than against a spec sheet. Listen for whether
  the defence comes back to something one of the two users actually
  does; a student who answers on brand or on price has a requirements
  table that is decoration, and one who only ever cites the main client
  has forgotten the second user is in the room too. The second reaches A2.2:
  naming a measure and what concern it mitigates, for a specific
  person, is harder than listing the settings and it is the part that
  never appears in a written checklist.
  Record: one line each in your own copy of the criteria table, in the
  row it speaks to.

The product evidence is the recommendation handed in on Day 16.
