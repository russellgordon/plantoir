The standard origin story of computing fits on a poster: a few
inventors, a few garages, mostly one country, starting around 1940.
It is not exactly false — those people existed — but it is radically
incomplete. Computing was also built by wartime codebreakers whose
work stayed secret for decades; by the women who did the actual
programming when programming was considered clerical; by engineers,
mathematicians, and manufacturers on every continent; and it rests on
something far older, because Indigenous communities in Canada and
around the world were encoding, storing, and transmitting information
in sophisticated systems for millennia before electronics existed —
and are building today's technology right now, from language
revitalisation apps to networks serving remote communities.

Questions worth arguing about:

1. Why do origin stories shrink to a few famous names? Who benefits
   when the story is small — and what does everyone else lose?
2. Early programming was largely women's work, low in status. Then
   the field became prestigious, and male-dominated. What changed —
   the work itself, or only who was welcome to be credited for it?
3. Inventing a processor counts as innovation. What about building
   it, testing it, documenting it, teaching it, translating it into a
   new language or a new community? Where is the line between
   "innovator" and "helper" — and who has been drawing that line?
4. Timelines of computing usually start in the 1940s. What has to be
   ignored to make that the beginning — and what would a timeline
   look like that took information systems, not electronics, as the
   subject?
5. The research *is* the activity: find one real innovator in digital
   technology from an Indigenous community or another community the
   standard story leaves out — local, Canadian, or global. Verify the
   story with two independent sources, then bring it to class. We are
   building the bigger poster together.

This is why [[Our Classroom Norms]] commits us to asking whose
contributions count — and a name you bring back may become your
starting point for [[The Innovation Brief]]. History is a dataset,
and this discussion is about its missing rows.
