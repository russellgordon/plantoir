Canadians came out of the Depression and the war having learned something
they had not intended to learn: that the federal government could organize an
entire economy when it decided to. Having watched Ottawa direct war
production, ration food and employ everyone, few people were willing to
accept that nothing could be done about unemployment, illness or old age. The
welfare state was built on that expectation.

## The programs, and what each one did

| Program | When | What changed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unemployment insurance | 1940 | Required amending the Constitution first |
| Family allowance | 1945 | A monthly payment per child, paid to mothers |
| Old age security | 1951 | A pension by age, not by proving need |
| Hospital insurance | 1957 | Cost-sharing made provincial plans work |
| Medicare | 1966 | Saskatchewan first in 1962, over a doctors' strike |
| Canada and Quebec plans | 1965 | Contributory — and Quebec ran its own |

Those years are the years the federal statutes passed — the *Old Age
Security Act* of 1951, the *Hospital Insurance and Diagnostic Services Act*
of 1957, the *Canada Pension Plan* of 1965, the *Medical Care Act* of 1966.
The money usually began flowing a year or two after the vote, which is worth
remembering whenever a date is offered as the moment something changed.

Notice the pattern. Almost every one began provincially or required a
federal-provincial bargain, because health, welfare and education are
provincial jurisdiction. Canadian social policy is therefore always also a
story about federalism, and Quebec's insistence on running its own version of
these programs is not a footnote to the Quiet Revolution but part of it.

## Who the universal programs still missed

"Universal" was a real achievement and it was not complete. Benefits often ran
through the male breadwinner's employment record, which left women whose work
was unpaid dependent on a husband's entitlement. Status Indians were
administered by a separate department under separate rules, and services on
reserve were funded at levels no province would have accepted. Immigrants
faced residence requirements. When you assess these programs, assess them
against who was inside and outside the working definition of a citizen.

## The rest of the postwar rebuild

Newfoundland joined Confederation in 1949 after two close referendums,
bringing a population that had voted narrowly, and reluctantly, for Canada.
The Massey Commission argued at the start of the 1950s that Canadian culture
would be swamped by American media without public support, and the Canada
Council followed. Canada adopted its own flag in 1965, over a bitter
parliamentary fight in which opponents argued that dropping the Union Jack
was a betrayal. The Cold War ran underneath all of it, and the Gouzenko
defection at the very end of the war put Canada at its beginning.
