## What you are making

Somewhere near you, a quantity is multiplying: water cooling toward
room temperature, a ball losing height with every bounce, a tire
losing pressure, a rumour finding ears. Choose one you can actually
measure, collect the data yourselves, and model it with an equation of
the form $y = a \cdot b^x$.

You finish with a one-page **data story**: the table, the graph, the
fitted equation, a doubling time or half-life in real units, and two
predictions — one inside your data, one beyond it — each with a
sentence on how much you trust it.

## Milestones

- [ ] Phenomenon chosen and a measurement plan written — what, how
      often, with what tool
- [ ] Data collected; table and graph drawn before any equation is
      attempted
- [ ] The constant ratio hunted down in the table — this is your $b$,
      as [[The Exponential Function]] explains
- [ ] Equation fitted and checked against the graph in [[Using Desmos]]
- [ ] The limits the situation itself sets written down — the values
      your quantity can never reach, and what stops it
- [ ] Predictions made; trust boundary stated; data story assembled

## How it is assessed

Growth over polish, per [[How Marks Work]]. The measurement plan and
the trust boundary carry as much weight as the equation, because they
are where the thinking shows — see [[What Makes a Model Good]].

You work in pairs and you are marked one at a time. The phenomenon,
the readings, the table and the graph belong to the pair. **Yours
alone** are the trust sentence under one of the two predictions —
signed by whichever of you wrote it, one prediction each — what you
tell me one at a time while the write-up is under way, and the ten
minutes of [[Math Journal]] you write in class on the day it is due.
Nobody in this course receives a shared mark.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like in your data story |
| --- | --- |
| Real data | You measured it; the messiness is visible and kept |
| A hunted ratio | The base $b$ comes from the table, not from a guess |
| A defended fit | Equation and graph demonstrably agree |
| Real-unit answers | Doubling time or half-life lands in minutes, bounces, or days |
| Limits the world sets | The values your quantity can never reach, named, with what stops it |
| Honest reach | Each prediction carries a trust sentence, signed by the partner who wrote it |
| The entry that goes with it | Your in-class journal entry names where this model should not be trusted, and why saying so helped |

> [!success]- If your data will not behave
> Real data never sits perfectly on a curve — that is a feature. If no
> constant ratio appears, check whether your quantity approaches a
> floor (room temperature, zero pressure) and measure the *distance to
> the floor* instead. A model that almost fits, with the gap explained,
> beats a perfect fit to invented numbers.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

Two people, one data story, and a mark that has to belong to one of them. Put
the questions to each partner while the other is out of earshot.

OBSERVE — Unit 2, Day 11, the working period

  Watch what a pair does when their table refuses to hand over a constant
  ratio. The folded callout tells them to look for a floor and measure the
  distance to it instead. The "Limits the world sets" row will have them name
  a floor either way, so the row is no help here: what it cannot tell you is
  whether that floor was something they went and found because their ratio was
  drifting, or a fact about the situation written down at the end because the
  table asked for one.

  Going well: somebody says out loud that the ratio is drifting, asks what the
  quantity is heading towards, and the pair opens a fresh column of distances
  from that floor.

  Stuck: readings rounded harder and harder until a ratio appears; or the
  three awkward measurements at the end quietly dropped and never mentioned.

  Record: one letter beside each pair on your day plan — F found a floor,
  D dropped rows, C clean from the first reading.

TALK — Unit 2, Day 12, moving between pairs while the write-up is under way

  Day 11's conference already has its own question on the agenda, so these
  belong to the following class, when the writing is going and you are free to
  move.

  Ask each partner in turn: "You decided how far apart to take your readings.
  What did that spacing cost you, and what did it buy you?"

  Then: "Which single reading in your table would you most want to take again,
  and why that one and not its neighbours?"

  A strong answer names the trade in BOTH directions rather than saying closer
  is better: readings packed tightly give more rows to test a ratio against,
  but each ratio is then measured across a smaller change, where the wobble in
  the instrument is a larger share of it; readings spread out cover more
  halvings and leave fewer rows to test. B3.1 itself permits data from a
  secondary source; this task does not, and the "Real data" row says so — but
  a table on the page looks the same either way. The conversation is where
  you find out the readings were taken, and taken on purpose.

  Record: a line against each NAME, never one line for the pair.

The product evidence is the data story handed in on Day 14, with the ten
minutes of journal that closes that period.
