**The final evaluation. The portfolio submitted complete, plus a written
critical analysis in the examination period.** Individual.

A visual arts course cannot be settled by an essay alone. Half of this
is a term's work; half is what you can do with an unfamiliar image.

## The portfolio

Everything, organised, from September — see [[The Portfolio]]:

- The drawing you made on the first day, before anything was explained
- The sketchbook, complete
- Photographs of every finished work
- The five elements studies, the information piece, the media trials
- Your written work: [[The Interpretation]], the society study, artist
  statements
- Critique notes, including feedback you received and what you did about
  it

## Written in the examination period

**Part one — an unseen work** (about 90 minutes). You are given a work
you have not studied, and you run the critical analysis process on it in
writing: reaction, description, analysis and interpretation, judgement.

**Part two — your own year** (about 60 minutes):

1. What changed between your September sketchbook and your June one?
2. What has making and analysing art done to how you see things outside
   this room?
3. What skills has this course built that are not about art, and where
   would each of them be worth having?
4. **Name four routes on from here**: a senior course you could take,
   a postsecondary program or a job that uses this, and **two** things in
   this community you could actually do before you leave this school —
   a festival that needs posters, a mural, a place that would take a
   volunteer, a wall somebody wants painted.
   Then say which one of them you would take, and what would have to be
   true for you to take it.

## What is marked

| Criterion | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Completeness | The record is there, organised and dated |
| Analysis | The unseen work is read carefully, with evidence |
| Self-assessment | Your written judgement of your own work is specific, and points at evidence anyone could go and look at |
| Insight | You notice something not obvious about your own year |
| Routes | Four real ones — a course, a program or job, and two things in this community — named accurately enough that somebody could act on them |
| Communication | Clear writing, correct terminology |

> [!tip] The September sketchbook is the evidence
> Nobody enjoys looking at their first pages. They are also the only way
> to see a term of change, and every strong reflection leans on them —
> which is why [[The Sketchbook Habit]] starts on the second day of the
> course.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

The examination period gives you nothing to watch and nobody to talk
to: a silent room, and afterwards a stack of writing. So for this one
the observation and the conversation have to be taken in the days
before it, while the portfolio is being built. Day 21 puts you at every
table for the second sketchbook marking, which is what makes the
observation cost nothing; Day 22 is a writing period, so the room is
working and you are free to move round it.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 21, the portfolio work period
  Watch for: the order the year gets laid out in. Some students spread
  it chronologically and then start looking; some sort it by quality and
  put the weakest work at the back. The second group is building a
  folder of best work, which is a different object and the one this task
  explicitly is not — and a submitted portfolio that is complete and
  organised looks the same either way.
  Going well: the September work is out where they can see it, and
  somebody is holding two versions of the same piece side by side.
  Stuck: a pile face-down, and a student asking whether the failed ones
  have to go in.
  Record: you are already at every table for the second sketchbook
  marking. Add one word to that note — chronological, or ranked.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 22, the writing period after the evidence hunt
  Ask: "You have judged work against a criteria table four times this
  term — somebody else's once, and your own three times. What do you
  notice first now, before you read a single row?"
  Then: "Show me a work in here you would defend against somebody who
  called it unfinished — and one you would not."
  A strong answer to the first names something that has become automatic
  and can say when it was not: where the eye goes, whether the darks go
  dark enough, whether the thing is doing what it was meant to do. That
  is B1.4 — a student who has built a strategy for judging work rather
  than an opinion about it. The second question separates a piece that
  stopped at the right point from one that stopped when the bell went,
  with the object there to point at. Neither is reachable in the
  examination, which asks about a work nobody has seen and about the
  year in general.
  Record: two words per student on the class list — what they notice
  first, and which piece they defended.

The product evidence is the portfolio, handed in at the final check,
and the examination writing.
