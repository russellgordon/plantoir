Texture is one of the elements of design, and it arrives in two different
ways. **Actual texture** is what your fingers would feel — thick paint
standing up off the surface, sand mixed into a ground, the ridge left by a
cut in a block. **Implied texture** is a flat surface that convinces your
eye it is rough, furry, wet, or worn. Most of the year you are producing
the second one, and these are the techniques that do it.

## Techniques that make a specific effect

**Cross-hatching.** Parallel lines in one direction, then a second set
crossing them, then a third at another angle. Each layer darkens the area,
and because the lines follow the form — curving around a cheek, straight
down a wall — cross-hatching builds depth and dimension at the same time as
it builds tone. Rotate the angle between layers or you get a plaid.

**Stippling.** Dots. Density does the work: crowd them for dark, space
them for light. It is slow and unforgiving, and it produces a soft,
granular surface that hatching cannot, which is why it survives.

**Scumbling.** A broken, scrubby layer of light, opaque paint dragged over
a dry darker layer so the one underneath shows through in patches. It is
how you make a surface look weathered, dusty, or atmospheric instead of
freshly painted.

**Dry brush.** Almost no paint on a splayed brush, dragged fast across
textured paper so the colour catches only the raised tooth. Grass, hair,
rust, old wood.

**Impasto.** Paint applied thickly enough to hold the mark of the brush or
knife. This is actual texture, and it changes with the light in the room —
a ridge casts its own small shadow, so the work is never quite the same
twice.

**Frottage.** Paper laid over something with relief — a floor grate, tree
bark — and rubbed with graphite or a wax crayon until the texture comes
through. The fastest way to collect real surfaces into a sketchbook.

> [!example]- The six-box sampler (click to expand)
> Divide a sketchbook page into six boxes and draw the same small object in
> each — one box per technique above. Same object, same light, six
> surfaces. You will find out in forty minutes which technique suits your
> hand, and you will have a reference page you use all semester.

Choosing a technique is choosing an effect. Ask what you want the surface
to do before you ask which method to use, and the answer usually names
itself. [[Drawing]] is where these marks start; [[Painting]] is where
scumbling, dry brush, and impasto belong.
