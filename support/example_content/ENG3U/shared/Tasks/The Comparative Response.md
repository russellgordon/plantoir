## The task

Put [[Monkey Beach]] beside [[The Inconvenient Indian]] and write about
what each can do that the other cannot.

## The trap to avoid

A comparison that alternates — a paragraph on one, a paragraph on the
other, forever. That is two essays sharing a stapler.

A real comparison has **one argument** that needs both texts. Two shapes
that work:

1. **One question, two answers.** "What does each text do with the fact
   that the reader may need convincing?" Then compare the strategies.
2. **A claim about form.** "The novel can withhold; the argument cannot
   afford to." Then prove it in both directions.

## What it must contain

- A thesis that requires both texts.
- At least two passages from each.
- One paragraph where the comparison complicates your own claim.
- Attention to form: what a novel does that an argument cannot, and the
  reverse.

## Reading required first

[[Whose Country, Whose Story]] and [[Reading Non-Fiction]] are the two
concept pages this task is built on. Read them before the drafting
period rather than after.

## How it is marked

| Category | What I am looking for |
| --- | --- |
| Knowledge | Both texts known precisely, including King's concessions and the novel's silences |
| Thinking | One argument, needing both texts; the comparison does work rather than existing |
| Communication | The reader always knows which text they are in and why |

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 3, Day 14, the period for gathering passages from both
texts
  Watch for: whether a student works in both books at once or finishes
  one and then goes looking in the other. The second route is where the
  alternating essay this task warns about is actually made, and by the
  draft it is too late to see how it happened.
  Going well: two books open, and a note that belongs to neither one on
  its own.
  Stuck: a full page on Robinson, then a blank page headed "King".
  Record: three columns on the seating plan — both, one then the other,
  one only.

TALK — Unit 3, Day 15, at the ninety-second check-in the agenda already
schedules
  Not the two shapes printed on the task page — they have read those.
  Ask: "Where did you have to stop and reread, in either book, and what
  was the writing doing at that point?"
  Then: "Say your argument in one sentence, without naming either book."
  A strong answer to the first points at something on the page — a joke
  that arrives where an objection would, a sentence that turns halfway, a
  scene left unexplained — and says what it makes a reader do. On the
  second, being able to do it at all is the test: a comparison that needs
  both books has one idea underneath it, and an alternating essay has
  two. That is B1.6 heard:
  analysing how various aspects of a text contribute to the ideas it
  explores. Listen for the student's reading of how each book is built.
  That is the evidence; what they think about the people either book is
  about is not what is being judged here, and saying so once at the
  launch on Day 13 is worth the ten seconds.
  Record: one line each, in seating order, on the same sheet.

The product evidence is the response handed in on Day 17.
