Nobody has defined a specialised cell for you yet, and that is on
purpose. Today you look, and then we argue about what you saw.

## What you are trying to find out

Every cell in a multicellular organism traces back to a single cell
that divided, and divided again. Division makes copies. So why does a
nerve cell not look remotely like a red blood cell, and why does a root
tip look nothing like a leaf?

You are after two separate pieces of evidence:

1. That cells **differ in ways that line up with what they have to do**.
2. That there is a **process producing more of them**, and that the
   process has stages you can catch in the act.

## What you have to work with

- A compound light microscope, and the objectives on it
- Prepared slides: an onion root tip, and a selection of specialised
  plant and animal tissues (bone, muscle, nerve, blood, leaf
  cross-section, root)
- Onion, forceps, dissecting needle, slides, cover slips, water
- Iodine stain and paper towel, if your group wants contrast

Nothing here is a numbered procedure, and there are two decisions you
have to make and justify in writing before you touch anything.

- **Choose three specimens** and **one structural feature** you will
  compare across all three — cell shape, wall or membrane, relative
  size, what fills the inside, how tightly packed they are. One feature
  compared across three specimens beats three specimens described
  separately, and you should be able to say why.
- **Choose the objective** you will make each observation on, and say
  why that one. Highest power is not automatically best; the field of
  view at 400× is too narrow to judge how cells are arranged.

## Safety

> [!danger] The microscope, the glass, and the stain
> - Carry it with **two hands** — one under the base, one on the arm.
> - Start on the **lowest-power objective**, every time.
> - Coarse adjustment on **low power only**, and bring the objective
>   and slide together while **watching from the side**. Focus by moving
>   the objective **away** from the slide. Never turn a focus knob
>   downward while your eye is at the eyepiece.
> - High power gets the **fine adjustment and nothing else**.
> - Slides and cover slips are glass. Report every chip and every break
>   the moment it happens — broken glass goes in the sharps container,
>   never in the bin and never into your hand.
> - Iodine stain is an irritant and stains skin and clothing
>   permanently. Eye protection on, one drop, and wick the excess from
>   the far edge of the cover slip with paper towel.
> - Nothing is eaten, drunk, or tasted in this room, and you wash your
>   hands before you leave whether or not you think you touched
>   anything.
> - Prepared slides are sealed and stay that way. They cost more than
>   you would guess and the next class needs them.

once: [[Using a Microscope]].

## The prediction you write first

In your [[Science Journal]], before the first slide goes on the stage,
write two predictions with reasons.

1. For each of your three specimens: **one structural feature you
   expect, and the job you expect it to serve** — "because a cell that
   has to ... would need ...". You will be wrong somewhere, and the
   place you are wrong is the interesting part of the discussion.
2. For the onion root tip: **which stage of division you expect to see
   most often**, and why.

That second prediction hides a real piece of reasoning. A prepared
slide is a single instant, frozen, across thousands of cells. If cells
enter division at random moments, then the fraction of cells caught in
a stage is a clue about **how long that stage lasts**. Decide what you
expect before you count, because it is very easy to count your way to
whatever you already believed.

## What to bring to the consolidation discussion

- The claim your comparison supports, in one sentence, with the
  observation that supports it.
- Your fraction table, and the estimate of relative durations you can
  build from it, with the arithmetic shown.
- The specimen that did **not** match your prediction, and what you
  now think is going on.

## What you should not claim

Be specific here — "there might have been errors" is not a limitation.

- **One field of view is not a random sample.** You chose where to
  look, and a root tip is not uniform. Your fractions describe your
  field; extending them to the whole root is a further claim needing
  further fields.
- **Relative duration rests on an assumption.** The count only tells
  you about stage length if the cells are dividing independently and
  continuously. If they are synchronised, the argument collapses.
- **Structure suggesting function is a hypothesis, not a result.** You
  observed a shape. You did not observe the cell doing anything. Say
  "consistent with", not "shows that".
- **A fixed, stained slide is not a living cell.** Fixing and staining
  change what you are looking at, which is why the specimen holds still
  in the first place.
