Biodiversity has three levels, and confusing them makes conservation
arguments sloppy.

- **Genetic diversity** within a species — the raw material of adaptation.
- **Species diversity** within an ecosystem.
- **Ecosystem diversity** across a region.

## Why it is not just about charismatic animals

An ecosystem's function depends on the connections between species, and
the species doing the most work are rarely the ones on the poster. Remove
the pollinators and the plants fail; remove the decomposers and the
nutrients stop cycling.

## Why an ecosystem needs it to keep working

The connection argument above says diversity matters when something is
removed. The stronger claim is that diversity is what lets an ecosystem
absorb things going wrong in the first place, and it works through two
mechanisms worth naming separately.

**Redundancy buys resilience under stress.** Where several species do
roughly the same job — several pollinators, several decomposers, several
grazers — a bad year for one is covered by the others, and the function
continues. Where one species does that job alone, the function has a
single point of failure. This is why a diverse grassland recovers
biomass after a drought while a monoculture in the same drought does
not: the diverse plot contains species that happen to tolerate dry
conditions, and the monoculture contains one that may not.

**Variety slows disease and invasion.** A pathogen or an insect
specialised on one host moves easily through a stand where every
individual is that host, and stalls where the next plant along is
something it cannot use. The same logic runs *within* a species: genetic
diversity means a disease that some individuals cannot resist, others
can. The reverse case is the one everybody knows — a crop grown from
genetically near-identical stock is a single organism spread over a
continent, and has repeatedly been treated as such by a single disease.

Both mechanisms describe an ecosystem's capacity to take a shock and
keep functioning, which is what **viable** means here. It is a different
argument from the ethical one in [[What Do We Owe Other Species]], and
both are worth being able to make;
this one holds even for somebody who values an ecosystem purely for what
it produces.

## The Canadian numbers

Canada holds about 20% of the world's remaining wilderness and about 25%
of its wetlands. It also has more than 600 species assessed as at risk.
Both facts are true at once, and any argument that uses only one of them
is being selective.

## Measuring it

Species richness counts how many; a diversity index also accounts for how
evenly individuals are spread among them. Two forests with the same number
of species can differ enormously in diversity, which is what
[[Biodiversity Field Study]] measures directly.
