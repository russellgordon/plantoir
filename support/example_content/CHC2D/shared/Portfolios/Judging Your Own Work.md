Every task page in this course ends with a table of criteria, published
before you start. Most people read it once on launch day and never
again, and then hand in an essay that misses two rows they could have
fixed in twenty minutes. This page is the twenty minutes.

We do it together the first time, on a draft of mine, so the move is not
new to you when it is your own work being judged.

## The routine

1. Open the task page and put its criteria table beside your draft.
2. Go row by row. For each one write **yes**, **not yet**, or **partly**
   — and one sentence of evidence: the paragraph, the footnote, the
   sentence a reader would point at. "Yes" with nothing beside it is a
   wish.
3. Find your weakest row. Not the easiest to fix — the weakest.
4. Write one sentence saying what you will change, and when.
5. Change it, in the rest of this period or in the next one. That is why
   this is never scheduled on the day something is due: a judgement you
   have no time to act on is only a worry.

Steps 2 and 3 are the whole exercise. Naming your weakest row honestly
is harder than any of the fixing, because it means writing down the
thing you were hoping I would not notice.

## The two rows people lie to themselves about

**Evidence.** It is not a count. Four citations attached to four
sentences that would have been just as true without them is a weaker
row than two sources genuinely weighed against each other — see
[[Using Evidence]]. Ask of each one: if I deleted this source, which of
my sentences would stop being defensible?

**Counter-argument.** Almost every draft has a paragraph beginning "some
might argue". Read yours out loud and ask whether the objection you
answered is one anybody actually held at the time, or one you built
because it was easy to knock down. [[Building an Argument]] calls that
the difference between an argument and a performance of one.

> [!example]- One row, judged honestly
> From [[The Wartime Decision]], the Perspective row: *"The reasoning of
> the time reconstructed, not caricatured."*
>
> **Partly.** I have the order-in-council and what the minister said in
> the House, so the government's reasoning is there. I have nothing
> from the people it was done to, and I wrote "they must have been
> terrified", which I cannot source. Weakest row.
> **Change:** find one first-hand account with a provenance I can
> state, or cut that sentence and say plainly that the record I have is
> the state's. Next period.
>
> That entry is worth more than "looks good" against all five rows, and
> it took less time to write.

## Where this fits

Your judgement of your own work is not part of your mark, and neither is
a classmate's — see [[How Marks Work]]. That is deliberate, and it cuts
both ways: nothing you write here can cost you anything, which is
exactly what makes it worth writing honestly.

It also happens to be the habit the discipline runs on. A historian who
cannot say which of their own claims is the weakest has not finished
reading their own evidence, and the entry you write here is the same
move you make in [[Your Inquiry Notebook]] when a source shifts your
position.

One last thing worth doing: read your judgement out loud to the person
beside you. You will usually hear the generous rows yourself before they
say anything.
