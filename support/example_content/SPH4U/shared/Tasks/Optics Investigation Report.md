> [!abstract] At a glance
> **Format:** a designed investigation, run and written up. **Pairs.** Due
> at the end of Unit 4. **Assessed on:** Thinking and Investigation.

You design this one. I supply the equipment and the safety veto.

## The brief

Ask a question about the wave behaviour of light that you can answer with
the equipment in this room, then answer it.

Questions that have worked before:

- How does the fringe spacing depend on slit separation, quantitatively?
- What is the thickness of a soap film at the moment a given colour
  vanishes?
- Can a CD be used as a diffraction grating, and what is its track
  spacing?
- How polarized is the light reflected from the lab window, and at what
  angle is it most so?

## Requirements

- A written procedure, checked by me BEFORE anything is switched on.
- A prediction with an uncertainty, submitted before data collection.
- At least one graph whose slope or intercept carries meaning.
- An honest treatment of what limited your precision.
- **A closing paragraph on the thing in the world.** Every question above
  runs on physics that somebody has already built a device out of — a
  grating in a spectrometer, the coating on a camera lens, polarizing
  film in a pair of sunglasses or a laptop screen, the pits on a CD.
  Name the one closest to your question, say what it is for, and say
  which of your own results explains why it works.
- Prose that meets the standard on [[Writing About Physics]].

## What is not required

A correct answer. A well-designed investigation that produced an
ambiguous result, analysed honestly, scores higher than a copied procedure
that produced the textbook number.

## What is yours alone

You design it together, you get the procedure approved together, and you
run it together — an optical bench needs two people and there is no
version of this you could do alone. The data is the pair's. **The
analysis and the conclusion you write separately**, and those are what
your own mark comes from. You will have the same graph; you should not
have the same paragraph underneath it.

## How this is assessed

The whole report is Thinking and Investigation, and these are the rows.
Read your own against them before you hand it in — see
[[Judging Your Own Work]].

| Quality | What it looks like in your report |
| --- | --- |
| A question that could come out wrong | One quantity you change, one you measure, and everything else named and held fixed |
| A procedure somebody else could run | Enough detail for another pair to repeat it, and approved before anything was switched on |
| A prediction on the record, with an uncertainty | Written down before data collection, with the reasoning that produced it, and not adjusted afterwards |
| A graph that carries the argument | Axes with units, the data on the graph rather than beside it, and a slope or intercept whose physical meaning is stated |
| Precision treated honestly | What limited you, by how much, and whether that is enough to explain the gap you found — or whether something else is going on |
| A conclusion your data supports | The answer these measurements allow, including "this apparatus cannot tell the two apart" where that is the truth |
| The physics is shown doing a job | One real device that runs on the effect you measured, what it is for, and which of your own numbers explains why it works — your result used to explain the device, not the device used to decorate your result |

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

The whole point of this task is that nobody handed them the question, so
the evidence worth having is about the design, and it stops being
gatherable the moment the report is written. Both prompts below sit
before the hand-in for that reason.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 12, the peer read on that agenda
  Watch for: what a pair does when the reader says the conclusion does
  not follow from the data. Two things happen in this room, and they
  look nothing alike. One pair pulls the graph back out. The other
  explains the sentence again, more slowly. The report shows you the
  final sentence and never which of those produced it.
  Going well: the conclusion comes back narrower than it went in.
  Stuck: the conclusion comes back louder.
  Record: two columns — reopened, defended. Ten seconds a pair, and the
  defended column tells you who to sit with on Day 13.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 9, while you are approving procedures
  You are already going pair by pair, so this costs you nothing extra.
  Ask: "Name a result you could get on Day 11 that would leave you with
  nothing to say. What would you do about it — that day, not afterwards?"
  Then: "Suppose it comes out at the accepted value on the first run.
  Would you believe it?"
  A strong answer names a genuinely uninformative outcome — a fringe
  spacing that does not shift at all, a film that never loses the colour
  — and has a move ready, usually widening the range of the thing they
  are changing. That is A1.1 heard: a question sharp enough to focus the
  inquiry. The second question tells you whether the prediction they are
  about to lock in is theirs or a copy of the expected answer; a strong
  answer wants a second run before believing anything, and can say what
  would have to agree with what. An approved procedure on paper shows
  you the plan and never shows whether the pair know what would sink it.
  Record: two ticks on the approval sheet — had a failure case, had a
  move. Anyone missing the second tick is likely to lose a period on
  Day 11.

The product evidence is the designed investigation written up, in on
Day 13.
