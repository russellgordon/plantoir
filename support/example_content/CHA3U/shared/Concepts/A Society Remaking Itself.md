Between the 1790s and the 1870s the United States changed faster in ordinary
daily life than in almost any comparable stretch of its history. People moved
off farms into towns, into factories, into new churches and new sects, and
into arguments about how children should be raised and educated. None of it
reached everyone, and the parts of the population it did not reach are the
test of any claim you make about the period.

## Belief on the move

A long wave of revival, later called the Second Great Awakening, spread
through camp meetings and circuit riders. Methodist and Baptist
congregations grew fastest, partly because they ordained preachers who came
from the same places as their congregations. Revival taught that a person
could be saved by their own decision — and that a society could be improved
by human effort. That second idea is the engine behind almost every reform
movement of the era.

New religious communities formed in the same climate. The Shakers built
celibate communal villages and left behind furniture, architecture and
account books that historians still use. The Latter Day Saints were founded
in the 1830s, met sustained hostility and violence, and moved west.

## Days that looked nothing alike

> [!note] Comparing lives is the skill, not the list
> The curriculum keeps asking you to compare a factory child, an enslaved
> child and a wealthy child. Do it on stated criteria — hours of labour,
> control over one's own body, access to schooling, legal standing, the
> ability to leave — rather than on general impressions of hardship. Two of
> those three children could in principle run away and be pursued as
> runaways; only one of them was legally the property of another person. Say
> which criteria you are using and the comparison becomes an argument
> instead of a mood.

A mill worker in a New England textile town might be a young woman on a
one-year contract, boarding in a company house under rules about church
attendance and curfew, sending wages home. A farm family in Ohio worked
longer hours but set them itself. A free Black family in Philadelphia built
churches, schools and mutual aid societies while living under laws that
could be changed against them and in a city where mobs sometimes burned what
they built. A newly arrived Irish family in New York took the work available
at the wages offered, which is why they appear in the records as both a
labour force and a target.

Cities grew faster than sanitation, housing or policing, and epidemics
followed. Public schooling spread, unevenly and mostly in the North, on the
argument that a republic needs literate voters — an argument that quietly
defined who counted as a future voter.
