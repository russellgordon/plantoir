You have just spent a unit measuring where energy goes and how fast it
gets there. This discussion asks what happens when those two quantities
are attached to a decision about how a country keeps its lights on and
its buildings warm.

The chemistry you need is already yours: [[Enthalpy]] tells you how much
energy a reaction releases, [[Rates of Reaction]] and
[[Collision Theory and Catalysts]] tell you what it takes to get it out,
and neither one tells you whether the arrangement is a good idea.

## The question

Compare two ways of supplying the same energy — one conventional, one
alternative — and say which you would build, on what grounds, and what
you are accepting in exchange.

The last clause is the assessment. Every option on the table has a real
cost. An argument that finds none is an argument that stopped looking.

## Three different things people mean by "efficiency"

Most of the disagreements in this room will turn out to be two people
using the same word for different quantities.

| What is meant | The question it answers | Where it misleads |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Conversion efficiency | Of the energy that went in, how much came out as the form I wanted? | Useless for comparing a fuel with sunlight or wind, where the input is not something anyone paid for |
| Energy returned on energy invested | Over its life, how much energy does this deliver compared with the energy it took to build, fuel, and maintain? | Depends heavily on where you draw the boundary, and honest sources say where they drew it |
| Emissions per unit of energy | How much is released into the atmosphere for each unit delivered? | Says nothing about what happens at the mine, the reservoir, or the end of the equipment's life |

A thermal plant burns fuel to make heat, and only a fraction of that
heat can ever become electricity — that ceiling comes from
thermodynamics, not from poor engineering, and no amount of investment
removes it. A wind turbine has no such ceiling in the same sense,
because it is not converting heat into work; what limits it is how often
the wind blows. **Comparing those two with one number is the commonest
mistake made in this argument**, and it is made in both directions.

## Enthalpy per mole is usually the wrong axis

A data booklet gives you enthalpies of combustion per mole, because
that is the chemically meaningful quantity. Almost no practical decision
is made per mole. Fuel is carried by mass and stored by volume.

$$\ce{CH4(g) + 2O2(g) -> CO2(g) + 2H2O(l)}$$

> [!example]- The same fuels, ranked three different ways
> Take the enthalpies of combustion from your own data booklet rather
> than from this page, and divide.
>
> Per mole, methane releases far more than hydrogen. Per **gram**, the
> ranking reverses and is not close: methane comes out near 55 kJ/g and
> hydrogen near 142 kJ/g, because hydrogen's molar mass is so small.
> That single fact is why hydrogen is attractive for anything that has
> to carry its own fuel.
>
> Then rank them per **litre** at ordinary pressure, and hydrogen
> collapses to last place, because a gas of molar mass 2 g/mol is
> extremely light for the space it occupies. Nothing about the chemistry
> changed between the three rankings. The denominator changed.
>
> This is why hydrogen's practical problem is storage rather than
> energy content, and why an argument about hydrogen that never
> mentions compression, liquefaction, or a hydride is an argument that
> has only done the first division.[^1]

## Conditions, and why the answer is always a compromise

The curriculum asks you to look at the conditions that make a reaction
efficient. The most instructive case in chemistry is one you will be
able to explain fully in a few weeks, and can already half explain now.

$$\ce{N2(g) + 3H2(g) <=> 2NH3(g)}$$

Ammonia synthesis is exothermic, and it goes from four molecules of gas
to two. Cold favours the product. High pressure favours the product. And
at a temperature cold enough to favour it properly, the reaction is so
slow that nothing useful comes out — because **direction and speed are
different questions**, which is the whole lesson of this unit.

So the process is run hot enough to be quick, at high pressure to claw
back what the heat cost, with an iron catalyst to lower the activation
energy, and the unreacted gas is recycled rather than vented. The
catalyst is worth being precise about: it changes the path, so both the
forward and the reverse reaction speed up. It does not change $\Delta H$
and it does not change how far the reaction goes — that will be
[[Le Chatelier's Principle|Le Châtelier's Principle]] and
[[Dynamic Equilibrium]] in Unit 4.

Two smaller cases, both worth an argument:

- **Combustion.** Enough excess air and the fuel burns completely;
  carbon monoxide and soot are unburned fuel, which is wasted energy and
  a health hazard at the same time. But a hotter flame makes more oxides
  of nitrogen out of the nitrogen already in the air. Tuning for one
  works against the other, and there is no setting that wins both.
- **The catalytic converter.** It only works once it is hot enough for
  the catalytic surface to become active, which is why a large share of
  a short trip's emissions happen in its first few minutes. That is a
  kinetics problem, not a design failure.

## What to bring

- [ ] Two named technologies that could supply the same demand
- [ ] For each: what reaction or process is doing the work, written as an
      equation where there is one
- [ ] One quantitative comparison, with the quantity **named** — per
      gram, per litre, per unit delivered, over what lifetime
- [ ] Where each number came from, by organisation and publication
- [ ] The cost you are accepting for the one you would build
- [ ] One thing you could not find out

Good ground for the numbers is the kind of body whose job is to publish
them: **Natural Resources Canada** and the **Canada Energy Regulator**
for Canadian energy production and use; **Environment and Climate Change
Canada** for emissions and environmental effects; **Statistics Canada**
for consumption; and the **International Energy Agency** for
international comparisons. Bring what the source actually says. "I could
not find a figure for that" is a legitimate contribution and a more
honest one than a number you cannot point at.

## Two positions that are both too easy

**"Renewables solve it."** Every option is built out of something. A
reservoir floods land somebody lived on or fished. A wind farm has to go
where the wind is, which is where somebody already is. Solar output at
Canadian latitudes is strongly seasonal, and the season of lowest output
is the season of highest heating demand. Nuclear leaves waste that
outlasts every institution now planning for it. None of that is an
argument for doing nothing; it is an argument for saying what you are
trading.

**"It is not practical."** This one usually smuggles in the assumption
that the current arrangement has no costs, which is false and is
measured. Emissions, extraction, and disposal are all costs; they are
simply ones that have already been accepted and stopped being visible.
Natural gas is a useful test of whether you are being even-handed: it
releases less carbon dioxide per unit of energy at the stack than coal
does, and methane escaping upstream of the stack is a real quantity that
belongs in the same comparison. If you use the first half of that
sentence, use the second.

## Ground rules

- Attack arguments, never people.
- No invented numbers. Name the organisation and the quantity.
- State the units of every comparison, because most of the arguing turns
  out to be about the denominator.
- Nobody's household heating is on trial in this room.

Afterwards, record the position you actually hold in your
[[Chemistry Journal]] — you are free to disagree with the one you were
assigned. This feeds directly into [[The Rate Investigation]] and, in
Unit 5, into [[Batteries and Corrosion]]. Related: [[Hess's Law]] for
getting an enthalpy you cannot measure directly, and
[[Reading a Data Table]] for handling the values you find.

[^1]: There is a second, quieter trap in those figures. The enthalpy of
    combustion of any hydrogen-containing fuel depends on whether the
    water produced is counted as liquid or as vapour, because
    condensing it releases energy that a table may or may not have
    included. Two sources can therefore print different values for the
    same fuel and both be correct. Check which convention your booklet
    uses before you compare it with anyone else's number — this is
    exactly the "under what conditions" question from
    [[Reading a Data Table]], and it changes results by a noticeable
    margin.
