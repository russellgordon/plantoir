The conventions of literary writing exist so that a reader can spend
their attention on your argument instead of on your formatting. Learn
them once.

## The rules that matter

1. **Present tense for the text.** *Juliet decides.* Past tense only for
   the world: *Shakespeare wrote the play around 1595.*
2. **Titles**: *italics* for plays, novels, films; "quotation marks" for
   short stories, poems, articles, episodes.
3. **Quote verse line by line**, with a slash for a line break in a short
   quotation: "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks? / It is
   the east, and Juliet is the sun." Longer than three lines: block it,
   indented, no quotation marks.
4. **Cite drama by act, scene, and line** (2.2.2–3), prose by page.
5. **Introduce and explain every quotation.** Claim, evidence,
   explanation — the third move is where the marks live.
6. **Never summarise the plot.** Your reader has read it.
7. **Name the writer**, not "the author".
8. **Develop an authoritative voice.** Write with confidence and analytical
   clarity, cutting apologetic qualifiers (*In my opinion*, *I feel that*).
9. **Maintain grammatical cohesion and parallelism.** Ensure subject-verb
   agreement, clear pronoun antecedents, and balanced sentence structures.

## The paragraph that works

> Hamlet's first soliloquy is not about his father. It is about his
> mother's speed — "within a month" appears twice in twenty lines, and
> the second time it has become "a little month". Shakespeare gives the
> grief a calendar, and the play's obsession with delay is set by a man
> who has been counting days since before the ghost arrives.

Claim, evidence, explanation, and a link outward. The quotation is three
words long: quote the phrase you are analysing, not the speech it lives
in.

## Signposting an argument without announcing it

Weak: *In this essay I will discuss three ways…*

Strong: *The feud survives every attempt to end it because the play never
lets an adult speak to a child without a threat in the sentence.*

The second names the argument, and the reader now knows what to watch
for. See [[The Extended Essay]] for the whole structure.

> [!warning] Three habits that flatten an essay
> **Padding** ("since the dawn of time"), **plot summary** wearing an
> analytical hat, and **quotation dumping** — a paragraph that is 60%
> quoted text is a paragraph that has not been written.
