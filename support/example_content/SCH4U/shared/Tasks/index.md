Everything in this course that carries a mark is on one of these pages.
Five term tasks, one per unit; the four write-ups gathered on
[[The Lab Reports]]; and the two pieces at the end that make up the
final thirty per cent. They are not decoration on top of the practice —
they are the places where you have to put everything together at once,
which is the only place I can see whether you can. What is **not** here
is not marked, and [[How Marks Work]] lists what that leaves out.

Each of the five term tasks has the same four parts, and they are in
this order for a reason.

| Part | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **The situation** | A real problem, with enough detail to constrain your choices |
| **Who it is for** | A specific audience, because who is reading changes what belongs in it |
| **What to hand in** | The deliverable, and the parts it must contain |
| **How it will be judged** | The criteria, in plain language, before you start |

| Task | Unit | The short version |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [[The Property Prediction]] | Structure and properties | Predict how a substance behaves from its structure alone, then check yourself |

## Five rules that apply to all five term tasks

**Every number carries a unit and a defensible number of significant
figures.** A pH quoted to four decimal places from a probe reading to
0.01, or an enthalpy to five figures from a temperature change good to
two, is not more precise than the honest version — it is less honest.
See [[Significant Figures and Units]].

**Every claim must be traceable.** You will never be penalised for
saying "I could not find a reliable figure for this". You will be
penalised hard for a number nobody can trace, including one you
half-remember and one a search engine summarised for you. Name the
source, and name what kind of source it is: a government agency, a
safety data sheet, a professional body, a peer-reviewed article, a
manufacturer's technical documentation. See
[[What Counts as Evidence]].

**Say which way your errors push.** A limitation with no direction
attached is a gesture, not an analysis. *Heat lost to the calorimeter
lowers the measured temperature change, so our value for
$\Delta H$ is less negative than the true one* is a sentence about
chemistry. *Sources of error: human error* is not.

**Predict before you measure, and date it.** Every task that involves
data asks for a written prediction produced beforehand, with a
mechanism. A prediction written afterwards shows, every time, and it
costs you the most interesting section of your own report — the part
where you were wrong and worked out why.

**Say where your model stops.** This is the Grade 12 addition and it is
the one that separates the top band from the rest. Electron counting
gets bond angles wrong when there is a lone pair. Collision theory says
nothing about what happens during a collision. Le Châtelier's principle
predicts a direction and never a magnitude. A confident claim with no
stated limits is weaker work than a modest claim that knows exactly
where it ends.

## Safety applies to tasks exactly as it does to labs

Anything you design and run is checked by me first, **in writing**, with
the hazards listed and your response to each hazard beside it. Some
proposals come back for a redesign, and that is the system working
rather than a judgement of you.

None of the standing rules is relaxed because the work is yours rather
than mine:

- **School reagents only, at the concentrations supplied.** Nothing from
  home, nothing from another room, no concentrated acids or bases, and
  no substituting a stronger reagent because the dilute one was slow.
- **Combine only what your approved procedure lists.** Substances that
  are harmless apart can produce a toxic gas together and there is no
  way to tell by looking, which is why this rule is absolute rather
  than a matter of judgement.
- **Add acid to water, never water to acid.** The heat has somewhere to
  go when the water is already there; the other way round it
  concentrates in a few drops that can boil and spit acid back at you.
- **Sodium hydroxide does not warn you.** It feels soapy rather than
  painful — that sensation is the solution attacking the fats in your
  skin — and it burns **deeper** than a dilute acid of the same
  strength because nothing makes you pull away. Rinse suspected contact
  for 15 minutes and tell me, whether or not it hurts.
- **Flammable organic solvents stay away from every flame and hot
  plate**, and are used in the fume hood. The vapour is the hazard, not
  the liquid — it is heavier than air and travels along a bench.
- **Electrolysis makes gas, including hydrogen.** No flames, and the
  cell is never sealed.
- **Calorimetry with a flame is a demonstration, not a student
  procedure.** Every calorimetry experiment you design is in a
  polystyrene cup, with solutions, at around room temperature.
- **Eye protection from the first move to the last of the cleanup.**
  Hair back, sleeves secured, closed-toe shoes.
- **Never pipette by mouth. Waft, never sniff. Nothing is tasted.
  Nothing is returned to a stock bottle.** Nothing that makes a gas is
  sealed in a rigid container.
- **Know where the eyewash, shower, extinguisher, blanket, and spill kit
  are before you start**, and **report every incident immediately**,
  however small.

The full agreement is in [[Safety in the Lab]] and
[[Lab Safety and WHMIS]].

Late? Talk to me **before** the due date and we will make a plan.
Afterwards there is much less either of us can do. If you are stuck at
the start rather than at the end, that is a different problem with a
better fix — [[Getting Unstuck]], then [[Help Sessions]].
