## What you are making

Ten short documents, one for each lab in [[Labs/index|Labs]], each written while
the circuit is still in front of you. A record is two pages at most.
It is not a report and it is not written up nicely afterwards — the
whole value of it is that it was made at the bench, in the period, by
the person whose hands were on the instrument.

The criteria never change, so the shape of a record does not either.
You predict, you measure, you reconcile the two, you name what fought
back, and you say what you would change so the thing survives. That is
the three-part shape [[Labs/index|Labs]] opens with, plus the fault and the
instrument settings, and it is the same on the first record in
the first bench day as on the last one at the end of the course.

Two of the ten labs use their own words for the first move and they
still count as a prediction: the logic block's truth table is filled in
before a wire is drawn, and the service bench writes its permission
matrix down before a single privilege is set. Everything below applies
to those two exactly as it does to the other eight.

## What each record carries

1. **The predictions**, computed rather than felt, in units wherever
   the quantity has them. Every predicted figure shows the law or the
   formula and the numbers you put into it — $I = V/R$, $P = VI$, a
   divider, an RC rise, a stack of tolerances. Where the prediction is
   a table rather than a number, the same rule holds: the intended
   truth table or permission matrix, written down before anything is
   built or set. A figure with no working behind it is a guess, and
   guesses do not reconcile against anything.
2. **The instruments and the procedure.** Which meter, which scope,
   which analyzer; the range, the timebase and the probe attenuation;
   where the probes and the ground clip actually sat; and the steps
   from that lab's own safety notes that you carried out before power
   — current limit set, logic levels compared, board unpowered while
   rewiring. Naming the instrument is half of it; naming its settings
   is the half that makes the number believable.
3. **The results table**, predicted beside measured, filled in at the
   bench in pen.
4. **The reconciliation.** Every gap between the two columns gets a
   named cause — a tolerance, an instrument limitation, a lead
   resistance, or an assumption of yours that was wrong. "Close
   enough" is not a cause.
5. **What fought back, and how you cornered it.** The fault, located
   rather than guessed: which half of the system each test ruled out,
   in the order you ran them. A record that says "swapped parts until
   it worked" has recorded the outcome and lost the method.
6. **The change you would make for margin**, with the number that
   justifies it and the thing it costs you. One change, specific.

## Written in class, and why

Every one of these is written in the period the lab runs, at the
bench, and handed in as it stands. Two reasons, and neither is
administrative.

The first is that it is the only way the record can be true. A number
reconstructed at home on Thursday is a memory of a number, and it
always comes back rounder and calmer than what actually happened. The
second is that work carrying a mark is done here, where the equipment
is, where I can see the process as well as the product, and where
nobody's mark depends on what they have at home.

So: [[How the Lab Runs]] already gives the recorder that job during
bench time and hands the last minutes to your journal, the record goes
in as it stands when the bench is reset, and you are never asked to
finish one between classes. Anything you add later is yours and is not
part of the mark.

One exception, at the start: the autopsy record from the first class is
written in the class after it, because on the day itself you had not
seen these criteria. Every record after that is made in the period.

## The record is yours

Benches are shared and that is how the work gets done: two or three
people, one scope, one set of readings, and the rotating recorder role
[[How the Lab Runs]] describes. The recorder keeps the bench's results
table for that lab; everyone copies those numbers into their own record
and writes the rest of it themselves. The bench's numbers are common
property. The record is not. Yours is written by you, in
your own words, and it says which parts of the bench work were yours on
that lab — who wired, who drove the instrument, who kept the table.
Two records from one bench may share every figure and should not read
the same anywhere else, because the reconciliation, the fault hunt and
the margin decision are yours to make and to defend.

## Success criteria

The same table for all ten. Read it once properly at the start of the course
and you will know it by Unit 2.

| Quality | What it looks like in the record |
| --- | --- |
| Predicted before it was built | Every predicted figure or intended result is present, with units where they apply and the reasoning behind it |
| Instruments named with settings | Meter, scope, analyzer or diagnostic tool, with range, timebase, attenuation, and where the ground sat |
| Procedure carried out first | The steps that lab actually asks for before power or before configuration, recorded as done |
| Reconciled, with named causes | Every gap between predicted and measured has a cause, not an adjective |
| Faults cornered, not swapped | Each test names the half it ruled out, in the order you ran them |
| A change with a number on it | One margin decision, its justifying figure, and what it costs |
| Repeatable by a stranger | Values, conditions and connections enough for another bench to get your result |
| Visibly your own | Your share of the bench work named, and the reasoning in your own words |

> [!tip]- The commonest way a record loses marks
> Not a wrong prediction — a vague one. [[How the Lab Runs]] collects
> your predictions before they can be revised, so nobody here can
> quietly back-fill a column; what people do instead is hedge. "It
> should be a few volts" survives any measurement and therefore proves
> nothing, and a prediction that cannot be wrong cannot be evidence.
> Commit to a figure, a unit and a condition, and leave the wrong ones
> exactly where they are — a prediction that missed by a factor of ten
> with a reason beside it is the strongest paragraph in most records.
