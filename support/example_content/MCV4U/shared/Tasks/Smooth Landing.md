## What you are making

A regional jet begins its descent at an altitude of $10\,000$ m,
twenty minutes out from the runway. You are designing the descent
profile — the altitude function $h(t)$ — and it faces three demands:
the plane must **arrive** (altitude zero exactly at the runway), it
must **touch gently** (vertical speed as close to zero as you can
manage at touchdown), and the ride must stay **comfortable** the
whole way down (acceleration kept small, no sudden lurch). You
finish with your chosen profile, its first and second derivatives,
the three graphs aligned one above another, and a **passenger
report** that says where the ride is gentlest, where it is worst,
and what your profile still asks of the people in the seats.

## Milestones

- [ ] Each demand translated into a precise statement about $h$,
      $h'$, or $h''$ before any candidate profile is proposed
- [ ] Two candidate families differentiated with [[Derivative Rules]]
      and [[The Chain Rule]] — one polynomial, one sinusoidal
- [ ] Touchdown checked exactly: $h$ and $h'$ evaluated at the moment
      of landing, not read off a zoomed graph
- [ ] Height, velocity, and acceleration graphed in a stack, per
      [[Motion on a Line]], and the stories cross-checked
- [ ] The passenger report written, with every comfort claim traced
      to $h''$

## How it is assessed

Per [[How Marks Work]], the reasoning is the product: a profile that
fails one demand, with the failure located and priced, outranks a
perfect-looking graph nobody differentiated. On the due date your
pair defends the touchdown out loud, and each of you takes one of my
questions alone.

The second piece that is yours alone is your [[Math Journal]] entry on
what the model ignores — wind, weight, the pilot — written in class on
the due date, with [[What Makes a Model Good]] as your guide. Between
them, the pair's profile and your own two contributions are the whole
of the evidence.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like in your work |
| --- | --- |
| Demands made precise | Each demand written as mathematics first |
| A toolbox at work | Derivatives taken by rule and stated cleanly |
| Exact touchdown | $h$ and $h'$ computed at landing, not eyeballed |
| Aligned evidence | Three stacked graphs tell one consistent story |
| An honest report | Comfort traced to $h''$, limits admitted |
| Yours alone | Your own note on what the model leaves out, and your own answer when I put a question to you rather than to your partner |

> [!success]- If your profile refuses one demand
> Adjust one feature at a time — stretching the descent time makes
> everything gentler, at the price of arriving later. If gentleness
> and punctuality genuinely pull against each other in your design,
> that trade-off *is* the finding: price it and present it.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 2, Day 16, the working period checking the profile against
every demand
  Watch for: whether the standard is set before the result or after it.
  A pair that writes down what "gently" will mean — a number, in metres
  per second, on the board — and then reports missing it is doing
  modelling. A pair whose definition of "gently" appears for the first
  time after they know what their profile does is doing something else.
  The passenger report presents whichever happened as though it were the
  plan, so the order is only visible while they work. The criteria row
  about an honest report does ask every pair to admit the limits, and
  most will, so their page is not evidence either way; what you are
  corroborating is whether the admission was volunteered or extracted by
  a number they could not argue with.
  Going well: a target written down, then missed, then said out loud.
  Stuck: "close enough" used for the first time about a number they have
  only just computed.
  Record: two columns down the class list — target first, target after.
  This is B2.5 being done rather than being written up.

TALK — Unit 2, Day 15, at the conference already on that agenda
  Hand one question to each of them. A pair hands you one profile; only
  this tells you which of the two of them can see what it does.
  Ask: "Your descent ends at touchdown. What does your function do a
  minute later — and why does that not matter?"
  Then, and this one is worth carrying on a card: "Here is a profile
  that arrives exactly on time, touches down at zero vertical speed and
  never breaks your comfort limit — and climbs twice on the way down. It
  passes all three of your demands. What did your demands fail to say?"
  A strong answer to the first says the function keeps going and the
  altitude goes negative, so the model means something only on a stated
  domain — B2.5's interpretation, and a thing no stack of graphs is ever
  asked to say. A strong answer to the second finds the missing
  condition: the three demands pin down the ends and the size of the
  acceleration, and say nothing at all about the sign of the velocity in
  between. That is B2.1 — velocity and the derivative connected in
  words — and a student who spots it has understood their own model
  better than the model shows.
  Record: initials and a single word each, on the class list, before you
  move to the next board.

A3.5 is product evidence here — the rules were either applied correctly
or they were not, and the write-up shows it. The product is the profile,
the three stacked graphs and the passenger report, handed in on Unit 2,
Day 17.
