Publication is not the end of a story; it is the moment the story
stops belonging only to us. Underneath the published piece — in
comments, reposts, and group-chat screenshots — the readers write
back, and what they write ranges from thoughtful correction to a
pile-on hunting for a target. A newsroom needs two plans it hopes
never to use: one for the work — when to reply, when to correct,
when to close a thread — and one for each other, because when
coverage draws heat, the heat lands on a teenager with a byline.

Questions worth arguing about:

1. A commenter is factually right and needlessly cruel about it.
   Does the tone change what we owe the correction — or only what
   we owe the commenter?
2. A thread turns on a student IN the story rather than on the
   writer. Whose job is protection now — the writer's, the
   editor's, the whole room's?
3. Reply, ignore, close: closing comments protects people and
   erases a public record of the response. What is lost each way,
   and who should make the call?
4. It is midnight and the writer of a criticized piece is typing a
   defence into the thread. What is the team rule — and why is
   "never reply alone" a kindness rather than a muzzle?
5. Your piece travels through a group chat as a screenshot,
   stripped of context, growing captions. No moderator anywhere.
   Is that beyond our responsibility — or exactly inside it?
6. What does checking on a teammate actually look like the week
   their byline takes heat? Three concrete moves, not sentiments.

Carry it back to practice: before anything goes live on
[[Publication Day]], the room agrees who watches the thread, and
nobody watches alone. Corrections run fast and plainly, per
[[Our Newsroom Standards]] — and a teammate under fire hears from
us before they hear from the crowd. [[Getting Help]] names the
adults who stand behind the room when the heat outgrows it.
