> [!abstract] At a glance
> Groups of 2 or 3 · five classes, four of them studio periods ·
> designs defended on the last · one annotated diagram, one
> optimisation argument

## What you are making

Your group will design a real physical thing under real constraints,
choosing one of two lanes: a **package** for a given product — contents
fixed, material costing money, shelves having heights — or a **scale
floor plan** for a defined space, with doors that must open and furniture
that must actually fit.

The deliverables are an annotated scale diagram of the final design and
a written optimisation argument: why this design beats at least two
candidates your group genuinely considered and rejected, with the
numbers that decided it. Rejected designs are not waste — they are the
evidence your argument stands on.

## How to work

1. Choose your lane, then write every constraint as a number with a
   unit before anyone sketches. A constraint you have not written down
   is a constraint you will violate.
2. Convert everything into one measurement system first — constraints
   arrive messy, in millimetres, metres, and a supplier sheet in
   inches — and show each conversion. [[Measurement and Optimization]]
   is the reference.
3. Generate at least three candidates. For each, compute what matters
   — area, surface area, volume — using [[Geometric Relationships]].
   Remember [[The Best Box]]: designs holding the same amount can
   differ wildly in what they cost to build.
4. Take one candidate and change a single dimension by a stated amount.
   Show what that does to the flat shape it is built from and to the
   space it encloses — the footprint's area and the package's volume,
   or the room's floor area and the air above it. The two do not move
   together, and the argument in step 5 usually turns on that.
5. Choose, and write the argument: what your group optimised for, what
   you traded away, and the numbers for every candidate — "it uses 18%
   less material and still clears the shelf" is an argument.
6. Draw the final annotated diagram to scale — every measurement
   labelled, every constraint shown satisfied.

Studio periods are class time, and that is the point: work that carries
a mark is built where I can see it being built — [[How Marks Work]]
explains why. In the studio period where the candidates are costed you
will also judge the design against the table below, following
[[Judging Your Own Work]]; the two studio periods after it are for
acting on what you found.

## What is marked as yours

The design is your group's; the mark is not. On the day the design is
defended, each member answers the Reflect question below out loud, on
their own — that answer is judged as yours. So is your ability to
account for any number on the page: I will ask whoever is nearest, not
whoever drew it.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like in your design |
| --- | --- |
| Constraints honoured | Every stated constraint is checked and shown met |
| Conversions shown | Unit changes are visible steps, not silent leaps |
| Real alternatives | At least two rejected candidates carry full numbers |
| A named trade-off | The argument says what was optimised and what it cost |
| One dimension moved | For one candidate you show what changing a single dimension does to the flat shape and to the space it holds |
| A true-to-scale diagram | Measurements are labelled and the scale is stated |
| Your own defence | You can name the candidate that came closest, the number that ruled it out, and account for any figure on the page |

## Reflect

At the defence, each group member answers one question aloud: which
rejected candidate came closest to winning, and what single number ruled
it out? No close second usually means the group stopped designing early.

> [!success]- If your group is stuck choosing (click to expand)
> Optimise for exactly one thing first — least material, most usable
> floor space — and build that design even if it is ugly. Then look at
> what broke. The constraint that broke is the trade-off your final
> argument is really about.
