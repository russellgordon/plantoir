> [!abstract] At a glance
> **Format:** a three-page brief for a decision-maker, a one-page
> technical appendix, and half a page on the people who do this work.
> **Topic chosen and researched in pairs; every page of it written by
> you alone.** **Due:** handed in on the first class of Unit 4.
> **Assessed on:** Application, Communication.

Write the brief a hospital board, a regulator, or a school board would
actually need before deciding.

## Choose one

CAR-T cell therapy · CRISPR for sickle-cell disease · genetically modified
salmon · gene drives for malaria mosquitoes · direct-to-consumer genetic
testing · synthetic insulin production.

## The brief (three pages, for a non-specialist)

1. What the technology does, in plain words.
2. What it would change for the people affected.
3. What it costs, and who pays.
4. **Where it stands with the regulator.** What Canadian rules currently
   allow, which body administers them, and how one other country has
   decided differently — with the difference explained rather than
   listed.
5. The risks, honestly weighted — not a list, an assessment.
6. Your recommendation, and the evidence that would change it.

## Who does this work

Half a page: **two** careers this technology creates or changes — one at
the bench or on the production line, one in the clinic, the regulator, or
explaining it to the public. For each, what the work involves in an
ordinary week and what training gets somebody there.
[[Where This Biology Leads]] is the starting point, not the finish.

## The appendix (one page, technical)

The molecular mechanism, in the vocabulary of [[Biotechnology]] and
[[Regulating Gene Expression]]: what is cut, copied, read, delivered or
expressed, in what order.

Name the components doing the work and say what each one contributes —
the plasmid or other vector, the enzyme that cuts, the recombinant
construct, the polymerase, the delivery vehicle. Every technology on the
list above is assembled from a handful of these, and knowing which ones
is the difference between describing a technology and understanding it.

## Written together, then written alone

Your pair chooses the technology together and researches it together: the
sources, the arguments, and the disagreements are shared, and Days 12 and
13 exist for exactly that. Then each of you writes your own brief, your
own appendix and your own career page. Two people may cite the same
sources. Two people who submit the same recommendation have submitted one
brief between them.

## How this is assessed

Published at launch, on Unit 3, Day 11. Run them against your draft with
[[Judging Your Own Work]] on Unit 3, Day 14.

| Quality | What it looks like in your brief |
| --- | --- |
| A non-specialist can follow it | Somebody who has not taken this course reads the three pages and can say what the technology does and what you want done about it |
| Sources judged, not just cited | Each source's origin, interest and date accounted for, and the claim separated from the evidence for it — a company's own trial is not the same kind of fact as an independent one |
| The regulation is real | Named Canadian rules and the body that administers them, one other jurisdiction compared, and the difference explained |
| Risk weighted rather than listed | The risks ranked, with the reason for the ranking, and the ones you judge small said to be small rather than omitted |
| A recommendation somebody could act on | Addressed to the decision-maker you chose, with the evidence that would change your mind named specifically |
| The mechanism is in the right order | The appendix takes the technology from start to finish without a step missing, and nothing in it is left in plain words because the mechanism was not understood |
| The components are named and explained | The vector, the enzyme that cuts, the construct, the polymerase, the delivery — whichever your technology uses — each with what it contributes rather than only what it is called |
| Two careers, not two job titles | What each job involves in a week, the training route into it, and how it connects to the technology you wrote about |

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 3, Day 13, the work time on the regulation section
  Regulation is where research habits show, because the real documents are
  long, dull and written for lawyers, and there is always a shortcut.
  Watch for: where the rule actually comes from. A federal page open on
  the screen, a news article about the rule, or the company's own
  "regulatory status" summary. Watch too for whether anybody notices the
  difference between guidance and law, or between a rule that exists and a
  consultation that has not closed.
  Going well: two windows open, a government one and a journalist's, and
  an argument at the desk about which one says what.
  Stuck: the whole section written from one page belonging to whoever
  makes the technology.
  Record: three columns on the day plan — went to the source, went to a
  report of the source, took the company's word. One circuit of the room.
  Note: the brief will name a regulator and cite something, and the
  citation row makes them account for it. What no brief can show you is
  whether they ever opened the thing they cited.

TALK — Unit 3, Day 12, at the conference already on that agenda
  Ask: "Who loses if your recommendation is taken, and what would they
  say about your risk section?"
  Then: "If the board took your advice and in three years it had gone
  badly, which sentence in your brief would they point at?"
  A strong answer can name a specific group, state their objection in its
  strongest form rather than its silliest, and then say why the
  recommendation still stands — or admit it moved. On the second question
  it can name a sentence, which means the writer knows which of their
  claims is carrying the most weight and how well supported it is. That is
  A1.9 heard: the brief will show you the sources they kept, not the
  reasoning that decided which to keep.
  Record: one line each — named the loser and the objection, named the
  loser only, neither.

The product evidence is the brief, the appendix and the career page,
handed in on the first class of Unit 4.
