By the end of Act 3 of [[Hamlet]], nearly every major character has formed
an opinion about what's wrong with him — and no two of those opinions
agree. This seminar asks whether Hamlet is actually losing his mind, is
performing madness on purpose, or whether that question stops making sense
somewhere along the way.

## Who's leading today

Two of you are running this seminar, not me. Before class, you'll have
prepared an opening question of your own to add to the list below, and
you're responsible for keeping the room on topic, drawing out anyone who
hasn't spoken, and deciding when it's time to move to the next question.
Leading a seminar like this is its own skill — different from having a
strong opinion in it — and it's one you'll each get a turn at across the
unit.

## The questions

1. Hamlet tells Horatio and the guards he may "put an antic disposition
   on" (1.5) — so we're warned in advance that he might fake it. Does that
   warning make it harder or easier to trust anything he does afterward?
2. Has anyone in your own life — or a character in another text we've
   read this year — ever acted strangely on purpose, to get people to
   underestimate them or leave them alone? Did it work the way Hamlet
   seems to want it to?
3. By the end of Act 3, is there a moment where Hamlet's "madness" and
   what he actually seems to feel look like the same thing, so the
   question stops being answerable?
4. If Hamlet is performing, who is the performance actually FOR? Is
   there anyone in the play he isn't performing for?

## Whose account do you trust?

Nobody in the play observes Hamlet without also wanting something from what
they conclude about him. That's worth mapping out before you decide who's
right.

| Character | Their account of Hamlet | What they gain if they're right |
|---|---|---|
| Ophelia (2.1) | Describes him as wordless, dishevelled, and frightening — reports it as something that happened to him | Little to gain — arguably the account with the least self-interest behind it |
| Polonius | Insists it's love-sickness for Ophelia | Being right proves his advice to her was wise, and hands him a theory to sell to the king |
| Claudius | Treats it as unpredictable and dangerous, worth having watched | An unstable Hamlet is one he can justify sending away, without admitting he fears him |
| Gertrude | Wavers between grief and worry | Least clear — which is itself worth discussing |

A strong answer to question 3 doesn't just pick a side and defend it for
the whole play. It locates one specific moment — the nunnery scene with
Ophelia is a common choice, but not the only one — and argues that in THAT
moment, "is he acting" and "does he mean it" can no longer be told apart,
using the actual language of the scene rather than a general impression of
Hamlet's state of mind.

## After the seminar: write it down

Before next class, pick one row from the table above and write a paragraph
in your reading journal: what does that character's account reveal about
their own bias, and how does knowing what they stand to gain change how
much weight their account should carry? This is the same skill you just
practised out loud, done again in writing, on the text itself rather than
on a performance of it.
