A system at equilibrium is not doing nothing. It is doing two things at
once, at matching speeds, which looks from the outside exactly like
doing nothing.

So here is the obvious next experiment: poke it. Add something. Take
something away. Warm it up. Then watch what a system that had settled
does when you refuse to leave it settled — and see whether what it does
is predictable enough that you could have written it down first.

You could have. That is the point of the period.

## What you are trying to find out

Three questions, in this order:

1. **Does a disturbed system move, and which way?** Add more of a
   reactant to a system at equilibrium and something happens to the
   colour. Which direction, and can you predict it before you see it?
2. **Does the constant change?** This is the question that separates
   people who understand equilibrium from people who have memorised a
   phrase. When a system shifts, has $K_c$ changed, or has the system
   moved to a different point on the *same* $K_c$? The two disturbances
   you will see today do not give the same answer.
3. **Can you measure a value of an equilibrium constant at all?** Part 2
   asks you to get one — an actual number, from your own data, for a
   real system.

## Part 1 — Systems you disturb yourself

**The carbon dioxide system**, which you have been running inside
yourself all day:

$$\ce{CO2(aq) + H2O(l) <=> H2CO3(aq) <=> H+(aq) + HCO3-(aq)}$$

Distilled water with a few drops of bromothymol blue, which is green
near neutral, yellow when acidic, and blue when basic. You have at least
three ways to disturb it: add carbon dioxide by bubbling exhaled air
through it, remove carbon dioxide by warming it gently or leaving it to
stand, and remove hydrogen ions with a drop of very dilute sodium
hydroxide. **You design which disturbances you apply and in what
order.**

**The calcium hydroxide system**, for a disturbance of a different kind:

$$\ce{Ca(OH)2(s) <=> Ca^2+(aq) + 2OH-(aq)}$$

Start with a clear, filtered, saturated solution — limewater — and add
a solution containing one of the ions already in it. Predict what you
will see before you add anything, because "nothing" is a defensible
prediction here and it is wrong.

## Part 1 — Systems I demonstrate

**Iron(III) thiocyanate**, which is the classic and which is mine:

$$\ce{Fe^3+(aq) + SCN-(aq) <=> FeSCN^2+(aq)}$$

Pale yellow and colourless on the left, deep blood-red on the right, so
the position of the equilibrium is visible from the back of the room. I
will add iron(III), then thiocyanate, then a reagent that removes
iron(III) from solution, and you will predict each one first.

**Sealed tubes of dinitrogen tetroxide and nitrogen dioxide**, for
temperature:

$$\ce{N2O4(g) <=> 2NO2(g)}$$

Two identical sealed tubes, one in iced water and one in hot water. This
is the tube from [[Dynamic Equilibrium]], and today it is going to do
something that none of the concentration disturbances do.

## What you have to work with

- Distilled water, **bromothymol blue** indicator, drinking straws or a
  small aquarium pump, a hot water bath.
- **Sodium hydroxide solution, 0.1 mol/L** — very dilute, and one drop
  at a time.
- **Filtered saturated calcium hydroxide solution**, and solutions of
  calcium chloride and sodium hydroxide.
- **Ethanoic acid at a stated concentration** and a calibrated pH probe,
  for Part 2.
- Test tubes, a rack, a white tile, a stirring rod, and a wash bottle.

Your design decisions, written before the lab:

- **Which disturbances you will apply, in which order**, and what
  control you will run alongside each. A tube of the same solution left
  alone on the rack is the control, and without it "it went yellow" is
  not evidence — solutions change colour over a period for reasons that
  have nothing to do with you.
- **How you will make the disturbance small.** One drop, not five. A
  large disturbance can drive a system so far that you cannot tell a
  shift from a completely different reaction.
- **What you will hold constant.** Volume, temperature, indicator
  quantity, and lighting — colour judgements are notoriously sensitive
  to the last of those, which is what the white tile is for.
- **How you will record a colour honestly.** Against a colour chart,
  against a control tube, or photographed beside a reference. "Darker"
  is not a measurement, and everybody's "darker" is different.

> [!danger] A thiocyanate salt, sealed tubes of a toxic gas, and one straw
> - **Thiocyanate salts are toxic.** No skin contact, no transfer with
>   bare hands, and nothing containing thiocyanate goes down the sink —
>   it goes to the labelled container, and I dispose of it. This is one
>   of the reasons the iron(III) demonstration is a demonstration.
> - **Nothing acidic goes anywhere near the thiocyanate.** Adding a
>   strong acid to a thiocyanate solution can generate hydrogen cyanide.
>   The demonstration is set up so that it cannot happen, which means
>   nobody adds anything to my tray, brings a tube over, or "just tries
>   one drop". If you want a disturbance tested, ask me and I will do
>   it.
> - **The sealed gas tubes contain nitrogen dioxide, which is severely
>   toxic**, and they are pressurised when hot. I handle them, behind a
>   screen, away from the edge of the bench, with a tray underneath. You
>   watch from the marked line. If a tube ever breaks, the room is
>   evacuated immediately and that is not a drill.
> - **You blow OUT through the straw and you never draw in.** This is
>   the one activity all semester where a tube goes near a mouth, and the

>   straw in the bin afterwards, and if there is any chance you will
>   inhale, use the pump instead. **Never pipette by mouth** still means
>   exactly what it always meant.
> - **Sodium hydroxide does not hurt at first.** Even at 0.1 mol/L it
>   feels soapy rather than painful — that sensation is the solution
>   attacking the fats in your skin — and a base burn goes **deeper**

>   whether or not it hurts. Same for limewater, which is a hydroxide

>   with a dye in it. **Waft, never sniff.** Eye protection from the
>   first move to the last of the cleanup.
> - **Hot water baths scald and hot glassware looks like cold
>   glassware.** Tongs, and watch it cool before you believe it is cool.
> - **Nothing returns to a stock bottle**, and every waste stream goes

not erase a row afterwards. The last column is the one that separates a

Then, separately, one sentence: **what would have to happen for you to
decide your rule was wrong?** If you cannot answer that, you have
written a description rather than a prediction.

## Part 2 — Measuring a value of an equilibrium constant

Predicting a direction is qualitative. Getting a number is not, and E2.3
wants a number.

Ethanoic acid in water is an equilibrium like any other:

$$\ce{CH3COOH(aq) + H2O(l) <=> CH3COO-(aq) + H3O+(aq)}$$

You know the concentration you started with, because it is on the
bottle. A pH probe tells you $[\ce{H3O+}]$ at equilibrium.
An ICE table gets you from those two facts to everything else, and then
the expression gets you a constant.

| | $\ce{CH3COOH}$ | $\ce{CH3COO-}$ | $\ce{H3O+}$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Initial | your stated concentration | 0 | about 0 |
| Change | $-x$ | $+x$ | $+x$ |
| Equilibrium | initial $- x$ | $x$ | $x$ |

Your $x$ comes straight from the pH, because
$[\ce{H3O+}] = 10^{-\text{pH}}$ — and the logarithm work, if
it is rusty, is in
[[Working with Logarithms in Chemistry]]. Then

$$K_a = \frac{[\ce{CH3COO-}][\ce{H3O+}]}{[\ce{CH3COOH}]}$$

Do it at **two different starting concentrations**. If $K_a$ really is a
constant, the two runs should give the same value even though the pH
values are different — and that, not the number itself, is the result.

Calibrate the probe before you use it, record the buffers you calibrated
against, and rinse the probe with distilled water between solutions. An
uncalibrated probe gives beautifully repeatable readings that are all
wrong together.

Carry full precision through the calculation and round once at the end.
A pH read to 0.01 gives you about two significant figures in
$[\ce{H3O+}]$ — and it is worth understanding why, because it
is the most common place a logarithm quietly destroys precision. See

## What to bring to the consolidation

- Your completed prediction table, unedited, with the wrong rows still
  legible.
- Your observation table, with the control tube recorded every time.
- **Your hit rate.** How many of the nine predictions did you get right
  before seeing anything? A number, not an impression.
- **The one disturbance that behaves differently from all the others**,
  and what is different about it. There is exactly one, and finding it
  is the finding.
- **Your two values of $K_a$**, and the percentage difference between
  them. Then the harder question: is that difference larger or smaller
  than what your pH resolution alone would produce?
- **The class table of $K_a$ values**, on the board. Look at whether the
  scatter is centred or lopsided, and compare with the accepted value in
  the data booklet.

## What you should not claim

- **A shift is not a change in $K_c$.** Adding more of a reactant moves
  a system to a new set of concentrations that satisfy the **same**
  constant. Only one of the disturbances you saw today changes the
  constant itself, and the write-up must say which and why. Getting this
  backwards is the single most common error in the whole unit.
- **You did not measure a shift — you observed a colour.** Colour is
  evidence about concentration and it is not a measurement of it. "The
  red deepened" supports "more $\ce{FeSCN^2+}$"; it does not support
  a number, a percentage, or a ratio, and it certainly does not support
  a $K_c$.
- **Your eyes adapt, and the error has a direction.** Stare at a red
  tube for twenty seconds and the next one looks paler. Judging a
  sequence of tubes in the order you disturbed them biases you toward
  seeing each successive change as smaller than the last. Compare each
  tube against the untouched control rather than against the previous
  tube.
- **A prediction that "worked" for the wrong reason is not a
  success.** If you predicted a shift to the right because "adding
  reactant always shifts right", and the system agreed, you have learned
  nothing about whether your reasoning is sound. The rows worth
  discussing are the ones where a classmate got the same answer by a
  different argument.[^1]
- **Your $K_a$ assumes things you did not check.** It assumes the bottle
  concentration is right, that the acid is the only source of hydronium
  in the solution, that the probe is calibrated, and that $x$ is small
  enough for the arithmetic you did. Each of those is defensible and
  none of them is verified. If your two runs disagree badly, one of
  those four assumptions is the place to look.
- **Two runs are not a spread.** They tell you whether you can repeat
  yourself once. Report both values; do not average them and quote three
  figures.

[^1]: Le Châtelier's principle — the name the next class attaches to
    what you saw — is a **heuristic**, and like the electron-counting
    rule in [[Building and Testing Models]] it has an edge. It predicts
    a **direction** and never a **magnitude**: it will not tell you
    whether the shift is 1% or 90%, and only the equilibrium constant
    can. It says nothing whatever about **how long** the shift takes,
    which is a rate question and a different unit. And it has cases it
    gets wrong if applied carelessly — pumping an unreactive gas such as
    argon into a fixed-volume vessel raises the total pressure and
    shifts nothing at all, because none of the concentrations in the
    expression changed. A rule that predicts eight things out of nine is
    worth having. A rule you cannot say the limits of is worth much
    less.
