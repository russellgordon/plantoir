Every task page carries a table of criteria, published on the day the
task launches. Most people read it once, at launch, and then hand in a
piece that misses two rows they could have dealt with in half a studio
period. This page is that half period.

We do it together the first time, on somebody else's study, so that the
move is not new to you when the work on the table is yours.
[[Assessing Your Own Work]] is the harder judgement underneath this
one — whether the piece does what you meant it to. This page is
narrower and more mechanical, and you can do it when you are too tired
to do the other.

## The routine

1. Put the task page's criteria table beside the actual object. Not
   beside your memory of it, and not beside the photograph — the thing
   itself, at the distance a viewer will stand.
2. Take the rows in order. For each one write **yes**, **not yet**, or
   **partly**, and one sentence of evidence: where on the piece a
   viewer would see it. "Yes" with nothing pointed at is a hope.
3. Find your weakest row. Not the easiest one to fix — the weakest.
4. Write one sentence saying what you will change, and in which period.
5. Change it, in the time the schedule gives you before the hand-in.
   This is why the self-check is never set for the day something is
   due: a judgement you have no time to act on is only a worry.

Steps 2 and 3 are the whole exercise. Naming your weakest row honestly
is harder than any of the fixing, because it means writing down the
thing you have been hoping nobody would mention.

> [!example]- One row, judged honestly
> From [[The Elements Study]]: *"The element — clearly dominant, and
> pushed rather than mentioned."*
>
> **Partly.** In the value study the darks go to about a middle grey
> and stop, so from two metres away the whole thing reads as one tone
> and the element is present rather than dominant. The texture study
> passes this row; the value one does not. Weakest row.
> **Change:** take the three darkest areas down with a softer pencil in
> Thursday's studio period, then look at it again from the back of the
> room.
>
> That entry is worth more than "looks good" written beside five rows,
> and it took less time.

## Two things that make it useless

**Seeing what you meant instead of what is there.** You know what the
piece was supposed to be, and your eye keeps supplying it, so a row you
have not met reads as met. This is the one place the two pages overlap:
[[Assessing Your Own Work]] has the fixes, and they work here
unchanged. Use them before you start, not after you disagree with
yourself.

**Grading yourself.** This is not a mark and it never becomes one. If
you find yourself deciding whether the piece is a seventy or an eighty,
you have stopped doing the exercise — the question is only whether a
row is met, and where.

## Where this fits

Your judgement of your own work is not part of your mark, and neither
is a classmate's — [[How Marks Work]] says why. That cuts both ways:
nothing you write here can cost you anything, which is exactly what
makes it worth writing honestly.

What it changes is the work. By the second unit most people find the
same two rows coming up every time, which is more useful than any
single fix — that is the thing to work on for the rest of the year, and
it belongs in your sketchbook where you will meet it again.
