> [!abstract] At a glance
> Groups of three to five · four class periods, then performed ·
> a staged scene of 60–100 lines plus a one-page director's note ·
> assessed on Communication, Thinking, and Application

## The task

Take one scene of *Romeo and Juliet*, decide what it means, and stage it
so the audience can see your decision. Then defend every choice in
writing.

## What "staging" means here

You are not required to act well. You are required to make choices and
execute them:

- **Cuts.** You may cut lines. Every cut is an argument about what the
  scene is for.
- **Blocking.** Where people stand, who moves toward whom, who is left
  alone on stage.
- **Delivery.** Which words carry the meaning — see
  [[Shakespeare's Language]] for how the verse tells you.
- **Setting and props**, if you want them. A scene set now is a claim
  about the play's relevance; a scene set in 1595 is also a claim.
- **Faces and hands.** Where the eyes go, what the hands are doing, who
  is looked at and who is pointedly not. In a room this size these carry
  as much as the words do, and they are the half of a performance an
  audience reads without noticing.
- **Silence.** The most under-used tool available to you.

## The director's note

One page, and it is where most of the marks are. You draft it after the
rehearsal and finish it in class on the two performance days, so the piece
that carries your individual mark is not written at midnight:

1. **Your reading of the scene** in one sentence.
2. **Three choices you made** and what each one shows.
3. **One line you cut** and the argument for cutting it.
4. **What you would do differently with another two weeks.**
5. **Two things you can already do as a reader or a writer** that made
   this scene better to listen to — and where each of them shows.

## How it is marked

| Category | What I am looking for |
| --- | --- |
| Communication | Your part of the scene reaches the audience: the lines you speak, or the choice you were responsible for, arrives with its sense intact — and what your face, hands and eyes are doing agrees with it |
| Thinking | The staging is an interpretation, defended in the note with evidence from the text |
| Application | The choices use what we learned about dramatic form, not just enthusiasm, and the note names two reading or writing skills you brought to the staging |

> [!important] There is no group mark on this task
> The staging belongs to the group; the mark belongs to you. What I judge
> is your own director's note, written and handed in individually, and
> the choices you carried out on stage. A group that falls apart cannot
> pull your mark down with it, and a group that carries you cannot lift
> it.

## If performing frightens you

There is a role in every group that is not on stage: director, dramaturg
(the person who works out what the lines mean), or designer. All three
are assessed on the same note. Come and talk to me in the first period of
the unit.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

Neither slot is a performance day. On Days 13 and 14 your attention is
owed to the group in front of the class, and what you would be writing
down is the staging itself — which arrives anyway, live, and is the
product.

OBSERVE — Unit 2, Day 8, the cuts period, while each group paraphrases
its own scene
  Watch for: what happens in the ten seconds after somebody in a group
  says they cannot read a line. A cut list cannot tell you: a line cut for
  meaning and a line cut because nobody could read it look identical on
  the page, and both arrive with a reason written beside them.
  Going well: a decoding strategy, and three are in play this period — a
  paraphrase offered and tested against the next line, a glossary or
  dictionary opened, the two lines either side read to infer the middle
  one. Those three are B3.2 being used.
  A fourth move is legitimate and is NOT decoding: marking the line to
  bring to me, which is what the day's Paraphrase Practice set asks for.
  It keeps the line in the scene, so count it as tried — but do not
  record it as B3.2. A group that marks everything has deferred the
  reading rather than done it, and that is worth knowing too.
  Stuck: the line leaves the scene inside those ten seconds with nothing
  tried at all. Also: a group whose cuts are all the hard passages and
  whose keeps are all the famous ones.
  Record: your group list, three columns — decoded it, marked it for me,
  cut it unread. One tick per group per difficult line; four ticks will
  be plenty.
  Evidence for B3.2: using appropriate decoding strategies to read and
  understand unfamiliar words, which in a Shakespeare scene is most of the
  reading. The director's note names one cut and defends it; it will never
  mention the eleven that went because the words were hard.

TALK — Unit 2, Day 10, while you go round the groups in the blocking
period, which that agenda already sends you to do
  Leave the note that period is for, then take one more minute.
  Ask: "Where in this scene do two of you disagree about what is
  happening — and which of you is the staging currently agreeing with?"
  Then: "Somebody in the back row misses half the words. Which lines must
  they hear, and what are you doing to your voices to make sure of it?"
  A strong answer to the first names the line and admits the staging has
  taken a side; a group that says it agrees about everything has not read
  the scene twice. A strong answer to the second names lines and then
  names what the voices will do to them — slower here, quieter on this
  half-line so the next one lands, one word pitched up because the verse
  turns on it. That is the IDENTIFYING half of A2.5: identifying a
  variety of vocal strategies, tone, pace, pitch and volume among them.
  The using half, and the sensitivity to what this particular audience
  needs, is what you will see on the night; only this minute tells you
  whether any of it was chosen rather than arrived at.
  Record: one line per group on your blocking round sheet, and a star
  beside any group that named a disagreement rather than denying one.

The products arrive on their own: the performance on Day 13 or Day 14,
and the director's note handed in at the end of Day 14.
