## What you are making

The culminating task. The lab opens its doors to invited guests —
family, staff, another class — and each student presents **one
artifact** from the course, technician-style, at their own bench: the
build sheet from [[The Build Sheet]], the network binder from
[[The Network Job]], the gadget from [[The Gadget]], or the machine
and verdict from [[The Refurb Report]].

Technician-style means four things, in order: what it is, how it
works, what went wrong on the way, and the service documentation that
proves all three. "What went wrong" is not a confession — the fault
you traced is the strongest proof you understand the system. Beside
the artifact stands a short **growth statement**: who you were at the
first bench period, who you are now, evidenced from your
[[Tech Journal]] — and, in two sentences, the two work habits from the
Ontario Skills Passport list that this term actually built in you, each
with an entry behind it.

## How to work

1. Choose your artifact — not the one that went smoothest, but the one
   whose story you can tell best. The two are rarely the same.
2. Gather everything the artifact generated — diagrams, code, photos,
   test results, journal entries — into one organised portfolio
   folder, sensibly named and backed up in a second place. One copy
   on one machine is a failure mode you know how to diagnose.
3. Write the growth statement, in class, on Day 15. [[Showing Growth]]
   shows how to pair a then-entry with a now-entry and
   [[Journal Checklist]] helps you mine the year; the
   [[Final Reflection]] in step 6 grows straight out of it.
4. Rehearse with a partner playing a guest who knows nothing — then
   with one playing a guest who knows everything. Both will come.
5. Stage the bench the period before, so a guest can look at everything
   and reach into nothing. Then showcase evening: artifact on the bench,
   documentation open, talk ready, questions welcome. Powered demos run
   the [[Safety in the Lab]] habits in front of guests exactly as on a
   Tuesday.
6. Afterwards, the writing: your [[Final Reflection]] is drafted in
   class on the day we debrief the evening, and finished in class in
   our last period, journal open beside you. It is the long form of the
   growth statement, and it is part of this task, not an afterword.

Every bench period is class time on purpose: work that carries a mark
gets done here, where I can watch it happen and you can ask. Day 14 is
a conference with nothing written down — the fault story, out loud. The
checkpoint is Day 15, once the documentation, the staged bench and a
draft growth statement exist to be read: I leave you one written note against
the criteria table below, and Day 16 opens with fifteen minutes for
acting on it.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| A working narrative | What it is, how it works, what went wrong |
| Proof on the bench | Guests can check any claim in the open package |
| An organised portfolio | Files gathered, sensibly named, backed up in a second place |
| An honest fault story | The thing that went wrong is told with its diagnosis |
| Evidence of growth | Early journal entries paired with late ones |
| Habits named with evidence | Two Skills Passport habits, each with an entry behind it |
| A reflection that cites itself | Two dated entries behind each claim, and one named route out of this course |
| A bench staged safely | Set up so a guest can look at everything and reach into nothing, power off before any panel moves |

## Reflect

The growth statement is the reflection, and it outlives the evening:
it opens your [[Final Reflection]] and starts the portfolio the
[[Careers in Computer Technology|people who hire technicians]] will
one day ask to see.

> [!success]- If presenting makes your stomach drop (click to expand)
> You are not performing — you are giving a tour of work that already
> exists. Every question a guest can ask is about something sitting on
> your bench, built and documented by you. Rehearse the first minute
> until it is automatic; the artifact carries the rest.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

This is the thirty per cent, so it is the one place where thin evidence
costs the most — and showcase evening is no use to you for gathering
any, because you will be steering guests and watching the power bars.
Everything below happens before it, and so does the safe-bench row: it
is judged at staging on Day 15, not from the floor on the night.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 15, the documentation-and-backup period
  Watch for: whether the second copy is ever OPENED. Making a backup
  and having a backup are different things, and only one of them is
  visible from a folder listing. Watch also whether files get their
  names as they are copied or in a panicky rename pass at the end —
  the finished portfolio reports the names but not the order they
  arrived in, so this corroborates what the listing already claims.
  Going well: a student opens a photo from the second copy, sees it
  open, and closes it without being asked to prove anything.
  Stuck: a copy dragged onto a drive nobody has opened, and forty files
  still called whatever the camera called them.
  Record: initials on the showcase floor plan, ticked when you see a
  file opened from the second copy — you are walking the benches for the
  checkpoint note anyway, so this costs you nothing extra.
  That is B1.3. A backed-up portfolio and a portfolio somebody has
  restored from look identical until the day one of them is needed.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 14, the conference already on that agenda
  Ask: "Which route out of this course does this artifact actually
  argue for — and what would you have to add to it before it argued
  for a different one?"
  Then: "Everything on your bench is evidence of something. Point at
  the piece a stranger would misread, and tell me what it really
  shows."
  A strong first answer separates the routes that start straight out of
  secondary school from the ones that need college, apprenticeship or
  more — that is D3.1 spoken, and a bench of hardware cannot show you
  whether a student knows where it leads. A strong second answer names
  a piece and the skill behind it: the fault log that looks like a
  record of failure and is in fact the diagnosis, which is D3.6 —
  choosing materials because of what they evidence.
  Record: two ticks per student on the seating plan, one per question,
  plus the route they named in one word.
  A whole class of solo stations will not fit in one period. Take the
  students you did not reach on Day 16, while the talks are being
  rehearsed bench to bench, and date each name so you can see who is
  still missing.

The product evidence is the artifact, the documentation, the talk on
Day 17, and the reflection handed in on the last day. Those arrive on
their own.
