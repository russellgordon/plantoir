An actor doesn't walk into rehearsal hoping the right pace and emphasis
happen naturally. They mark the script first — in pencil, before they ever
say the lines out loud — so the choices are made once, deliberately, and
then rehearsed rather than improvised fresh every read-through.

## The marks

A small, consistent set of symbols does almost everything you need:

- **Underline** a word to mark the stressed word in the line — the one a
  listener should hear land hardest.
- **/** for a short pause, **//** for a longer one.
- **↑** or **↓** above a word for pitch rising or falling — a question
  that genuinely doesn't know the answer rises; a statement that's really
  a threat often falls.
- **CAPITALS** or a bracket around a phrase for a shift in volume, up or
  down.
- A wavy line under a phrase you're still deciding how to deliver —
  unresolved, on purpose, until you've tried it a few ways out loud.

None of these are fixed rules from a textbook. They're a private notation
— what matters is that you use the same marks consistently enough that you
can read your own script back a week later and remember what you meant.

## A worked example

> [!success]- Click to see a marked-up excerpt
> **To be, or not to be**— / **that** is the question: /
> Whether 'tis nobler in the mind to suffer
> The slings and arrows of outrageous fortune, ↑
> Or to take arms against a sea of troubles //
> And by opposing end them.
>
> The underlines mark where the stress falls — not automatically the
> first word of the line. The single slash after "not to be" is a short
> pause, letting the question sit before Hamlet restates it. The double
> slash after "troubles" is a longer pause — the line before it turns
> over an entire plan, and the pause gives an audience room to feel the
> weight of what's being decided.

## Marking before you rehearse, not during

Do this pass on your own, quietly, before your first read-through with a
scene partner — deciding the choices in advance means rehearsal time goes
toward trying them out and adjusting, not toward figuring out what the
line even means for the first time in front of someone else.
[[To Be Heard]] gives you a chance to hear how other students marked and
delivered the same soliloquy, which is often the fastest way to notice a
choice you didn't consider. When [[Staging the Unseen]] launches, this is
the first thing you'll do with your assigned scene.
