You have already rescued a buried lede — in [[Lead Paragraph Practice]]
you took a draft that opened with the weather at the game and made the
reader wait four paragraphs for the final score. Hauling that score to
the top felt almost rude. It was journalism.

## Most important first

A news story is shaped like a pyramid balanced on its point: the most
important fact first, then supporting detail, then background, then the
material only a completist will love. Two forces built that shape, and
both are still at work:

- **Readers leave.** Most people read the first sentence; fewer read
  the second; almost nobody reads the tenth. Whatever you put last, you
  have chosen to tell almost no one.
- **Editors cut from the bottom.** When a story must lose ten lines to
  fit the page, the cut comes from the end, fast, with no time to
  rewrite. A story built top-down survives the cut still making sense.

The shape is also a courtesy. It lets a reader stop anywhere and still
leave with the story — which honours the deal behind every judgement in
[[News Values]]: their limited attention, traded for something worth
knowing, no padding first.

> [!example] The same fact, buried and rescued
> **Buried:** On Thursday the senior volleyball team travelled to
> Riverside for a match that had been rescheduled twice. The gym was
> packed and loud. In the fifth set, the team won its first
> championship in nine years.
>
> **Rescued:** The senior volleyball team won its first championship in
> nine years on Thursday, taking a fifth set at Riverside in a match
> that had been rescheduled twice.

## Writing the lede

The lede is the tip of the pyramid: one sentence, usually under thirty
words, that answers *who*, *what*, *when*, and *where* — and hints at
why anyone should care. Not everything. The one thing. If the rest of
your story vanished, a reader holding only the lede should still know
what happened.

Write the lede last if you have to. Reporters routinely discover their
real lede sitting in their final paragraph, because writing is how they
worked out what mattered. Move it up — the reader should not have to do
that digging. It is the first thing your editor will check in
[[Your First Byline]], and the habit underneath everything in
[[Writing for Publication]].
