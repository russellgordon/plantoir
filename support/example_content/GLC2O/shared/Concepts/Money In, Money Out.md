Personal finance is mostly one habit: knowing what comes in, knowing
what goes out, and not letting the second quietly overtake the first.
Everything else on this page is a tool for making that easier or a trap
that makes it harder.

## Money in

Income is what arrives, and it is smaller than what you earned. Gross
pay is the number on the job ad; net pay is what lands, after income
tax, Canada Pension Plan contributions, and Employment Insurance
premiums come off. The first time you see a pay stub this is a shock,
and it should not be a surprise — read the deduction lines once and you
will understand every pay stub you ever get.

> [!quiz] A pay stub shows gross pay of \$400 and deductions of \$62. Which amount belongs on the income line of your budget?
> - [ ] \$400
> - [x] \$338
> - [ ] \$462
> - [ ] \$62
>
> > [!explain]- Why
> > Net pay is what lands: gross pay minus the deductions, and 400 less
> > 62 is 338. A budget built on gross pay comes up short by the
> > deductions every payday.

Income can also include grants, bursaries, scholarships, awards, and
support from family or community. Some of that is money you keep and
some is money you repay, which is the distinction that matters most.

## Money out, and where to keep it

Saving is easier when the money is somewhere you do not see daily.
Chequing accounts are for money moving through; savings accounts pay
some interest and are for money standing still. Banks and credit unions
both offer them — a credit union is owned by its members rather than by
shareholders, which sometimes shows up in fees. Registered accounts such
as a TFSA change how the interest is taxed rather than where the money
sits. Compare the fee against the interest before you are impressed by
the interest: an account paying a little while charging \$5 a month is
losing you money, and both numbers are on the institution's own site.

## Borrowing, compared honestly

Borrowing is not automatically a mistake. It is a trade: money now, more
money later. What separates the forms is how much more later, and what
happens if you cannot pay.

- **Student loans**, federal and provincial, including OSAP. Designed
  for this purpose, with repayment that starts after study.
- **Bank or credit union loans and lines of credit.** Lower cost than
  cards, and they require qualifying.
- **Credit cards.** Useful, and expensive if the balance is not cleared.
  Paying the full statement amount each month builds a credit record and
  costs nothing extra; paying the minimum is where people get stuck.
- **Payday loans.** Very high cost for very short terms. Legal, and a
  last resort.
- **Family or community lending.** Flexible, and the cost of not repaying
  is a relationship rather than interest.

> [!tip] Never quote a rate from a page like this one
> Interest rates, fees, and loan limits change, and any figure written
> into a lesson is out of date before it is taught. The skill being
> assessed is finding today's number at the source — the institution,
> the government program, the posting — and saying where you found it.
> You will do exactly that in [[The Budget]].
