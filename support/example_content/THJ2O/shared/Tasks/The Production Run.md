**Launched mid-Unit 3, delivered at the end.** Small groups, with an
individual mark.

A batch of something, produced to a specification, on a schedule, for a
real recipient — the school, a community garden, a plant sale, or the
site project in Unit 4.

The batch is the group's; the mark is yours. **You hold a named station
on the line, you keep your own timing and materials figures for it, and
you write your own review — section 5 below — on your own.** Nobody
here receives a mark for a batch somebody else produced.

## The work

1. **Specify.** What, how many, to what standard, by when.
2. **Design the process.** Steps in order, who does what, what equipment
   is needed, and where the bottleneck will be — using the design and
   problem-solving process from
   [[Design and Problem Solving in the Green Industries]].
3. **Run it**, recording time, materials, and losses.
4. **Deliver**, on the date promised, to the standard specified.
5. **Review.** What the run actually cost in time and materials, what
   you would change, and what an innovation in this sector — automation,
   controlled environment growing, a new propagation technology — would
   do to your process, your costs, and the people doing the work.

## What is marked

| Criterion | The standard |
| --- | --- |
| Process design | A sequence that works, with the bottleneck identified in advance |
| Execution | The batch meets the specification |
| Delivery | On time, in a state the recipient accepts |
| Data | Real numbers for time, materials, and losses |
| Analysis | The innovation question answered with reasoning, including who gains and who loses |

Process design, execution, delivery and data all describe one batch
made by a group, so each is judged on your own station: the sequence
you designed for it, the units that came off it, your timings, and what
you can say about the process when I ask you on your own. The analysis
row is your writing already.

> [!warning] A production run is judged by the last unit, not the best one
> Anyone can make one good pot. Consistency across the batch is the
> actual skill, and it is what a grower is paid for.

[[Systems That Do the Work]] covers the equipment; the review section
draws on what recent innovations have done to this sector's work and
employment.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 3, Day 8, setting up the line
  Watch for: whether a handful of units is pushed through the whole
  sequence first, or every station is perfected before anything moves.
  The first group finds its bottleneck inside ten minutes; the second
  finds it in the last ten, with the batch half made and no time to use
  what it has just learned.
  Then watch what happens to the process sheet when a queue builds. A
  line that is re-balanced while it runs is B1.3 — competence in
  applying a production process — and it is the half of B1.3 the
  delivered batch cannot show you, because a batch that met spec by
  luck and one that met it by adjustment arrive in the same crate.
  (A3.1 is a "describe" expectation, so it is not what you are watching
  here; it belongs in the conversation below and in the written review.)
  Going well: something comes off the end of the line badly, early, and
  the sheet gets a line through it.
  Stuck: four people at one station, none at the next, and a process
  sheet identical to the one drawn on Day 7.
  Record: write on your day plan the clock time each group's first
  finished unit appeared. One number per group, and it predicts most of
  what follows.

TALK — Unit 3, Day 13, at the conferences already on that agenda, each
group member on their own
  The period measures consistency across the batch, so whether they are
  consistent is already answered by the time you sit down. Ask what
  DECIDES it instead. Do not ask the innovation question either —
  section 5 of the task page prints it, and they have had it since
  Day 7.
  Ask: "Which single step decides whether the batch is consistent — and
  what happens to that step if the order doubles?"
  Then: "Show me where your process sheet stopped matching what you are
  actually doing."
  A strong answer points at a real divergence, says when it happened,
  and says what made it necessary. That is A3.1 — describing the steps
  of the process as applied — which is a "describe" expectation and so
  belongs in conversation, and it is the one thing a tidy final process
  sheet is guaranteed to hide.
  Record: a name and a sentence, station by station down the day plan.
  Four people made that batch and four separate marks come out of it.

The product evidence is the delivered batch on Day 16 and the review
handed in on Day 17.
