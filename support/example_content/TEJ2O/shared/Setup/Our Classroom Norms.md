This course hands you tools, components, and machines — and asks you
to be confused in public while holding them. That takes norms — and
they bind everyone, including your teacher.

> [!danger] The core agreement
> Nobody's dead bench gets laughed at. Every technician you would
> trust has killed a component, crossed a wire, and asked for help —
> that is how the trade is learned.

## What we agree to

- **Predict before you power.** Saying out loud what should happen —
  and being wrong — is the fastest way to learn to diagnose.
- **Symptoms are information.** Beeps, lights, and error screens are
  the machine being as helpful as it knows how to be; we read them
  before we groan at them.
- **Roles rotate.** Hands, reader, recorder — the screwdriver changes
  hands every lab, no exceptions for "but I'm faster".
- **Your accounts are yours; theirs are theirs.** Nobody touches a
  machine someone else is signed into. Lock your screen when you step
  away — it protects you, and it is the habit
  [[Security and Ethics|the real world runs on]].
- **Honest about help.** A classmate's fix, a guide you followed, an
  AI assistant's suggestion — all fine WHEN NAMED, in your journal and
  your write-ups, the way professionals credit sources. Passing off
  someone else's work as your own thinking is the one unforgivable
  fault.
- **Technology serves people.** When we discuss e-waste, security,
  and [[The Trades Are Tech|whose work counts]], we argue with ideas,
  never at people.

## Why this is not just being nice

A lab that shares half-broken builds learns faster, because faults
get found while they are cheap to fix — which is precisely how real
shops work. The habits above are not school rules with a tech
coat of paint; they are the profession's own working norms, learned
early.
