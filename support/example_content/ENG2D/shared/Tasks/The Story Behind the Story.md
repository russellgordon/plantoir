> [!abstract] At a glance
> Individual · five class periods · a short story of 800–1200 words plus
> a 300-word craft note · assessed on Application, Thinking, and
> Communication

## The task

Write a short story. Then write about how you built it.

The story is judged as a story. The craft note is judged as analysis of
your own work — and it is the part that makes this an English task
rather than a creative-writing exercise.

## The story

Constraints, because constraints are what make a short story a short
story:

- **One central character** who wants something.
- **A time frame under a week.** Preferably under a day.
- **A turn** — something changes: a decision, an understanding, a
  refusal.
- **No more than three named characters.**
- **A last sentence you chose**, not the one you stopped at.

## The craft note

Three hundred words on the decisions you made and why:

- Where did you start the story, and what did you leave out?
- Whose perspective, and what did that choice cost you? See
  [[Narrative Perspective]].
- One technique you took from a story we read — name the story and what
  you adapted.
- What did the ending do that the beginning set up?
- **Two things you can now do as a writer** — name them — and what each
  one has changed about what you notice when you read somebody else's
  story.

## How it is marked

| Category | What I am looking for |
| --- | --- |
| Application | The story uses the techniques from the unit deliberately, and the writing is controlled |
| Thinking | The craft note explains choices, not intentions — what is on the page and why — and names two skills you now have as a writer and what each one changes about how you read |
| Communication | The story holds a reader; the note is precise and readable |

> [!tip] Where these usually go wrong
> Too much time. A story covering two years in 1000 words becomes a
> summary with dialogue in it. Choose the afternoon everything turned on,
> and let the reader infer the rest — that is what
> [[Short Stories]] shows five different writers doing.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 1, Day 14, the revision period, while the middle third is
read aloud to a partner
  This is not evidence the product hides — the criteria table already
  judges the words on the page, and its Application row asks whether the
  writing is controlled. What the period adds is a check on that
  judgement, and the cheapest one available: the story shows you the words
  that survived and cannot show you whether they were chosen or were
  simply the first ones that turned up.
  Watch for: where the pen moves. A writer who is really hearing it stops
  mid-line, at the word the mouth tripped on, and changes that word. A
  writer who is performing the exercise reads the page at speed and edits
  with their eyes afterwards, if at all.
  Going well: a crossing-out made while the partner is still listening;
  somebody who starts a sentence again from a different verb.
  Stuck: a page read fluently in forty seconds and handed back clean, or
  a listener who is correcting punctuation.
  Record: class list, three columns — changed a word while reading,
  changed nothing, never got to the reading. Thirty seconds.
  Evidence for C2.3 — using words and phrases that make writing clear and
  vivid for a reader — corroborated rather than discovered, which is worth
  the thirty seconds because a writer who will not change a word under
  their own voice will not change one under time in the examination room
  either.

TALK — Unit 1, Day 13, the conference already on that agenda
  The agenda promises one question about your opening and your last
  paragraph. Ask it, then keep them for a minute. You will not reach
  everybody in one period; the rest come to you at the start of Day 14,
  while the room revises, and that agenda says so. Nothing from the craft
  note's own five prompts — they write answers to those in class tomorrow.
  Ask: "Whose side is this story on — and where does the writing show
  that, rather than say it?"
  Then: "If I read this to the class without saying whose it is, what
  would give you away?"
  A strong answer to the first names a sentence: a detail given to one
  character and withheld from another, an ending that lets somebody off.
  That is C2.5 heard — explaining how their own beliefs and values show
  up in their writing — and it is a thing very few fifteen-year-olds will
  write down in a craft note they know is being marked. A strong answer
  to the second names something particular — a place they know, a turn of
  phrase, the thing they always end up writing about — rather than "my
  handwriting". It is the same expectation from the other side: a writer
  who can see their own fingerprints on the page has understood that the
  page has some.
  Record: two columns on the conference list — named a sentence, named a
  theme. The second column is who to sit with tomorrow.

The product arrives on its own: the story and the craft note, handed in
on Unit 1, Day 15.
