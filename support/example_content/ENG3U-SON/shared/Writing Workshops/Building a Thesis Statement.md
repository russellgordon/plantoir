A thesis statement is not the topic of your essay. It is the answer to a
question about that topic — specific, arguable, and sharp enough that
someone could disagree with it.

## What a thesis statement actually claims

"This essay is about growing up in *Boys and Girls*" is a topic. It tells a
reader what territory you're in, but it commits you to nothing. A thesis
commits: it says what you think happens to the narrator, and why that
matters, in one sentence a reader could push back on.

## Weak versus strong

> [!fail] Weak
> *Boys and Girls* is about a girl learning about gender roles on a farm.
>
> This is a summary wearing a thesis statement's clothes. It describes the
> setting and the general subject, but it makes no claim — nobody could
> argue with it, because it doesn't say anything specific enough to
> disagree with.

> [!success] Strong
> In "Boys and Girls," Munro shows that the narrator loses a battle she
> doesn't know she's fighting until her father's casual dismissal — "she's
> only a girl" — names the side she's already been assigned to.
>
> This one makes a claim: the loss happens *before* the narrator notices
> it, and a single line of dialogue is what reveals it to her. A reader
> could reasonably argue the turning point happens somewhere else in the
> story — which is exactly what makes this a thesis and not a summary.

## The three-step build

1. **Start with a question**, not a topic. Not *"What is 'Boys and Girls'
   about?"* but *"At what exact moment does the narrator stop being able to
   ignore what's expected of her?"*
2. **Answer it in one sentence.** Your answer is a claim, not a fact — it
   should be possible, in principle, for a reasonable reader to disagree.
3. **Say why it matters.** A thesis that only identifies a moment is half
   finished. Add what that moment reveals, changes, or costs — the "so
   what" that gives your reader a reason to keep going.

## Try it: from topic to thesis

| Step | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| Topic | Silence in "The Lottery" |
| Question | Why does nobody in the village say what everyone can plainly see? |
| Claim | The villagers' silence isn't ignorance — it's a choice they've made together, over and over, because breaking it would cost more than the ritual itself |
| Sharpened thesis | In "The Lottery," Jackson shows that the villagers' silence is not a failure to notice the ritual's cruelty but a collective agreement to protect it, because naming the cruelty out loud would make continuing it impossible |

Notice the sharpened version still fits in one sentence — length isn't what
makes a thesis strong, specificity is.
