A reader can stop, re-read a sentence, and look up a word. A listener
cannot — the sentence is already gone by the time they realize they missed
it. Writing for audio is a different craft from writing for the page, and
today you will practise it.

## Why writing for the ear is different

Compare these two openings to the same short piece:

- **For the page:** "Although the town's original water tower, built in
  1911 and decommissioned decades later after the plumbing beneath it
  finally failed, no longer holds water, it still holds the town's
  attention every summer, when local teenagers illegally climb it."
- **For the ear:** "There's an old water tower on the edge of town. It
  hasn't held water in decades. But every summer, teenagers still climb
  it."

Both openings are true and well written. Only one of them survives being
heard once, at listening speed, with no way to go back. The audio version
uses shorter sentences, puts one idea in each one, and saves the
complicating detail — *illegally*, *1911*, *decommissioned* — for later,
once the listener already has something to hold onto.

Three habits carry most of the difference:

- **Short sentences, one idea each.** A listener who is still processing
  your first clause has already lost your second one.
- **Signpost out loud.** A reader sees a new paragraph; a listener needs
  to be told — "here's the part that surprised me," "so how did it
  start."
- **Write the way you would say it**, contractions included. Formal
  written phrasing sounds stiff and oddly official read aloud, in a way it
  never looks on the page.

## Try it: the read-aloud test

Take a paragraph you have already written this term — a journal entry, a
piece from a Writing Workshop, even a few sentences from an essay draft.

1. Read it aloud to a partner, exactly as written.
2. Have your partner tell you, honestly, where they lost the thread or had
   to guess what you meant.
3. Rewrite the paragraph as something meant to be *heard*: shorter
   sentences, one idea per sentence, spoken-out-loud phrasing.
4. Read the new version aloud. Ask your partner the same question.

Most writers are surprised by how much changes once a sentence has to
survive being said out loud instead of read.[^1]

[^1]: Radio and podcast producers call this the difference between writing
    *for the eye* and writing *for the ear* — it is one of the oldest
    pieces of craft advice in broadcasting, long predating podcasts
    themselves.
