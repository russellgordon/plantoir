These questions follow [[Making Photographs]] and our rounds of
[[Caption This]]. A caption starts by answering who, what, and when.

## Questions

1. The photo: a player in mid-jump, arm drawn back to serve, during
   Thursday's regional volleyball final. You know she is Amara Osei,
   a Grade 11 setter. Write the caption.
2. **Fix this caption.** Same match, different photo: "A student
   holds a trophy." Name the three missing pieces, then rewrite it.
3. The photo was taken by Devan Kaur, a Grade 10 newsroom
   photographer. Write the credit line, and say where it appears
   relative to the caption.
4. The photo: about forty students filling the bleachers, mid-cheer.
   You cannot name them all — so what does the caption name instead?
5. Write alt text for the serve photo from question 1, then explain
   how alt text and a caption do different jobs.
6. **Challenge.** You have a strong photo of two students at the
   food-drive sorting table, but nobody recorded their names. Run it
   with a nameless caption, or hold it? Argue your call.

## Answers

> [!success]- Answer 1
> "Amara Osei, a Grade 11 setter, serves during Thursday's regional
> final." Who, what, when — in one sentence, present tense, no
> adjectives the photo does not already prove.

> [!success]- Answer 2
> Missing: who (name and grade), when (Thursday's regional final),
> and why it matters (the school's first title in nine years). "A
> student holds a trophy" describes the pixels, not the news.

> [!success]- Answer 3
> "Photo: Devan Kaur" — the credit sits apart from the caption
> sentence, usually after it or along the photo's edge. Credit is
> not decoration; it is attribution, the same duty a quote gets.

> [!success]- Answer 4
> The moment: "Fans pack the bleachers as the deciding set opens."
> When a crowd is the subject, the caption names the event and the
> feeling the photo shows — never guesses at individuals.

> [!success]- Answer 5
> Alt text: "A volleyball player leaps, arm drawn back to serve."
> Alt text describes what is *seen* for a reader who cannot see it;
> the caption adds what the photo *means* — names, stakes, timing.
> A screen reader gets both, so they should not repeat each other.

> [!success]- Answer 6
> A judgement call with two defensible answers. "Students sort
> donations at the food-drive table" is honest and safe; holding the
> photo until you can name them respects that people deserve their
> names in print. What is *not* defensible is guessing — a wrong name
> under a photo is a correction and an apology waiting to happen.
