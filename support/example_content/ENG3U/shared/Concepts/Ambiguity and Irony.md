Ambiguity is not vagueness and irony is not sarcasm. Both are precise
tools for saying two things at once, and Grade 11 is where readers stop
treating them as decoration.

## Irony, in three kinds

| Kind | The gap |
| --- | --- |
| Verbal | Between what is said and what is meant |
| Situational | Between what is expected and what happens |
| Dramatic | Between what a character knows and what the audience knows |

*Macbeth* runs on the third: Duncan admires the castle's "pleasant seat"
while walking into it to be killed. The play makes the audience complicit
— we know, and we watch anyway.

## Ambiguity worth arguing about

A text is ambiguous when the evidence genuinely supports more than one
reading. That is different from a text you have not read carefully
enough. The test: can you build both readings out of quotations, and does
each account for the ending?

The witches are the standard case. Do they cause anything, or do they
describe a man who was already deciding? The play declines to settle it,
and the refusal is the point — see [[Whose Reading Counts]].

## Writing about ambiguity without hedging

Weak: *It could mean many things, so it is up to the reader.*

Strong: *The prophecy is phrased so that it forecloses nothing: "shalt be
king" names an outcome without naming a means. Macbeth supplies the
means, and the play's argument about responsibility depends on the gap he
fills.*

Take a position and acknowledge the other one. Refusing to choose is not
sophistication; it is the essay stopping early.
