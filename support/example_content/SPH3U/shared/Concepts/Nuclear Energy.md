The energy released when a nucleus changes — and the reason Ontario's
grid looks the way it does.

## Fission, briefly

A heavy nucleus (uranium-235) absorbs a neutron, splits into two lighter
nuclei, and releases energy plus more neutrons. Those neutrons can split
more nuclei: a chain reaction. A reactor is a machine for keeping that
chain exactly critical — each fission causing exactly one more — using
control rods and a moderator.

## Where the energy comes from

The products weigh slightly less than what went in. That missing mass
appears as energy, at the exchange rate $E = mc^2$. "Slightly" is doing
real work in that sentence: a fraction of a per cent of the mass, but
$c^2$ is an enormous number.

## Radiation and half-life

Alpha, beta, and gamma differ in what they are, how far they travel, and
what stops them. Each radioisotope has a **half-life**: the time for half
of a sample to decay. After ten half-lives, about a thousandth remains —
which is why spent fuel is both a short-term and a very long-term problem.

Ontario gets roughly half its electricity from nuclear generation, which
makes this a local question rather than an abstract one. See
[[Where Our Electricity Comes From]].
