This is the planning investigation for [[The Civic Action Project]], and
it is deliberately unromantic. You take one issue you care about and
work out, in detail, what would actually have to happen for it to change —
including the possibility that nothing you can do in six weeks would
change it, which is a legitimate finding.

## The question

**What would it take to change this, who would have to agree, and which
of the available routes has the best chance?**

## Step one — the issue, seen from four positions

Choose an issue of personal interest that is genuinely contested. Then
describe how it is seen by at least four groups, one of which must be a
group that disagrees with you.

For each: what do they want, what are they afraid of, what do they gain
or lose, and what evidence do they cite? Write each in their own terms,
not in yours. The test is whether a member of that group would recognise
the description.

This step is where most projects are won or lost. An action aimed at a
problem you have only seen from one side tends to solve the wrong thing.

## Step two — the decision point

Name the body that could grant the change and the specific instrument it
would use: a bylaw, a policy, a budget line, a regulation, a contract, a
staff decision. Then find the next date on which that body considers such
things. Actions with a date attached succeed far more often than actions
without one.

## Step three — the routes, compared

Take each route from [[How Change Actually Happens]] — direct request,
formal process, organising, litigation, election — and assess it against
this issue and this timeline. For each, write:

- What it would require from you, honestly costed in hours.
- What would count as success, stated so that someone else could verify
  it.
- The most likely reason it fails.
- Who else is already doing it, and whether joining them beats starting
  something.

> [!success]- The question that changes most plans
> Is there an organisation already working on this issue? If so, the
> highest-value action is usually to help them at a moment they choose,
> rather than to run a parallel campaign that competes for the same
> attention. Students resist this because it feels less like their own
> project. It is very often the version that changes something.

## What you hand in

A two-page plan: the issue and the four perspectives; the decision point and
its date; the routes compared; and your chosen route with its success
criterion written down before you begin. That last sentence is what makes an
honest report possible at the end of the course, because it was written when
you still did not know the answer.
