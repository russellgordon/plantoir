Every task page publishes its criteria table on the day the task is
launched, which means you can find out how the work will be read
before you have written a line of it. Almost nobody uses that. The
table gets read once at launch, and then work is handed in missing two
rows that twenty minutes would have fixed.

This page is those twenty minutes. We run it together the first time,
on a draft that belongs to nobody in the room, so that the move is
already familiar the day it is your own work under the microscope.

## Three passes, in order

1. **Put them side by side.** The task page open on one half of the
   screen, your work on the other. Not from memory — from the actual
   table.
2. **Rule on every row.** For each one write **yes**, **not yet**, or
   **halfway**, and beside it the evidence a reader would see: the
   file, the line number, the paragraph, the sentence. A "yes" with
   nothing beside it is a hope, not a judgement.
3. **Name the worst row and set a time.** Not the easiest to fix — the
   worst. Then one sentence: what you will change, and in which
   period you will change it.

The ruling is the hard part, not the fixing. Writing "not yet" beside
a row means admitting the thing you were quietly hoping nobody would
open, and everybody's first instinct is to award themselves the row
and move on.

> [!example]- One row, ruled honestly
> From [[The Device Recommendation]]: *"Requirements drive the choice —
> every number traces back to something they do."*
>
> **Halfway.** The memory traces back — she said twelve tabs and a
> video call, and I wrote that down. The processor does not: I put the
> faster one in because the model I liked came with it, and nothing she
> told me needs it. Worst row.
>
> **Change:** go back through my interview notes for anything that
> would actually wait on a slower chip, and if there is nothing, drop
> to the cheaper one and put the money into the screen. Next period,
> first thing.
>
> That ruling took four minutes and is worth more than "looks good"
> written nine times.

## Why it never happens on the due date

Look at where this page is linked from in the schedule: always a
period or two **before** something is handed in, never on the day. A
judgement you have no time to act on is not assessment, it is just
worry — so the arc always leaves a working period between the ruling
and the deadline, and that period is where the mark actually moves.

## Where this sits in the mark

Nowhere, and deliberately. Your judgement of your own work is not part
of your mark, and neither is a classmate's — [[How Marks Work]] says
why. Nothing you write here can cost you anything, which is precisely
what makes it worth writing honestly.

What it changes is the work. By the end of the course most of you run these
three passes without being asked, on a program nobody is going to collect,
because you would rather find the broken row than be told about it. That
habit is the actual point of the page.

> [!tip] Read your ruling out loud
> To the person beside you, or to the wall. Generosity is much easier
> to hear than to see, and you will usually catch the row you were
> being kind about before anyone else has to mention it.
