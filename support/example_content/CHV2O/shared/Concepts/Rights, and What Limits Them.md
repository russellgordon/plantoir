No right in Canada is absolute. That is not a loophole somebody added
later — it is written into the first sentence of the *Canadian Charter of
Rights and Freedoms*, and understanding it is most of what this unit
teaches.

## Where the rights come from

Four documents, and the order matters. **Magna Carta**, 1215, established
that even a king was subject to law — a settlement between a king and his
barons, not a grant of rights to ordinary people. The **British North
America Act, 1867**, now titled the ***Constitution Act, 1867***, created
the Parliament and provinces described in [[Who Decides What, and Where]].
The **Canadian Bill of Rights**, 1960, listed rights in a federal statute
— which meant Parliament could override them by passing another statute,
and it applied to federal law only. The ***Canadian Charter of Rights and
Freedoms***, 1982, put rights into the Constitution itself, binding every
legislature in the country, with the courts empowered to enforce them.

Section 2 of the Charter guarantees the fundamental freedoms: conscience
and religion; thought, belief, opinion and expression, including freedom
of the press; peaceful assembly; and association. Section 15(1)
guarantees equality before and under the law without discrimination based
on race, national or ethnic origin, colour, religion, sex, age, or mental
or physical disability.

## The first limit — section 1

The Charter's opening section guarantees the rights in it "subject only
to such reasonable limits prescribed by law as can be demonstrably
justified in a free and democratic society."

Read that clause slowly, because every word is doing work. **Prescribed
by law** means a limit has to be written in a statute or regulation, not
invented by an official at the counter. **Demonstrably justified** puts
the burden on the government, which must produce evidence rather than
assert that the limit is sensible. **Free and democratic society** is the
standard against which the justification is measured.

When a court applies section 1, it asks whether the government's purpose
is important enough to justify limiting a right, whether the limit
actually serves that purpose, whether a less restrictive measure would
have worked, and whether the benefit outweighs the harm. That is why so
many Charter cases turn on evidence rather than on principle.

> [!warning] Section 33 lets a legislature act notwithstanding a right
> Parliament or a provincial legislature may declare that a law operates
> notwithstanding section 2 or sections 7 to 15 of the Charter. Such a
> declaration expires five years after it comes into force unless it is
> re-enacted. It cannot reach every part of the Charter — democratic
> rights and language rights are outside it. Its use is genuinely
> contested in Canadian politics: some argue it keeps final authority
> with elected legislatures, others that a right a government can switch
> off is not much of a right. Both arguments are worth stating properly
> before you take a side.

## Who enforces any of this

Courts do, when someone brings a case — which means rights are enforced
by people who can afford, survive, or find help to litigate. Alongside
them sit human rights tribunals, privacy and information commissioners,
ombudsman offices, and police oversight bodies, most of which are cheaper
and faster to reach than a courtroom and are where a real complaint
usually starts.

## Rights in a global context, and international violations

Canada's rights protections exist within an international legal framework:

- **International conventions** — such as the *Universal Declaration of Human
  Rights* (UDHR, 1948), the *International Covenant on Civil and Political
  Rights* (ICCPR), the *UN Convention on the Rights of the Child* (UNCRC), and
  the *UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples* (UNDRIP) — set
  global standards for dignity, liberty, and self-determination.
- **Domestic implementation**: Ratifying a treaty creates binding commitments
  under international law, but international agreements generally do not become
  directly enforceable in Canadian courts until Parliament passes domestic
  enabling legislation.

Around the world, human rights face ongoing and severe violations: authoritarian
crackdowns on independent journalists and peaceful protesters, persecution of
religious and ethnic minorities, state surveillance, arbitrary detention, and
suppression of democratic elections.

When international violations occur, the global community responds through a
range of tools with varied effectiveness:

- **Targeted sanctions** (such as Canada's *Justice for Victims of Corrupt
  Foreign Officials Act / Magnitsky Law*), freezing assets and barring travel for
  foreign officials responsible for gross human rights abuses.
- **International judicial accountability**, such as investigations and
  indictments by the International Criminal Court (ICC) for war crimes and
  crimes against humanity.
- **Diplomatic censure and multilateral monitors**, including formal UN Human
  Rights Council resolutions and fact-finding missions.
- **Civil society action**, including documentation and global campaigns by
  organisations like Amnesty International and Human Rights Watch, and emergency
  refugee resettlement for persecuted populations.

Assessing responses requires examining what actually changed for victims on the
ground rather than evaluating official statements alone.
