This is the part of the course that overlaps with your own life, and that
makes it harder rather than easier. You already have opinions about most of
it, you have heard it argued about at home, and the outcome is not yet in.
Recent history is still history — it just has to be done with more care about
what you are assuming.

## The economy Canadians argued themselves into

The 1988 federal election was effectively a referendum on free trade with the
United States. The agreement took effect in 1989, was extended to Mexico as
NAFTA in 1994, and was renegotiated and replaced in 2020. Supporters promised
access to a market ten times Canada's size. Opponents predicted the loss of
manufacturing and, eventually, of the capacity to make independent social
policy. Four decades on, both sides can point to evidence, which is a sign
the question was more complicated than either campaign said.

Two shocks are worth knowing in detail. Manufacturing employment in Ontario
and Quebec fell steeply from the 1990s onward, and towns built around a
single plant did not recover simply because national output did. And in 1992
the federal government closed the northern cod fishery, ending a way of life
that had run in Newfoundland for five centuries. It remains the largest
single industrial shutdown in Canadian history, and the stock has not
returned to what it was. Recessions in the early 1990s and in 2008–09, and
the disruption of the 2020 pandemic, each reshaped who worked and for how
much, and housing cost became a defining political issue.

## A different population

Canada's population was **41,417,056** on 1 April 2026 — and Statistics
Canada recorded that quarter as a decline of 0.1 per cent, following
deliberate federal cuts to immigration and temporary-resident targets.[^1]
That is genuinely new: for most of the period since 1982, population growth
came overwhelmingly from immigration, and the source countries shifted
decisively away from Europe.

[^1]: Get in the habit of asking where a population figure comes from and
what date it describes. A number from a census is not the same thing as a
quarterly estimate, and a national total tells you nothing about which
regions grew. Statistics Canada publishes both, with its own notes on how
each is produced.

The country also aged, urbanized further, and became far more diverse in its
largest cities than in its smaller ones — a gap that shows up in politics.
Indigenous populations grew faster than the national average, and a large
majority of First Nations, Métis and Inuit people now live in cities, which
is not the same as leaving their communities.

## What changed in what people expect

Rights arguments moved from legislatures into courts, because the Charter
gave individuals a way to challenge laws directly. Same-sex marriage became
legal nationally through that route and then by statute. Nunavut was created
in 1999, the first change to Canada's internal map driven by an Indigenous
land claim. Truth and reconciliation entered ordinary public language, along
with the 30 September day of recognition. Meanwhile the internet and then the
smartphone reorganized how Canadians get information and how they argue — a
change you are inside, and therefore worst placed to see.
