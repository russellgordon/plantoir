Almost everything you will learn about a civic issue reaches you through
somebody who wanted you to learn it. That is not a scandal — a journalist,
a government, an advocacy group and a friend all have purposes — but it
does mean the first question is never "is this true?" It is "who is
telling me, and how would I check?"

## Five checks, in the order that saves time

**1. Find the original.** Almost every civic claim traces back to a
document: a bill, a budget, a court decision, a report, a dataset. A post
about a law is not the law. Getting to the original settles most
disputes, and it is usually two clicks.

**2. Read laterally.** Instead of studying the page in front of you,
open other tabs and find out what the source is. Fact-checkers work this
way; students who stay on the original page do worse. Who runs it, who
funds it, who else covers this, and what do they say?

**3. Separate the claim from the framing.** "Crime is up" and "reported
incidents of one offence rose 4 per cent in one city over one year" may
be the same fact. One is checkable. Ask what the number counts, over what
period, compared with what.

**4. Ask what the source stands to gain.** A government reporting on its
own program, an industry association reporting on its own industry, and
an advocacy group reporting on its own cause are all still capable of
being right — and all need corroboration from someone with different
interests.

**5. Notice your own agreement.** The claims you accept fastest are the
ones you already believed. That is when to slow down, because that is
when a source that wants to move you has the least work to do.

## What social media changes

It changes the sorting, not the truth. A feed shows you what holds
attention, and outrage holds attention better than accuracy — so the
version of an issue that reaches you is systematically more extreme than
the issue. Screenshots strip a claim from its source. Reposting is
costless, so volume is not evidence. Generated images and cloned voices
are now cheap enough that a video of a politician saying something is no
longer proof that they said it.

None of this makes social media useless. It is often the fastest route to
people directly affected by a decision, to organisers, and to primary
documents posted by the bodies that made them. Follow the institutions
themselves — a council, a ministry, a court, a statistical agency — and
you get the source rather than the summary.

## Foreign interference and disinformation

Democratic values and public discourse in Canada face targeted manipulation by
foreign state actors and coordinated online networks:

- **Methods of interference**: Running covert bot networks to amplify extreme
  positions, spreading fabricated claims during elections, targeting diaspora
  communities, and financing third-party groups without disclosure.
- **Strategic objectives**: The goal is often not to secure a specific policy win
  but to erode democratic trust — making citizens believe elections are rigged,
  institutions are illegitimate, and participation is futile.
- **Protecting democratic integrity**: Canada's security agencies and
  independent public inquiries monitor foreign interference. For citizens,
  applying lateral reading and verifying claims against primary records is the
  first line of civic defence.

## Applying political thinking to current events

When analysing any civic news story, apply the four concepts of political
thinking:

- **Political Significance**: Why does this issue matter, who does it affect, and
  what precedents does it set?
- **Political Perspective**: Whose beliefs, values, and economic interests are
  represented in the report, and whose perspective was omitted?
- **Stability and Change**: Does this development represent a fundamental policy
  shift or an ongoing continuation of existing structures?
- **Objectives and Results**: What was the policy's stated goal, and what were
  the actual, measurable outcomes?

> [!tip] The thirty-second habit that does most of the work
> Before you use anything: name the person or body who produced it, the
> date, and where the underlying evidence is. If you cannot fill all
> three blanks, you do not have a source yet — you have something you
> read. Write those three things beside every entry in
> [[Your Civic Notebook]].

Citations exist for the same reason: not to prove you did the reading,
but so a reader can go and check whether you read it correctly. A
citation nobody could follow costs marks in every task in this course.
