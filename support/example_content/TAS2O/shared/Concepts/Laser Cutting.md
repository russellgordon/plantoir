On the first day of the shop unit you held four objects and argued
about how each was made. The laser-cut one gave itself away: an edge
so straight and so slightly browned that no saw made it. This page
names what you noticed.

## What the machine is doing

A laser cutter focuses a beam of infrared light — invisible to you — to
a spot smaller than a pencil point. Where it lands, the material heats
so fast that it burns or vaporises away. The head then moves the spot
along a path, like a pen that removes instead of draws.

Three settings decide what happens along that path:

| Setting | What it controls | Turn it up and… |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Power** | How much energy the beam delivers | It cuts deeper — and burns the edges darker |
| **Speed** | How fast the head moves | Less energy lands per millimetre, so it cuts shallower |
| **Focus** | How tightly the spot is squeezed, set by the distance to the material | Out of focus, the spot spreads and cuts poorly |

Power and speed trade against each other, the way shutter and aperture
do in [[Doing the Numbers]]. Slow and gentle can cut the same line as
fast and fierce, with different edges — which is why every material
gets a test card before anything real is cut.

## Cut, score, engrave

The same beam does three jobs, depending on the settings and the kind
of line in the file:

- **Cut** — enough energy to go right through. A cut line is a closed
  outline around a part.
- **Score** — a single fast, shallow pass along a line. Lettering drawn
  as outlines can be scored in seconds.
- **Engrave** — the head sweeps back and forth like a printer, burning
  a filled area line by line. It looks rich and takes by far the
  longest, so we keep it small.

Every cut also burns away a sliver of material, the **kerf** — a
fraction of a millimetre, and it is why a square drawn at 50 mm comes
out a little under. [[From Sketch to Cut File]] shows how to set up a
file for all three jobs.

## Why it matters beyond this room

Technology teachers have a short list of ideas that any made thing can
be judged by — among them its **function**, its **aesthetics**, its
**material**, and how it was **fabricated**. The laser changes all
four at once: parts come out identical, fast, and precise, in flat
sheet materials, with an edge that looks machined because it was.
That is why lasers turn up in very different technology areas — cutting
signs and packaging prototypes, fabric in a garment workroom, scale
models in an architect's office, and, with far more powerful lasers,
sheet metal on a factory floor.

## What can go wrong, and what stops it

| Hazard | What prevents it |
| --- | --- |
| **Fire** — the material is being burned on purpose | Somebody watches every second of every job; the approved materials list; the extinguisher beside the machine |
| **Fumes and smoke** | Extraction running whenever the laser is; the lid closed until the job ends |
| **The beam itself** | The enclosure and its lid switch, which are never defeated |
| **Hot edges and sharp acrylic** | Parts rest a moment before they are handled; glasses on for trimming |

The rules themselves are in [[Safety in the Shop]].

## Keeping it working

A laser cuts only as well as its lens is clean. Smoke settles on the
lens and the mirrors and eats the power; the honeycomb bed collects
scraps that can catch fire. So every shop day ends the same way: bed
cleared, crumb tray emptied, lens checked and cleaned with the lens
wipes and cleaner the maker specifies, and the day's settings problems
written on the settings sheet.
