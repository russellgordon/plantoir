Your mark comes from what you can do with economics, not from how much
of it you can recite. Ontario splits the work into four achievement
categories, and every assessed piece in this course is marked against
some combination of them.

## The seventy and the thirty

Every Grade 12 credit in Ontario is built the same way. Seventy per
cent of your mark comes from the work you do across the term, and it
leans towards your **most recent and most consistent** work rather than
averaging September against January — a term spent getting better is
marked as a term spent getting better. The other thirty per cent is a
final evaluation at the end.

**The seventy** is the eight tasks you hand in during the term, plus
the marked parts of [[The Notebook and Portfolio]]. In the order you
meet them: [[The Scarcity Analysis]], [[The Market Model]],
[[The Firm Study]], [[The Intervention Argument]],
[[The Indicators Report]], [[The Policy Brief]],
[[The Trade Question]], and [[The Economic Issue Report]]. They are not
equally weighted: the Economic Issue Report is the culminating task and
is worth several of the smaller ones on its own, because it runs the
longest and asks for everything at once. Ask me for the exact split
whenever you want it — it is not a secret, it is just not the useful
thing to memorise.

**The thirty** is [[The Data Examination]]: three hours, an unseen
dossier, real tables supplied. It is deliberately not a fifth unit
test. It reaches across the whole course, which is the only fair way
for one afternoon to be worth that much.

## The four categories, in economics

| Category | What it means here | Where you meet it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Knowledge and Understanding | The concepts, the models, and what each one is for | Every unit; heaviest in Unit 1 |
| Thinking | Choosing the right model, criticising the source, judging whether a claim survives its own evidence | [[The Firm Study]], [[The Policy Brief]], [[The Trade Question]] |
| Communication | An argument a reader can follow, with charts that are labelled and sources that are named | Every written task, and the seminars that rehearse them |
| Application | Taking a model to a live Canadian question and getting a defensible answer out of it | [[The Intervention Argument]], [[The Economic Issue Report]] |

Thinking is where this course lives. Knowing what elasticity is belongs
to the first category; deciding that elasticity — rather than market
structure — is the right lens for the question in front of you, and
then saying why the estimate you found is or is not credible, belongs
to the second. That judgement is what separates a good paper here from
a tidy one.

## Three kinds of evidence, and two of them are not paper

**What you hand me** is the obvious kind: reports, briefs, models,
milestone entries. It arrives, it holds still, and it can be marked on
a Sunday.

**What I watch you do** is the second. Whether you check that a table
exists before you commit to a topic. Whether a figure that contradicts
you gets investigated or gets closed. Whether you can drive a
statistical release or only describe one. Every task you hand in has
working periods in class, and that is one of the reasons why.

**What you tell me** is the third. The conferences and check-ins the
schedule gives every task are not progress checks — they are evidence in
their own right, and some of what you understand will only ever show up
there. If your draft is behind and your conference is sharp, the
conference is the strongest evidence of that day, and it counts as
exactly that.

The four seminars work the same way. A seminar carries no mark of its
own — a task's own defence, like the one on
[[The Intervention Argument]], is a different thing and is marked — but
what happens in a seminar is evidence for the tasks it feeds, gathered
by watching and by listening.

## What is not in your mark

**How you work is reported separately.** Responsibility, organization,
independent work, collaboration, initiative and self-regulation appear
on your report card as **E, G, S or N**, in their own section. They
matter, we will talk about them, and they do not move your percentage.
The mark is about the work; that column is about the worker, and mixing
the two tells you less about both. There is no participation mark in
this course, and there is no version of one worth having — a mark for
talking rewards the confident rather than the prepared.

One expectation here, A2.2, does mention work habits among the things
economic reasoning gives you to carry elsewhere. What is marked against
it — on [[A Model of Your Own]] — is the reasoning you took into a
decision of your own, not how you conducted yourself in this room.

**Your judgement, and your classmates', are not part of it either.**
You will assess your own work against the published criteria often;
[[Judging Your Own Work]] is the routine, and the schedule sets aside
time for it in every unit, always with working time after it. The peer
readings and the peer challenges do the same job from the other side.
None of it is marked, in either direction, and that is what makes it
safe to be honest in.

**Practice is not marked.** The weekly figure you put into
[[Your Economics Notebook]] between classes, the reading, the drawing
and re-drawing of a diagram until it comes out — worth doing, all of it,
and none of it carries a mark on its own. A homework line that names
something due is reminding you to bring it, not marking the evening you
spent on it.

Every task you hand in gets real working periods in class for the same
reason: as much of it as possible should happen where I can see it and
where everybody has the same hour and the same access to me. The three
marked pieces of [[The Notebook and Portfolio]] go further and are
written in class outright.

## What is worth marks, and what is not

Say this one out loud: **a correct number with no source is worth less
here than a well-sourced approximation.** A figure you cannot attribute
cannot be checked, cannot be updated, and cannot be argued with, so it
does no work in an argument no matter how right it happens to be. A
figure that names its table and its release date can be checked by
anyone, including by you in three weeks when it has changed.

## Where the criteria live

Every task page publishes what it is looking for before the work
starts, in words you can hold your own draft against. Read them on
launch day rather than the night before the hand-in — then read them
again with [[Judging Your Own Work]] beside your draft, in the time the
schedule sets aside for it. If a mark ever surprises you, those
criteria are the whole story, and [[Getting Help]] lists the ways to
reach me.
