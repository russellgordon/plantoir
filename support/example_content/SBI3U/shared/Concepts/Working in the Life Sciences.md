Biology is not one job. It is a way of asking questions that turns up in
hospitals, on fishing boats, in courtrooms, on farms, in parks, and in
front of a microscope — and the people doing it took very different
routes to get there.

## Where the work actually is

| Role | What the week looks like | Usual route in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Zoologist | Field seasons, tagging and tracking, long stretches of data analysis | Biology degree, then graduate work |
| Botanist | Herbarium and field survey work; increasingly, genomics | Biology or plant science degree |
| Geneticist | Sequencing, analysis, and interpretation — mostly at a screen | Genetics or molecular biology, usually to graduate level |
| Genetic counsellor | Meeting families, explaining risk, supporting decisions | Science degree, then a genetic counselling master's |
| Medical laboratory technologist | Running and interpreting diagnostic tests in a hospital lab | College diploma, then certification |
| Conservation officer | Enforcement, monitoring, and public education, outdoors | College diploma in resources or a related degree |
| Epidemiologist | Population data: who got sick, where, and what changed | Health sciences, then graduate work in public health |
| Agronomist | Soil, crop, and yield problems on real farms | Agricultural science degree |
| Wildlife technician | Field data collection and habitat work | College diploma, hands-on from the start |
| Science teacher | Explaining all of it, every day | Science degree, then teacher education |

Two things are worth noticing. The routes are not all long — several of
these are college diplomas that put you in the field within two years.
And almost all of them involve more writing and more statistics than
students expect, which is why the reference pages in this course exist.

## The work exists because somebody needed something

None of the roles above appeared because biology found them
interesting. Each one exists because a society had a problem and paid
for an answer, and the technology in this course arrived the same way.

| What people needed | What it produced | Where you meet it in this course |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Food that keeps, from land that is already farmed | Selective breeding, then crop genetics and controlled-environment growing | [[Plants and Agriculture]] |
| To stop dying of infections | Antibiotics — and then the resistance problem that came with using them | [[Bacteria and Archaea]] |
| To know a diagnosis before the symptoms | Genetic screening, and the counselling profession that had to be invented alongside it | [[Genetic Technologies]], [[Genetic Counselling Brief]] |
| To survive organ failure | Transplant surgery, tissue matching, and the immunology behind rejection | [[Keeping Conditions Steady]] |
| To sit down for a living without it shortening life | Exercise physiology, fitness measurement, and a food industry reformulating what it sells | [[Animal Systems Overview]] |
| To breathe in a hospital when lungs fail | Ventilators, blood-gas measurement, and pulse oximetry | [[The Respiratory System]] |

Two things follow that are worth arguing about rather than accepting.
**The need shapes what gets studied**, so the questions with money
behind them get answered first — which is why a disease common in
wealthy countries has more research on it than one affecting more people
elsewhere. And **every one of these creates a second problem** the
original need did not anticipate: antibiotics produced resistance,
screening produced questions about who is entitled to the result, and
transplantation produced a permanent shortage and a set of decisions
about who is on the list. Neither observation is an argument against the
technology. Both are arguments for the people using it knowing where it
came from, which is what a course like this is for.

## Three Canadians, and what they actually did

**Lap-Chee Tsui** led the team at Toronto's Hospital for Sick Children
that identified the gene behind cystic fibrosis in 1989, working with
Jack Riordan and Manuel Buchwald in Toronto and Francis Collins's group
in Michigan. The gene, *CFTR*, was the first found by mapping its
position rather than by knowing the protein first — which is why the
discovery mattered far beyond one disease: it showed the approach that
the Human Genome Project would later use at scale.

**Louis Bernatchez**, at Université Laval, held a Canada Research Chair
in genomics and the conservation of aquatic resources. His work took
population genetics out of the laboratory and into fisheries management
— using the genetic differences between populations of the same species
to work out which stocks are distinct, which are in trouble, and what
conservation would actually have to protect. He continued publishing
until his death in 2023.

**Colin D'Cunha** was Ontario's Chief Medical Officer of Health during
the 2003 SARS outbreak, the province's most serious public-health
emergency in decades. The role is a reminder that biology in practice is
often about decisions under uncertainty, made in public, before all the
evidence is in.

> [!note]- Why the course names Canadians specifically
> Not patriotism — visibility. Students routinely assume that research
> of this kind happens somewhere else, and it does not. Two of the three
> above worked within a few hours of this classroom, and the
> universities and hospitals where they worked take undergraduates every
> year.

## What to do if any of this interests you

The single most useful step is not more reading. It is asking one person
who does the work what their week is actually like — most will answer a
polite email from a student. After that: keep your biology and chemistry
credits open, take the mathematics that the programs actually require,
and get any hands-on experience you can, including volunteer work at a
clinic, a farm, a stable, a park, or a lab.
