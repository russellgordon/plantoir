The moment you finish a drawing, you own it. In Canada copyright exists as
soon as a work is made — no form to file, no symbol to add, no fee. That is
worth knowing for two reasons. It is your protection, and it is everyone
else's, which means the image you just found and were about to trace belongs
to somebody in exactly the same way yours belongs to you.

## Learning from work versus using it

Copying is a legitimate way to learn, and artists have always done it.
Working from a photograph, a comic panel, or a painting to study how somebody
solved a problem is practice, and practice belongs in
[[The Sketchbook]] and in [[Working From Reference]].

What changes is what you do next. Presenting that copy as your own original
work — in a portfolio, in a show, online — is a different act, and it is the
one that causes trouble. The line is not about how much you changed it. It is
about what you claim.

Reference sits in between and is generally fine: using several sources for
the anatomy of a hand, or the shape of a car, and building your own image
from them. Trace one source closely and you have made a copy of somebody's
composition, whatever medium you finished it in.

> [!warning] Crediting someone is not the same as asking them
> Naming a source is honest, and it is not permission. Permission comes from
> the person who holds the rights, in advance, and it can be refused. Some
> work is offered under licences that grant permission ahead of time — those
> licences have conditions, and the conditions are the whole point. Read them
> rather than assuming what they say.

## What this means in this studio

Ask before you use somebody's image in your own work, and be prepared to
hear no. Keep a record in your portfolio of where every source came from —
you will not remember by the end of the course. Photograph people only with
their agreement, especially if the photograph is going on a wall or a
website. And when you show work in [[The Exhibition]], the label carries
your name because the work is yours; if it depends on somebody else's image,
that belongs on the label too.

The habit works in your favour as well. Sign and date what you make. Keep
your files. When you photograph work for [[The Portfolio]], you are building
the record that proves what you made and when — which is the practical form
that ownership takes.
