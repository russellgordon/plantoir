The model kits came out again for [[Building and Testing Models]], with
one instruction: build $\ce{C4H10}$. Two groups did, and
put two different objects on the bench. Neither had made a mistake.
Every carbon had four bonds, every hydrogen had one, and the two models
could not be twisted into each other however long anyone tried.

That is the problem this whole unit is built on. A molecular formula
counts atoms, and counting atoms turns out to be a surprisingly weak
description of a substance.

## A formula is a weak claim

Butane and 2-methylpropane are both $\ce{C4H10}$. They are
different substances with different properties, sold separately, boiling
at different temperatures. **Isomers** are compounds with the same
molecular formula and a different arrangement of atoms.

Organic chemistry has this problem worse than any other branch, because
carbon forms four strong bonds, bonds to itself indefinitely, and makes
chains, branches, and rings without complaint. The count grows
alarmingly:

| Formula | Structural isomers |
| --- | --- |
| $\ce{C4H10}$ | 2 |
| $\ce{C5H12}$ | 3 |
| $\ce{C6H14}$ | 5 |
| $\ce{C7H16}$ | 9 |
| $\ce{C8H18}$ | 18 |

Nine possible molecules for one seven-carbon formula, and that is only
the alkanes — allow a double bond, a ring, or an oxygen and the number
rises much faster. Any system for naming these has to distinguish all of
them unambiguously, which is why [[Naming Organic Compounds]] is a
system rather than a list.

## Structural isomers — the connections differ

In a structural isomer, atoms are joined to different partners. There
are three kinds worth naming.

**Chain isomers** differ in how the carbon skeleton branches. Butane is
a straight chain of four; 2-methylpropane is a chain of three with one
carbon hanging off the middle. Butane boils at about $-0.5$ °C and
2-methylpropane at about $-11.7$ °C, and you can now say why: branching
makes a compact molecule with less surface to lie against a neighbour,
so its dispersion forces are weaker. That explanation comes straight out
of [[Intermolecular Forces]] with nothing new added.

**Positional isomers** have the same skeleton and the same functional
group, attached in a different place. Propan-1-ol has its $-\ce{OH}$
on an end carbon; propan-2-ol has it on the middle one. They are both
alcohols and they behave differently enough that one is rubbing alcohol
and the other is not.

**Functional group isomers** are the dramatic case: the same atoms
assembled into different *classes* of compound. The formula
$\ce{C2H6O}$ can be ethanol, which boils at about
78 °C, or it can be dimethyl ether, which boils at about $-24$ °C.
One is a liquid you can hold in a beaker
and the other is a gas at room temperature, and the difference is a
single oxygen sitting between the carbons instead of on the end. Ethanol
has an $-\ce{OH}$ and hydrogen bonds to itself; the ether has no
hydrogen on its oxygen and cannot.

## Stereoisomers — the connections are the same

Here the atoms are joined to exactly the same partners, and the
arrangement in **space** differs.

**Geometric isomers** appear when rotation is blocked. A carbon–carbon
single bond spins freely, so nothing is fixed. A carbon–carbon *double*
bond does not spin, so the two ends are locked in position. In
1,2-dichloroethene, the two chlorines can be on the same side of the
double bond or on opposite sides — *cis* and *trans* — and the molecules
cannot interconvert without breaking the bond.

Then apply [[Polarity]]. The *cis* form has both C–Cl dipoles pointing
partly the same way and is polar; the *trans* form has them opposed and
is not. The polar one boils around 60 °C and the non-polar one around
48 °C. Same formula, same bonds, same connections, and a measurable
difference in boiling point that comes from nothing but geometry.

**Optical isomers** are subtler. A carbon bonded to four *different*
groups can be assembled two ways that are mirror images of each other
and cannot be superimposed — the same relationship your left hand has to
your right. Such a carbon is called **chiral**.

The two forms have identical melting points, identical boiling points,
identical densities, and identical solubilities. Almost every physical
measurement you know cannot tell them apart. They differ in exactly two
respects: they rotate plane-polarised light in opposite directions, and
they behave differently towards anything else that is chiral.

> [!question] Why would biology care about a mirror image?
> Because the receptors and enzymes a molecule meets in a living thing
> are themselves chiral — built from amino acids that come in one
> handedness only. A chiral molecule fitting into a chiral site is a
> left hand going into a left glove. Its mirror image has the same
> atoms, the same bonds, and the same energy, and it does not fit.
>
> So two optical isomers of the same substance can have entirely
> different biological effects: one active, the other inert, or worse.
> This is why pharmaceutical synthesis often has to produce a single
> handedness rather than a mixture, and why that is an expensive thing
> to require. A property that no ordinary laboratory measurement can
> detect turns out to be the property that matters most.

## What isomerism actually tells you

Take the general lesson, because it outlives the unit.

A molecular formula is a **count**. A structural formula is a
**description**. Only the second predicts anything, and the whole method
of this course — structure predicts behaviour — collapses if you stop at
the first. When you are handed $\ce{C3H8O}$ and asked
for a boiling point, the honest answer is a question: *which one?*

That is also the reason organic chemists draw so much. A structural
formula, a condensed formula, and a line diagram are three ways of
writing the same information, and you will be expected to move between
them fluently. Practise that in [[Organic Naming Practice]]; put it to
work identifying a real substance in
[[Identifying an Unknown Organic Compound]]; and see how the classes
themselves are sorted in [[Functional Groups]].
