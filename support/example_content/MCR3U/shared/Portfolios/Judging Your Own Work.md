Every task page in this course carries a table of success criteria,
published on the day the task launches. Most people read it once, at the
launch, and never again — and then hand in work that misses two rows they
could have mended in fifteen minutes. This page is those fifteen minutes.

We do it together the first time, on a gallery of mine, so that the move
is not new to you when the work being judged is yours.

This is a different job from [[Checking Your Own Work]], which asks
whether your answers are right. This page asks whether your work does
what the task said it had to do — which is a question a correct answer
can still fail.

## The routine

1. Put the criteria table beside your work — the actual page, not the
   memory of it.
2. Go down it row by row. For each row write **yes**, **not yet**, or
   **partly**, and one sentence of evidence: the graph, the line, the
   number a reader would point at. "Yes" with nothing to point at is a
   hope, not a judgement.
3. Find your **weakest** row. Not the quickest to fix — the weakest.
   Those are almost never the same row.
4. Write one sentence saying what you will change, and when.
5. Change it, in what is left of this period or in the next one. This is
   why the self-check is never scheduled on a hand-in day: a judgement
   you have no time to act on is just a worry with a table attached.

Steps 2 and 3 are the whole exercise. Naming your weakest row honestly is
harder than any of the fixing, because it means saying out loud the thing
you have been quietly hoping nobody would look at.

> [!example]- One row, judged honestly
> From [[The Tide Problem]]: *"A measured miss — model-versus-data
> disagreement stated in metres."*
>
> **Partly.** My curve sits on the data at both high tides on day one and
> pulls away from it by the third day, and what my page actually says is
> "the fit is pretty good". That is a feeling, not a measurement, and
> "pretty good" is exactly the phrase [[What Makes a Model Good]] tells me
> to distrust. Weakest row. **Change:** read the vertical gap at the six
> worst points, take the largest, write it in metres, and say which end of
> the window it happens at — this period.
>
> That entry is worth more than **yes** written five times, and it took
> less time to produce.

## Why this is safe

Your judgement of your own work is not part of your mark, and neither is a
classmate's — [[How Marks Work]] says so, and it is Ontario policy rather
than a preference of mine. That cuts both ways: nothing you admit on this
page can cost you anything, which is precisely what makes it worth writing
honestly.

What it changes is the work. A self-check that leads to one real fix is
the cheapest improvement available in this course, and by the last unit
most people run it without being asked, which is the actual point.

## In mathematics, particularly

Two rows go wrong here more often than the rest, and they are worth
checking first every time.

- **The row about restrictions and limits.** It is the one people mark
  **yes** from memory, because they know the rule. Knowing the rule is not
  the same as having written the restriction on the page.
- **The row about tying the mathematics back to the situation.** Letters
  explained "in water", parameters tied to the world, a total interest set
  beside its principal. Work that is correct and untranslated reads as
  finished to the person who wrote it and as unfinished to everybody else.

And a general warning: the row you have been calling "basically fine" is
your weakest row roughly half the time. "Basically fine" is what we say
about the part we have stopped looking at.
