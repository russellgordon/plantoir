After 1945 Canada stopped being a country that was told when it was at war
and became one that chose its commitments — and then had to explain them.
The curriculum asks a sharp question about that record: whether each
development "marked a change in Canada's approach to or role in
international relations," or merely continued what came before.

## The Cold War, and the shape it forced

Canada helped found NATO in 1949 and joined NORAD with the United States in
1958, tying its defence to a continental and an Atlantic alliance at once.
It sent forces to Korea. In 1956 it proposed the emergency force that ended
the Suez Crisis, and Lester Pearson took the Nobel Peace Prize for it the
following year.

Out of that came the story Canadians tell about themselves: the peacekeeping
nation, the helpful fixer, the middle power.[^story] The record around it is
more mixed — a founding member of two military alliances, a supplier of
uranium, a host to American radar lines across Inuit homelands, and, through
the Canadian International Development Agency, a donor whose spending rose
and fell with budgets at home.

## Since 1982, the same question with harder cases

Canada joined the sanctions against apartheid South Africa, fought in the
Gulf War, and served in Bosnia and Somalia. It had troops in Rwanda in 1994
and could not stop the genocide. It went to Afghanistan after 2001 and
declined to join the invasion of Iraq in 2003. It sent money and people
after the Indian Ocean tsunami and the Haiti earthquake, much of it through
non-governmental organizations rather than the state.

Set those beside one another and the "key factors" the curriculum asks you
to explain start to show: what the Americans wanted, what the United Nations
authorized, what an election was about that year, and what the country was
willing to pay.

**The question worth asking of any one of them:** *was this a departure, or
the same policy in new circumstances?* You cannot answer it with a single
event. Continuity and change need two points in time, which is the method
set out in [[Continuity and Change]].

Compare the 1939 decision in [[Canada Goes to War Again]], follow the
constitutional side in [[Canada Since 1982]], and use the testimony in
[[Oral History and Testimony]] to hear what these deployments were like from
inside them.

[^story]: Treat "peacekeeping nation" as a claim with a date. It was made
loudest from the 1960s onward, by governments that found it useful, and it
describes some of Canada's record while quietly omitting the rest. That is
exactly what [[Historical Perspective]] is for.
