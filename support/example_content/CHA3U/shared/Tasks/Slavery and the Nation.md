**Due: the middle of Unit 2.** Individual. About 1,100 words.

The hardest task in the course, and the one that most rewards precision.
Slavery in the United States was made by statute, sustained by capital,
resisted continuously, and it built institutions that outlived it.

## The six parts

1. **The law.** Quote a clause from one statute or code that made
   enslavement work — a colonial or state slave code, a fugitive
   provision, a Black Code passed after emancipation. Say what it
   authorised, whom it obliged, and what it reveals about what enslaved
   people were already doing.
2. **The money.** Establish who profited, and how, with sourced figures,
   then follow it past the plantation: the credit that financed it, the
   shippers and insurers, the mills that bought the cotton.
3. **The people.** Reconstruct something specific about how enslaved
   people lived — work, family, faith, skill, purchase and sale,
   separation — naming individuals wherever your sources name them.
4. **Resistance.** Show its full range: the daily and deniable, the
   collective, the legal, flight, and armed revolt. Resistance is the
   normal condition of the record, not its exception.
5. **The West.** Explain how expansion turned enslavement from a
   sectional arrangement into the central question of national politics
   — which territories, admitted how, and who decided.
6. **What outlasted it.** Name one institution, practice, or
   arrangement that survived emancipation and show the mechanism by
   which it carried over. Not a mood. A mechanism.

## Where the evidence is

Statute books and county court records. Bills of sale, probate
inventories, insurance policies, plantation account books.
Advertisements placed to sell people and to recapture them. The records
of the Bureau of Refugees, Freedmen, and Abandoned Lands, held by the
United States National Archives as Record Group 105 and covering
1861–1872. And the Federal Writers' Project interviews with formerly
enslaved people, gathered between 1935 and 1940 — which need their own
provenance work: how old was the speaker at emancipation, who was
holding the pencil, and what could not safely be said in that decade?

## How it is marked

| Criterion | What earns the marks |
| --- | --- |
| Law | A real clause, quoted and read, not a summary of a summary |
| Economics | Figures, sourced, and followed beyond the plantation |
| Personhood | Enslaved people appear with names, skills, and decisions |
| Resistance | Shown across its range, and explained rather than celebrated |
| Legacy | One mechanism traced past 1877, not a general sense of aftermath |

> [!important] Language, and the difference it makes
> Write **enslaved people**, not "slaves" — the noun turns a condition
> imposed by law into a description of who someone was. Write
> **enslaver**, not "master", except inside a quotation. And do not
> reproduce violence at length for effect: state it, date it, source it,
> move on. See [[The Words Historians Use]].

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

The care this task asks for is care about EVIDENCE. Both prompts below watch
how a student handles the record, never how they feel about what is in it.

OBSERVE — Unit 2, Day 5, the research period that follows the archive
lesson
  Watch for: what a student does when a finding aid returns nothing. There
  are three moves and they are not equivalent. Change the search terms;
  change the archive; or change the question so that the gap stops
  mattering. The third is the common one, and it is invisible in the
  finished essay, because the essay is about whichever question survived —
  most often the sixth part, "what outlasted it", quietly missing.
  Going well: search terms changed to the ones the records were INDEXED
  under, which are the period's words and not ours — a surname, a ship, a
  county court, an estate — rather than the terms we would use to describe
  the same thing today.
  Stuck: the same three modern words retyped into the same box for twenty
  minutes; or a part of the task dropped with "there was nothing".
  Record: initials and one letter on the day plan — T for terms, A for
  archive, Q for question.

TALK — Unit 2, Day 7, the conferences already on that agenda
  Ask: "Read me the clause you chose. Which single word would you have to
  strike out to stop it working?"
  Then: "Who would have had to enforce this, and how would they have
  known?"
  A strong answer puts a finger on one operative word rather than
  paraphrasing the whole clause, and then names a person inside the
  machinery — a constable, a patroller, a ship's captain, a neighbour with
  a reward to collect. Seeing that a statute needs an enforcer who needs
  knowledge is how a student gets from the law to C1.2 — the daily lives of
  different groups, INCLUDING how they responded to the challenges of
  everyday life, which is the expectation's own phrase. A written essay
  can quote the clause and paraphrase a textbook around it; only this shows
  you whether the student found the load-bearing word themselves.
  Record: one line per student, the word they chose. It is a good record of
  who is reading and who is summarising.

The product evidence is the essay handed in on Day 9.
