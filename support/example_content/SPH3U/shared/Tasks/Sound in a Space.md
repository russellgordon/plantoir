> [!abstract] At a glance
> **Format:** three pages plus measurements. **Measured in pairs; the
> three pages are yours.** Due at the end of Unit 4.
> **Assessed on:** Thinking and Investigation, Application.

Pick a room in this school and explain what it does to sound — then say
what you would change and what it would cost.

## The brief

1. **Measure** something real about the space: the time a clap takes to
   die away, where the echoes come from, or how the loudness of a fixed
   source changes with distance across it.
2. **Explain** what you measured using [[Wave Properties]],
   [[Interference and Beats]], and [[Resonance and Standing Waves]] —
   whichever actually apply. Do not force all three.
3. **Recommend** one change (soft surfaces on one wall, a different
   speaker position, angling a reflector) and predict its effect.
4. **Cost it**, roughly. A recommendation with no cost attached is a wish.

## Rooms that repay the effort

The gym, the cafeteria at lunch, the stairwell, the music room, and the
corridor outside the library are all acoustically interesting for
different reasons. The physics lab is not.

Measure in pairs — one person claps or holds the source, the other reads
the meter, and you argue about the number. The data table may be shared.
The explanation, the recommendation and the costing are written and
handed in by each of you, because that is the part that shows what you
understood.

## How it is marked

| What is judged | What it looks like in your three pages |
| --- | --- |
| The measurement | Taken by you, in that room, with the conditions recorded — where you stood, what was making the sound, how many times |
| The explanation | ONE wave idea, chosen because it fits your data, with the parts of your measurement it accounts for pointed at |
| The recommendation | Follows from your own number, not from what soft rooms are generally like |
| The prediction | Says what your change would do to the quantity you actually measured, and by roughly how much |
| The costing | A real figure with a source, and what it would buy |

## What I am looking for

A measurement you actually took, connected to a wave idea that genuinely
explains it, ending in a recommendation someone could act on. Three
paragraphs of general facts about sound, with no numbers, is the failure
mode.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 11, the second working period with the meters
(Day 10 has the conference in it; do not try to do both at once)
  Watch for: what happens to a reading that looks wrong. The criteria ask
  for the number of times they measured, so the table will tell you HOW
  MANY — what no table can tell you is which reading was thrown away and
  why, or which one should have been and was not. Watch too whether the
  meter is held at the same height and angle each time, or waved about
  until a satisfying number appears.
  Going well: a surprising reading is repeated before it is written down,
  and the reason is said out loud.
  Stuck: the notebook fills faster than the room is understood, and the
  largest number of the three is the one that got kept.
  Record: on the class list, a tick when you see a repeat, plus the one
  word that prompted it.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 12, while the pairs are reading each other's drafts
  Ask: "Suppose I take your meter away. How would you show somebody that
  this room has the problem you say it has?"
  Then: "You measured a clap dying away. What happens to that time when
  the room is full of people, and why?"
  A strong first answer designs a measurement rather than describing the
  problem — clap at a paced distance from the wall and time twenty of
  them, or find the quiet spots between two speakers and pace them out.
  That is E2.4 used as a tool rather than recited, and the three pages
  never ask how else it could have been shown.
  A strong second answer goes to the surface and then to the design:
  soft, irregular things absorb and scatter the wave instead of returning
  it, so less energy comes back, it dies away sooner — and therefore a
  soft wall is a cheaper fix than a reflector for the problem they
  measured. That last step is what makes it E1.1, which asks how the
  properties of sound INFLUENCE THE DESIGN of a space; a recommendation
  section shows you the conclusion without ever showing whether the
  mechanism under it is understood.
  Record: one line per pair on the conference list.

The product evidence is the three pages, handed in on Day 13.
