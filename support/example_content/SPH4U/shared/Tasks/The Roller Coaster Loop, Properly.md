> [!abstract] At a glance
> **Format:** four pages of analysis, with a model or a video of one.
> **Pairs.** Due at the end of Unit 2. **Assessed on:** Knowledge,
> Thinking and Investigation, Application.

In Grade 11 you built a coaster and found that the loop needed a release
height higher than $2.5r$ — and could only say "friction" about the
difference. You now have the tools to do better: circular motion,
rotational energy, and momentum.

## The brief

Take a loop — one you build from foam insulation, one from a Grade 11
coaster, or one you film at an amusement park with a known radius — and
account for its behaviour properly.

1. **The classical minimum.** Derive $v_{top}^2 = gr$ from
   [[Centripetal Force]], then use conservation of energy to get the
   frictionless minimum release height of $2.5r$. Show the derivation, not
   the result.
2. **The rolling correction.** A rolling ball carries kinetic energy in
   its SPIN as well as its motion. For a solid sphere rolling without
   slipping, the total is $\tfrac{7}{10}mv^2$ rather than
   $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$. Redo the minimum release height with that
   correction. The answer is $2.7r$, and deriving it is the heart of
   this task.
3. **The measured height.** Find the release height that actually works.
   Compare with both predictions.
4. **What is left over.** Whatever gap remains after the rolling
   correction is friction and air resistance. Quantify it as a fraction of
   the release energy, and say where it went.
5. **A design recommendation.** Real coasters use clothoid loops — tighter
   at the top than a circle — rather than circular ones. Explain why, in
   terms of the $v^2/r$ your own analysis produced.

## What separates a good report from an excellent one

The excellent one states, before doing the arithmetic, which principle
applies at each stage and why: energy for the height-to-speed step,
circular motion for the condition at the top, and a clear statement of
what is assumed to be conserved and over what interval.

## What is yours alone

You build, run and measure together, and the data table is shared —
which is right, because one person cannot release the ball and read the
height at the same time. **Items 4 and 5 you write separately**, each of
you, and they are what your own mark is drawn from. They are the two
places in this task where the answer is a judgement rather than a
derivation, and two identical judgements are one judgement.

## How this is assessed

Read your own report against these rows before you hand it in — see
[[Judging Your Own Work]].

| Quality | What it looks like in your report |
| --- | --- |
| The derivation is shown, not quoted | Every step from the condition at the top to the release height, in symbols, with the principle named before the step that uses it |
| The rolling correction is understood | The $\tfrac{7}{10}$ accounted for rather than asserted, and the new minimum height derived from it rather than copied from this page |
| Prediction and measurement are both there, with their uncertainties | Both numbers, both ranges, and a measured release height found by measuring rather than by knowing the answer is $2.7r$ |
| The leftover energy is accounted for | What remains after the rolling correction, as a fraction of the release energy, with a destination named for it |
| The recommendation follows from your own numbers | The clothoid argument made in terms of the $v^2/r$ your own analysis produced, not in terms of what you read about coasters |

> [!note] Why this task exists
> It is the same apparatus as Grade 11 and a different course. The
> discrepancy that had to be waved at with the word "friction" turns out to
> be mostly not friction at all — it is energy hiding in the rotation, and
> finding that is a genuinely satisfying piece of physics.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

The derivation is the heart of this task and it is also the part that
gets tidied up before you see it. Nobody hands in the page where they
substituted too early; they hand in the fair copy. So both prompts below
sit in the derivation period — you are already going pair to pair for
the conference, and what you walk past on the way is the observation.

OBSERVE — Unit 2, Day 13, the derivation working period
  Watch for: how long the page stays in letters. The agenda asks for the
  minimum speed in symbols, so everyone has been told; what you are
  finding out is who can actually do it under time pressure and who
  reaches for a number to feel safer. A fair copy looks symbolic either
  way.
  Going well: ten minutes of a whiteboard with no digits on it, and the
  pair arguing about which principle applies before either writes.
  Stuck: 9.8 in the second line, and arithmetic that will all have to be
  redone when the radius changes.
  Record: two columns — symbols held, numbers early. It is a glance per
  pair.

TALK — Unit 2, Day 13, at the conference already on that agenda
  Its printed question, and item 4 of this page, have both already been
  read, so ask them and then go past them.
  Ask: "Somebody claims a loop that returns the car to its starting
  height every single time. Where does that break, in your numbers
  rather than in principle?"
  Then: "If the ball slid instead of rolling, which way does your
  predicted release height move?"
  A strong first answer points at their own leftover-energy figure and
  says that transfer only goes one way; a weak one recites that
  perpetual motion is impossible. A strong second answer says the height
  drops back towards two and a half radii, because energy that was being
  paid into the spin no longer has to be. That is C3.4 heard — the
  implications of conservation for a real mechanical system — and C2.3,
  the energy argument used to solve the problem rather than quoted at
  it. Neither is separable from a tidy report.
  Record: one line per pair on the conference list.

The product evidence is the four pages, in on Day 16, with the model or
the video.
