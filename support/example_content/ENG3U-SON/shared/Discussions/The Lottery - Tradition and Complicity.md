[[The Lottery]] never explains what the lottery is for. Nobody in the
village can, either — the black box is chipped and shabby, part of the
original ritual has already been dropped, and the whole thing keeps
happening anyway. This seminar is about what that says about tradition, and
about who is actually responsible for what happens at the end.

> [!note] Why this story still gets talked about
> When "The Lottery" was first published, in *The New Yorker* in June 1948,
> the magazine received hundreds of letters — many readers were disturbed,
> some cancelled their subscriptions, and a number wrote in asking where
> they could go and watch a real lottery like it. Jackson herself said she
> got more mail about this one story than anything else she ever wrote.
> That reaction is worth holding next to today's questions: a story about a
> village that goes along with something terrible produced readers who,
> first, went along with believing it was real.

## The questions

1. Old Man Warner defends the lottery by insisting tradition alone
   justifies it — "there's always been a lottery." Is he wrong? If so, what
   exactly is wrong with that reasoning, and not just the conclusion it
   leads to?
2. Tessie Hutchinson never objects to the lottery itself — only to being
   the one chosen, once she's chosen. Does that change how much sympathy
   she deserves?
3. Everyone in the village, including the children, picks up a stone. Is a
   villager who never proposes the lottery, never leads it, but still
   throws when told to, guilty in the same way as the person running it?
4. The story never tells us what the lottery is FOR anymore — nobody
   remembers. Does that make the ritual more frightening than if there were
   still a reason behind it, or less?

A strong answer to question 3 resists collapsing the whole village into one
mass of equal guilt, because the story itself doesn't do that — it gives
Old Man Warner a speech defending the practice, gives Mr. Summers the job of
running it every year, and gives most other villagers nothing but
participation. The stronger claim names that difference and then argues
whether participation without leadership is still complicity, using at
least one specific villager as evidence rather than "the town" as a whole.
