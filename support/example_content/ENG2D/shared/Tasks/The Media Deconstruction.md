> [!abstract] At a glance
> Pairs · five class periods · one analysis of about 600 words, one media
> text you make, and a note of your own · assessed on Thinking,
> Application, and Communication

## The task

Take apart a media text that works on you, then make one of your own
using the same techniques deliberately.

## Part one: the analysis

Choose **one** text: an advertisement, a trailer, a documentary segment,
a front page, a feed you screenshot yourself, or a piece of political
communication. Not one you find stupid — one that works.

Answer, with evidence from the text itself:

1. Who made it, and what did they want to happen?
2. Who is the intended audience, and how can you tell from the text
   rather than from a guess?
3. What is the stated message, and what is the implied one?
4. Which four techniques carry it? Name them from
   [[Media Forms and Techniques]] and say what each does.
5. What is left out?

## Part two: the text you make

A poster, a thirty-second video, a front page, or a social card — using
at least three of the techniques you identified, aimed at an audience you
name. It may sell, argue, warn, or persuade; it may not lie.

## Part three: your own note

Two hundred words, written in class on the revision day, one per person.
This is the piece of the task that is marked as yours alone.

- **Your audience**, named as people rather than as an age bracket.
- **The form you chose** — poster, video, front page, social card — and
  why that form for those people rather than another one.
- **Your three techniques**, and which of the three did the most work.
- **Two things you can already do as a reader or a writer** that you used
  while making this.
- **What you would do differently** if you made a second one, and why.

> [!important] There is no group mark on this task
> The analysis and the made text are the pair's work. Your note is yours,
> it is written in class, and it is the part of this task that carries
> your individual mark.

## How it is marked

| Category | What I am looking for |
| --- | --- |
| Thinking | The analysis reads the text rather than describing it; the "left out" answer is specific |
| Application | The made text uses its techniques on purpose rather than by accident |
| Communication | All three parts are clear, and the audience the made text is aimed at is identifiable from the text itself |
| Application, in your note | The form is justified for those particular people, the three techniques are named and ranked, two reading or writing skills you used are named, and the change you would make is specific |

> [!important] The ethics line
> You may persuade. You may not fabricate: no invented statistics, no
> invented quotations, no real person made to appear to say something
> they did not. The whole point of learning these techniques is knowing
> what it means to use them — see [[Who Is This For]].

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 7, the first ten minutes of the making period
  The made text arrives in one form. It can never show you the form that
  was rejected, or whether one was ever considered — and choosing the
  form is half of what part two is for.
  Watch for: whether a pair puts up more than one way of doing it before
  the first one starts getting built. A pair who open a poster template
  in the first ninety seconds have chosen a tool, not a form.
  Going well: two rough starts side by side; a pair who give up on a
  video because they cannot get the sound they need, and can say what
  they lose by dropping it; an argument about one image against three.
  Stuck: the first idea, executed, with the audience named afterwards to
  fit it.
  Record: pairs list, three states — tried a second form, argued about
  the first, took the first. Ten minutes, then you are free.
  Evidence for D3.2: selecting a media form to suit the topic, purpose
  and audience, and explaining why it is an appropriate choice. The note
  carries the explaining; only this period carries the choosing, and a
  pair who never had a second option are explaining a decision they never
  made.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 5, while the room researches what its text left out
  That period has no job of yours in it after the tutorial: you teach
  Research and Sources at the top and then the room works for half an
  hour. Take partners one at a time, on their own and not across the
  desk — the note is individual and so is this. You will reach most of
  the room; the pairs you miss are the ones to find in the making period.
  Neither question is the analysis's own second one, which they answered
  in writing before this class; that one stops at who the text was made
  for.
  Ask: "The text you took apart was aimed at somebody. Who else sees it,
  who it was never made for — and what do they get from it?"
  Then: "And the one you are about to build on day 3 — who will see that
  who you are not aiming at?"
  A strong answer names a particular second audience and a particular
  different reading — the advertisement that is aspiration to the people
  it was made for and a joke at the expense of the people in it; the
  front page that is reassurance in one house and a warning in the next.
  A weak one is "everyone would like it". That is D1.1 heard: explaining
  how a media text is created to suit a particular purpose and audience.
  The people it was not made for are only the route in — a student who
  can say what a text does to somebody outside its audience has
  understood how tightly it was aimed. The written analysis rarely gets
  there, because the question on the page stops at who the text is for.
  The second answer is worth more than the first, because it is a
  prediction: they have not built anything yet, and on Day 9 the gallery
  will tell all of you whether they were right.
  Record: pairs list, two initials and one word each — the second
  audience they named. Blank is the useful entry.

The products arrive on their own: the analysis, the made text and each
partner's own note, handed in on Unit 4, Day 9.
