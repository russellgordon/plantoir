> [!abstract] At a glance
> Groups of three · one 35-minute seminar led by your group, plus the
> five you do not lead · marked individually, never as a group · assessed
> on all four categories

## The task

Lead a discussion of one act of *Hamlet* that the room could not have had
without your preparation.

## What your group brings

1. **A question the play does not settle**, approved two classes ahead.
2. **Four passages** with act, scene, and line — at least one of them
   chosen and prepared by you.
3. **A lens**, named: which critical approach your question comes from,
   and why that one. See [[Critical Approaches]].
4. **A plan for the silence** and a closing move.
5. **The passages where the room can see them** — a handout, a slide, or
   the lines written up — and one of the four **heard as well as read**:
   a recorded performance, or two of you reading it aloud having
   prepared it differently. Then say what the performance decided, and
   what in the lines let it decide that. Both are prepared the evening
   after your question is approved.
6. **A division of the thirty-five minutes**, written on the plan you
   hand me on approval day: who opens, who takes the middle, who closes.
   That stretch is the part of the seminar you are marked on.

## What is yours alone

You prepare together and you are marked separately — there is no group
mark here, and nobody's mark depends on anybody else's preparation.
Yours comes from four things: the stretch of the thirty-five minutes you
lead, named in advance on the plan; the passage you bring and what you
do with it; your follow-up; and what you do in the five seminars you do
not lead. Every row of the table below is read against your stretch, not
against the group's half-hour.

Two groups lead on each of the three seminar days, and those periods run
end to end — no opening, no landing.

## What leading looks like at this level

You speak less than a fifth of the time, and your job is to make other
people's contributions collide productively. The moves are on
[[Seminar Skills]]; the one that separates a strong seminar from an
adequate one is **inviting the objection you fear**.

Most of making room is not spoken. Where you are looking when somebody
is finishing, which way you turn, whether your hand goes up before they
have stopped — the room reads all of it, and so do I.

## The five you do not lead

Assessed on the same terms: evidence, building, conceding. In this course
a concession is worth more than a clever point, because it is the only
move that proves listening happened.

## After your seminar

Once every group has led, the whole room writes a short follow-up, in
class, in about ten minutes. Three things, in your own words:

- **The move that worked** — one thing you did as a speaker or as a
  listener that changed what the discussion did next, and how you know.
- **The moment you lost the room**, or the moment you talked over
  somebody, or the objection you decided not to invite. One of these
  happened.
- **The one thing you will do differently**, and where you will next get
  to try it: the discussions still ahead, the four-person testing in
  Unit 3, and the questions you ask at the defences in Unit 4.

This is marked, and it is the one place in the course where how you work
is part of the mark rather than reported beside it: reflecting on
yourself as a listener and a speaker is a curriculum expectation here,
not a habit grade. See [[How Marks Work]]. It is written in class
because a reflection written at home a fortnight later is a memory of a
memory.

## How it is marked

| Category | What I am looking for |
| --- | --- |
| Knowledge | The act is known closely; the passages are the right ones for the question |
| Thinking | The discussion tests something; your lens is used rather than announced |
| Communication | You listen, respond precisely, and make room |
| Application | The room can see the passages and has heard one of them; the performance is used as evidence about the lines, not as decoration |

The follow-up is marked against its own three points, above.

> [!tip] Questions that have worked here
> "Does the Mousetrap prove anything?" · "What does the play do with
> Ophelia's silence in Act 3, and who is responsible for it?" · "Is
> Horatio a character or a device?"

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 2, Days 11, 13 and 15, and specifically the ninety
seconds after each group plays its heard passage.
  That clip is new to this task and it is the only moment in six
  seminars when the whole room has just met the same evidence at the
  same instant. What happens next is either interpretation or politeness,
  and no transcript would tell them apart.
  Watch for: whether anybody's next contribution is about the
  PERFORMANCE — the pause the actor took, the line thrown away, the
  choice to say it to the audience rather than to the other character —
  or whether the discussion resumes from the printed text as though
  nothing had been played.
  Going well: somebody disagrees with the recording and goes to the line
  to do it; a listener asks the group why they chose that version; a
  position visibly moves between the clip and the next speaker.
  Stuck: the clip ends, the room waits, and the question that follows
  could have been asked before it was played.
  Record: your seating plan, photocopied six times. A circle beside
  anyone who used the recording, a tick per contribution. Nothing after
  the bell.
  Evidence for A1.5: developing and explaining an interpretation of an
  ORAL text, using the oral and visual cues in it — which is what a
  recorded performance is and what the printed page is not.

TALK — Unit 2, Day 9, the question-approval slot already on that agenda,
while the rest of the room annotates.
  Ask: "Read me your key passage aloud, the way you want the room to
  hear it." Then, when they stop: "Which words did you slow down on, and
  why those?"
  A strong answer is audible before it is spoken — the reading itself
  makes the choices — and then names the shade that mattered: *seems*
  against *is*, *ground* meaning proof rather than earth, *conscience*
  carrying both knowledge and guilt. That is B3.3 heard rather than
  written, and it separates four passages that were chosen from four
  that were collected.
  The second minute is worth taking too: "What will you do if the room
  agrees with you in the first four minutes?" The groups that have an
  answer are holding a passage in reserve; the ones that do not have
  planned a lesson, not a discussion.
  Record: two words on the approval sheet, in front of them.

The products arrive on their own: the seminar, and the follow-up written
on Day 17.
