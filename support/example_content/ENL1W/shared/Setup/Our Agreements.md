English is the subject where the material gets personal without warning.
A story about a family argument, a poem about a language somebody lost, a
news article about a place your family is from — any of it can land
somewhere tender. These are the agreements that make that survivable.

## What we agree to

1. **Attack the idea, never the person.** "That reading of the poem does
   not fit the last stanza" is English. "You always say stupid things" is
   not, and ends the discussion for everyone.
2. **You may pass.** In a circle discussion, "pass" is a complete
   sentence, and nobody will ask why.
3. **What is said here about somebody's own life stays here.** Ideas
   travel; personal disclosures do not.
4. **Disagreement is the point, not a problem.** A class where everybody
   agrees has stopped reading and started nodding.
5. **You may change your mind out loud.** That is the most advanced
   thing that happens in this room, and it will be treated that way.
6. **Ask about a word you do not know.** Every single time. There is no
   version of this class where that is embarrassing.

## The texts, and why some of them are hard

Several texts in this course deal with residential schools, racism, and loss.
They are here because they are true, because they are well written, and
because
[[A3.3|the course asks us to learn from First Nations, Métis, and Inuit voices]]
rather than about them at a distance.

You will always know before we start what a text deals with. If you need
to step out of a class, step out; you do not need to explain, and it will
not cost you anything. Come and talk to me afterwards and we will find
another way in.

> [!important] Whose stories these are
> When we read an Indigenous writer, we are guests in that writer's
> telling. That means naming the nation the writer belongs to, using the
> words they use, and not deciding on their behalf what their story is
> "really" about. [[Indigenous Storywork]] explains how that changes the
> way we read.

## Using AI honestly

Language models will write you a passable paragraph. They will not learn
anything for you, and this course is entirely about what happens in your
head.

| Fine | Not fine |
| --- | --- |
| Asking for a word you are reaching for | Having it write the sentence |
| Asking it to explain a term from a text | Having it summarise a text you were asked to read |
| Asking it to quiz you before a discussion | Having it produce your ideas about a text |

Where you use it, say so and say how, in one line at the end of the
piece. That is the same rule professional writers work under, and it
costs you nothing.
