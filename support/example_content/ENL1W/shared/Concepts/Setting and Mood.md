Setting is where and when. Mood is how it feels to be there. The first is
information; the second is craft, and it is built almost entirely out of
word choice.

## Setting does four jobs

1. **It makes the world specific.** "A school" is nowhere. "A portable
   with a broken heater partway through" is somewhere.
2. **It applies pressure.** Winter, distance, a curfew, a border — a
   setting can be an antagonist.
3. **It carries history.** A place has a past, and in Canadian writing
   the past of a place is often the subject: whose land, whose road,
   whose school.
4. **It reveals character.** What a person notices about a room tells you
   what they are afraid of.

## Mood is made of small choices

Take a hallway. *The hallway was long and the lights hummed.* Now:
*The hallway stretched, and the lights buzzed like something trapped.*
Same hallway. The difference is two verbs and a simile — and the second
version has told you to be uneasy without saying so.

Notice the moves available:

- **Verbs**: *stretched* rather than *was long*.
- **Sound**: hums are neutral, buzzes are agitated.
- **Comparison**: *like something trapped* imports a whole feeling.
- **Sentence length**: short sentences quicken the pulse; long ones let
  dread build.

## Atmosphere versus tone

**Mood** is what the reader feels. **Tone** is the writer's attitude to
the material — amused, furious, tender, cold. They are usually aligned
and occasionally, deliberately, not: a tender tone about something
horrifying is one of the most disturbing effects in writing.

> [!tip] For your own writing
> Do not tell us the weather unless the weather is doing something. Every
> detail of setting should be earning its place: making the world real,
> making the situation harder, or telling us something about the person
> noticing it.
