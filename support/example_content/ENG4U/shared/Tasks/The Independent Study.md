## The task

Choose a text, read it alone, research it, build an argument, write it at
length, and then defend it out loud to me and to two classmates who have
read your essay.

[[Choosing Your Independent Study Text]] has the list and the rules.

## The checkpoints

| Checkpoint | When | What is due |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. The choice** | End of Unit 1 | Text approved, plus a paragraph on why this one |
| **2. The reading** | Middle of Unit 2 | Annotations, a passage log of at least sixteen entries, three candidate questions |
| **3. The argument** | End of Unit 3 | Thesis with a mechanism, a full outline, three secondary sources logged with your own sentence on each |
| **4. The draft** | Middle of Unit 4 | A complete draft, workshopped in class |

Each is assessed. They exist because the failure mode of a semester-long
project is invisible until the end of the course, and completely fixable at
the second checkpoint.

## The essay

Two thousand words, evidence from across the whole text, at least three
secondary sources used to extend or complicate rather than to agree, a
counter-reading section, and a conclusion that says what follows. Method
on [[The Extended Essay]] and [[Research Writing]].

## The defence

Ten minutes, out loud, with nothing to read from. Pace and pauses are
part of it — a defence delivered too fast to follow has not answered
anything. I ask, and so do your two readers:

- Why that passage and not the one before it?
- What is the strongest objection to your reading, and why does it fail?
- Which source changed your mind, and how?
- What would you need to find in the text to abandon your thesis?
- This is the one text this term nobody read alongside you. What do you
  do as a reader that you learned somewhere else in this course, and
  where did it change what you noticed?

Being asked those in front of somebody is the fastest way to find out
whether an argument is genuinely yours.

## How it is marked

| Category | What I am looking for |
| --- | --- |
| Knowledge | The whole text; evidence from all of it, not the first hundred pages |
| Thinking | An argument that could only come from reading this text closely |
| Communication | 2,000 words a reader can hold, and a defence that answers what was asked, at a pace a listener can follow |
| Application | Research integrated and cited; conventions correct throughout |

In the workshop period you will judge your own draft against this table
before the rewrite — [[Judging Your Own Work]] — and the rewrite period
that follows exists for whatever that turns up.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

The defence on Unit 4, Days 9 to 11 is a PRODUCT, not a slot for either
of these. Its questions are published a class in advance and you are
chairing the room while it happens, so what you hear has been drafted
and you have no hand free to record it. Both slots below sit earlier,
where the work is still moving.

OBSERVE — Unit 3, Day 22, the twenty minutes of the research tutorial in
which two sources are found and judged in the room.
  Watch for: how a source gets put DOWN. The one that ends up in the log
  is the one that survived; the log will never mention the four that did
  not, or say why. This is the one period in the whole project where the
  deciding is in front of you instead of in somebody's bedroom.
  Going well: reading past the first screen before deciding; checking
  who wrote it and when; writing their own sentence about a source
  before copying anything out of it.
  Stuck: two sources chosen in six minutes, both agreeing with the
  thesis, neither read past the abstract.
  Record: class list, one word each for the reason a source was put
  down — "old", "not about my text", "just agrees with me". The last of
  those is worth a conversation.
  Evidence for C1.3: locating and SELECTING information that will
  actually support the ideas being written about. Selecting is a verb,
  and this is the only twenty minutes in which you can watch anybody do
  it.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 1, the checkpoint-three conferences already on that
agenda, while the rest of the room drafts.
  Ask: "Name one thing you do when you read that you have started doing
  when you write. Where in this outline can I see it?" Then: "Nobody in
  this room has read your text. In three sentences, what do I need to
  know before your first quotation means anything?"
  A strong first answer names a transferable move and points at it —
  marking a shift in register while reading, then using paragraph breaks
  to do the same work in prose. That is C4.2 heard rather than inferred.
  The essay will show the effect; only the student can tell you where it
  came from, and a student who cannot has been imitating an essay rather
  than writing one.
  The second is a live audience problem, answered out loud with no notes,
  which is A2.1 under the only conditions that test it. Watch what gets
  cut: a student who leads with plot has not decided what the argument
  needs.
  Record: two words per student on the checkpoint sheet, in front of
  them.

The products arrive on their own: four checkpoints, the essay, and the
defence.
