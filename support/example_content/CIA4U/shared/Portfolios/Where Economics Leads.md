Most people who use economics for a living are not economists. The
reasoning travels — into law, journalism, policy, human resources, and
running a business — far more often than the job title does. This page
asks you to find where it lands for you.

| Role | The economic skill it actually uses | Where you would see it in Canada |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Accountant | Reading cost structures; distinguishing a real cost from a sunk one | Audit and tax work for firms of every size |
| Banker | Pricing risk; reading interest rate and credit conditions | Lending decisions after a Bank of Canada rate announcement |
| Economist | Building and testing models against published series | Statistics Canada, the Bank of Canada, a bank's research desk |
| Educator | Explaining a model honestly, including its limits | Any classroom where an economic claim gets made |
| Entrepreneur | Marginal thinking; reading demand and a competitor's structure | Deciding whether one more location earns its cost |
| Financial consultant | Real against nominal returns; the arithmetic of time | Advising on savings when inflation runs above target |
| Fundraiser | Incentives, and what actually moves a donor | A charity's campaign design and its results |
| Human resources manager | Labour supply, wage setting, and turnover cost | Setting pay bands against a tightening labour market |
| Journalist | Judging whether a number supports the sentence around it | A story on the monthly Labour Force Survey release |
| Lawyer | Regulation, market power, and who bears a cost | Competition Bureau proceedings; contract and trade disputes |
| Policy analyst | Trade-offs, distribution, and unintended effects | A briefing note on a housing or tax measure |
| Politician | Allocating scarce resources among competing claims | Any budget debate, and the document behind it |

Read that list against what you have already done in this course. If you
can take a statistical table, extract the figure that bears on a
question, and write a paragraph a skeptical reader cannot knock down,
you have the transferable core of every row above. Those are the Ontario
Skills Passport essential skills — document use, numeracy, writing,
computer use, oral communication — in their working form.

Be honest about the shape of it. Two of these twelve roles are studied as
economics; the rest use it as a tool alongside something else. That is
the argument for taking the course seriously even if you never take
another one: the reasoning is portable, and the job titles are not the
measure of where it goes.

The test you will keep applying is in [[Judging an Economic Claim]]; the
demonstration is [[The Economic Issue Report]]. This page is written in
class, in the period after that report is delivered, and
[[The Notebook and Portfolio]] says what it is judged on.
