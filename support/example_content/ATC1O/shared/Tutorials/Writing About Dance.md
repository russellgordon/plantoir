Writing about movement is harder than writing about a book, because the thing
you are describing has already gone — you cannot quote a dance. What you can
do is name what happened precisely enough that a reader who was not there can
see it, and that skill carries most of your mark on every written task here.

## Describe before you judge

The first sentence anybody writes about a dance is a verdict: it was moving,
it was awkward, it was amazing. Verdicts are where writing ends, not where it
starts. Describe first, judge afterwards, and the judgement usually changes.

- Not *the duet was tense* — **they stayed in arm's reach and never touched.**
- Not *the ending was weak* — **the music stopped four counts before they did.**

The second version is what the first was made of, and a reader can disagree
with it — which is exactly what makes it useful.

## Name the elements

When the words will not come, run [[The Elements of Dance]] through your head:
body, space, time, energy, relationship. Ask which one the moment was about. A
description stalls when you describe the *body* and what struck you was the
*timing*.

## Use terminology as a tool, not decoration

A term you can define earns its place; a term dropped in to sound expert costs
you. If you write *retrograde*, your sentence should make clear the phrase ran
backwards — [[Dance Terminology]] is the working list. The same rule governs
naming a form: give what identifies it, such as the foot patterns or the
relationship to the pulse, rather than asserting the label. Those
characteristics live in [[Forms From Around the World]].

## Evidence a claim with a moment

Every claim needs a moment attached — a point you could rewind to:

> The piece is about hesitation rather than fear. ==Twice, the soloist began
> the same reaching phrase and stopped it at the elbow== — the second time
> with the group already still behind her. The movement is not frightened; it
> is unfinished, and the ending leaves it that way.

Take the highlighted clause out and the paragraph is only an opinion. Leave it
in and the reader can check you. [[The Critical Analysis Process]] sets the
method out at length, and [[Content and Interpretation]] — what was danced
against what it communicated — is the distinction most of your paragraphs turn
on. Practise small: four lines in [[Your Movement Journal]] after every class
is what makes [[The Dance Review]] possible by the end of the course.
