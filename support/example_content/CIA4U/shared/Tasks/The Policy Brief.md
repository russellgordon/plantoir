**Launched mid-Unit 3; delivered in the last week of the unit.** Pairs.

Two pages, one recommendation, written for someone who has fifteen minutes
and has to decide.

## The form

A policy brief is not an essay. It opens with the recommendation, supports
it, names what could go wrong, and stops. Two pages is a hard limit, and
the discipline is the assignment: everything you know that does not change
the decision comes out.

## The question

Choose one macroeconomic question currently live in Canada, and take a
side. Workable: should fiscal policy be tightened or loosened given the
deficit track set out in the Spring Economic Update of 28 April 2026;
should the Bank of Canada's inflation-control agreement, which runs to
31 December 2026, be renewed unchanged; how should the federal government
respond to a specific trade shock; what should be done about business
investment.

## What the brief must contain

**The recommendation**, in one sentence, at the top.

**The fiscal side.** D2.1 asks what actually shapes fiscal policy in
Canada — the economic cycle, stakeholder demands, the responsibilities
shared with provinces, infrastructure, debt service. Say which of those
constrains your recommendation.

**The monetary side.** D3.1 and D3.2 ask what the Bank of Canada can do and
how money and interest rates transmit to the economy. The policy rate was
held at 2.25% on 15 July 2026 with the next fixed announcement date of
2 September 2026 — retrieve the current one from series `V39079` before you
submit, and say what it is on the day you write.

**The politics of it.** D2.2: who will try to influence this decision, by
what means, and how effective is each likely to be? Voting, lobbying,
petitions, media campaigns and protest are not equally powerful, and
saying which is which is analysis.

**What could go wrong.** Two named risks and the indicator that would tell
you early.

> [!warning] Do not recommend both tightening and loosening
> The commonest weak brief hedges until it has recommended nothing. A brief
> that takes a clear position and names its risks is stronger than one that
> is impossible to disagree with — and it is far easier to mark generously.

## How it is marked

- **A recommendation, in one sentence, that somebody could act on.** Not a
  topic and not a range of options.
- **A constraint named on each side.** One fiscal factor from D2.1 and one
  monetary mechanism from D3.1 that genuinely bear on this recommendation,
  with the current rate retrieved and dated.
- **Influence assessed, not listed.** Who will try to move this decision,
  by what means, and which of those means is actually likely to work.
- **Two risks and an early indicator for each.** Named series, not moods.
- **Two pages, and the cutting shows.** What stayed is what changes the
  decision.
- **Your own answer at the delivery.** One question from the floor, taken
  without help, on your own sections — judged against the five above,
  exactly as the writing is.

## Working in pairs, marked individually

Two authors, two marks. Divide the brief so that each of you writes and
signs named sections — the fiscal side and the politics, say, against
the monetary side and the risks — and put on the page who wrote what.
The recommendation at the top is agreed between the two of you, and it is
not marked as a thing on its own. What I mark on each of your papers is
whether YOUR sections actually carry that recommendation, and your own
answer when a question comes from the floor at the delivery.

Neither the sections nor the answer is shared, and nothing your partner
or anyone on the floor says about your work is part of your mark.

Build from [[Reading a Budget]], [[Circular Flow]],
[[The Money Multiplier and Monetary Policy]] and
[[Aggregate Supply and Demand]].
