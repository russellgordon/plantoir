Your mark reflects what you can do with civic evidence and civic
reasoning. It does not reflect which positions you hold — see
[[Our Working Agreement]], where that promise is made in full.

## What this course is

Civics and Citizenship is a **half credit**: 55 hours of scheduled
instructional time, which here is 42 periods plus a two-and-a-half-hour
final evaluation. It runs across **the second half of the semester**, picking
up the timetable slot Career Studies had for the first half of the
semester.

Ten weeks is not long. It is why the notebook starts in week one and why
almost nothing in this course is a review period.

## The seventy and the thirty

Every Ontario credit, half or whole, is built the same way. **Seventy per
cent** of your mark comes from work spread across the term, and it leans
towards your **most recent and most consistent** work rather than
averaging the start of the course against the end of it. A skill you did
not have in week two and have in week nine is a skill you have.
**Thirty per cent** comes from
a final evaluation at the end: [[The Issue Examination]], two and a half
hours on sources nobody has seen before.

**The seventy** is the five pieces you hand in during the term — in the
order you meet them, [[How Government Works]], [[The Issue Brief]],
[[The Rights Case]], [[Whose Voice Is Missing]] and
[[The Civic Action Project]] — plus the three seminars, the milestone
entries you write in class in [[Your Civic Notebook]], and the final
entry, [[Where You Stand Now]]. They are not equally weighted: the Civic
Action Project is worth several of the smaller ones on its own, because
it runs ten periods and asks for everything at once, and the three
seminars together are the smallest share of it. Ask me for the exact
split whenever you want it. It is not a secret; it is just a professional
judgement rather than arithmetic, and it is not the useful thing to
memorise.

**The thirty** is the examination alone, and it is deliberately about
sources you have not studied. It is the one place the whole course has to
travel at once.

The notebook entries that count are the ones written **in class, at a
milestone** — one after each of the three seminars, in the last ten
minutes of that period, plus the final entry in the second-to-last class.
The weekly current-events entry you do at home is practice: it is the
best preparation for the examination there is, and it is not something I
mark.

## Four kinds of thinking, not one

Every task asks for more than one of these, and the balance shifts with
the task. A correct account of who holds a power is never the whole of
it.

| What is being judged | What it means here |
| --- | --- |
| What you know | Orders of government, the constitutional documents, how a bill becomes law |
| How you think | Weighing a limit against a standard, testing a claim, choosing between routes |
| How you communicate | A brief a busy reader can act on, a position stated so its opponent recognises it |
| Where you apply it | Using all of it on an issue nobody worked through in front of you |

The fourth is why Unit 3 exists, and why the examination uses an issue
the course never taught.

## Three kinds of evidence, and two of them are not written down

**What you make** is the obvious one: briefs, cases, reports, the
artefact you send. **What I watch you do** is the second: whether you
open the decision itself or somebody's summary of it, whether you count
before you argue, whether you check a power against a source rather than
assigning it from instinct. **What you tell me** is the third — the
conferences in the work periods and the three seminars are not progress
checks, they are evidence in their own right.

If your writing is careful and quiet, the conference is often where the
strongest evidence of the week comes from. That is not a consolation
prize. It is how the mark is meant to work.

The seminars are marked against two expectations, and it is worth knowing
which. **B1.5** is the reasoning half: your own position on a civic
issue, with the evidence for it and the beliefs and values underneath it
said out loud. **A2.2** is the other half: whether you can state a
position you reject so that somebody holding it would accept your
version, on a topic where that is genuinely hard. Neither of them is
marked on how often you speak.

## What earns a high mark here

- **A claim with a source attached.** A dated fact somebody else can
  check beats a confident sentence every time.
- **The other side, at its strongest.** Stating the best version of a
  position you reject, then answering it, is the clearest single signal
  of a strong civics answer. It appears in the criteria of most tasks in
  this course.
- **Precision about who holds power.** "The government" loses marks;
  naming the order, the body, and the instrument earns them.
- **Honesty about uncertainty and about outcomes.** "The evidence does
  not settle this, and here is what would" is worth marks, not lost ones.
  So is a civic action that did not work, reported accurately.
- **Your own judgement.** Every assessed piece ends in a position. A
  summary of an issue, however accurate, is not an answer.

## What is not in your mark

How you work is reported separately, on the same report card, as **E, G,
S or N** — six habits: responsibility, organization, independent work,
collaboration, initiative, and self-regulation. They matter, we will talk
about them, and they do not move your percentage. Your mark is about the
work; that column is about the worker, and mixing the two tells you less
about both. Nothing here is marked for turning up, for volunteering, or
for effort.

The one exception is narrow and it is written into the curriculum: how
you conduct a disagreement — listening to a perspective you find wrong,
and answering it rather than the caricature of it — is expectation A2.2,
which this course is required to teach and therefore assesses. It is
assessed in [[The Rights Case]] as well as in the seminars, so it is not
a mark that exists only in my head. That is a named skill in the
document, not a tidy way of marking politeness.

Your own judgement of your work, and your classmates', are not part of
your mark either. You will judge your own work against the criteria
often — see [[Judging Your Own Work]] — and a classmate will put a
hostile question to you more than once. Both change the work; neither
moves a number.

Practice you do between classes is practice. The reading, the notebook's
weekly entry, and finding your sources are how the periods become
possible, and none of them is marked on its own. The work that carries
marks is done in this room, where I can see it being done.

## Late work, and revision

Talk to me before the deadline and we set a new one. Work that arrives
without that conversation is still marked, but in a ten-week course the
feedback reaches you too late to use, which is the real cost.

Every task in this course has a checkpoint before it is marked, and a
named block of time afterwards for acting on what the checkpoint found.
How much time depends on the task, and it is written on the class page:
[[The Rights Case]] gets a **whole period** with nothing else in it;
[[How Government Works]] gets **all of one but the last twenty minutes**;
[[The Issue Brief]] and [[Whose Voice Is Missing]] get the **first
fifteen or twenty minutes** of the next class; and the plan for
[[The Civic Action Project]] gets the **rest of the checkpoint period**
and the start of the one after it. In a ten-week course that is what
there is — it is on the schedule, and it is not optional.

> [!tip] The criteria live on the task page
> Every task ends with a table of what earns the marks for that specific
> piece of work. Read it before you start, not after you finish — and if
> a criterion is unclear, ask, because that question benefits everybody.
