A thin stream of water falls from a burette. Bring a charged plastic rod
near it — no contact, no spark — and the stream bends towards the rod
and stays bent. Run the same demonstration with a non-polar organic
liquid, well away from any flame, and the stream falls perfectly
straight.

Nothing was added and nothing reacted. The difference is in the
molecules themselves, and the shapes you worked out in
[[Molecular Shapes]] are about to earn their keep.

## A polar bond is not a polar molecule

Start one level down. When two atoms of different **electronegativity**
share a pair of electrons, the pair sits closer to the greedier atom.
That atom carries a small negative charge, written $\delta^-$, and its
partner carries $\delta^+$. The bond has a **dipole**: a size and a
direction, pointing from positive towards negative.

Rough guides on the Pauling scale, and rough is the honest word:

| Difference in electronegativity | Usually called | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Below about 0.4 | non-polar covalent | C–H, about 0.4 |
| About 0.4 to 1.7 | polar covalent | O–H, about 1.4 |
| Above about 1.7 | ionic | Na–Cl, about 2.1 |

Those boundaries are conventions, not laws, and they leak. Hydrogen
fluoride has a difference near 1.8 and is unmistakably a molecular gas,
not a salt. Bonding is a continuum, and the table is a naming
convenience laid across it.

Here is the step everybody skips. **A molecule made entirely of polar
bonds can have no dipole at all.** Bond dipoles are vectors. They add
like forces, not like magnitudes, and vectors pointing in opposite
directions cancel.

## Symmetry decides

Carbon dioxide has two strongly polar C=O bonds. It is linear, so the
two dipoles point directly away from each other, in exactly equal
measure, and their sum is zero. Carbon dioxide is **non-polar**.

Water has two O–H bonds that are individually less polar than the C=O
bonds in carbon dioxide. It is bent, so the two dipoles do not oppose —
they add to give a resultant pointing from the hydrogens towards the
oxygen, reinforced by the two lone pairs sitting on the other side.
Water is strongly polar, and that stream bends.

$$\ce{CO2 \text{ (linear, symmetric)} -> \text{non-polar}} \qquad \ce{H2O \text{ (bent)} -> \text{polar}}$$

A working test, in the order you should apply it:

1. Draw the structure and get the **shape** from
   [[Molecular Shapes]].
2. Mark a dipole on every bond between different elements.
3. Ask whether the arrangement is symmetric enough for them to cancel.

The shortcut version: **if every outer atom is identical and the central
atom has no lone pairs, the molecule is non-polar.** That rule catches
$\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{CCl4}$, $\ce{BF3}$, and $\ce{SO3}$, and it
is right often enough to trust — but it is a shortcut, not the
principle. Square planar $\ce{XeF4}$ has two lone pairs on the
central atom and is still non-polar, because those lone pairs sit
opposite each other and cancel too. When the shortcut and the vectors
disagree, the vectors win.

> [!example]- Four cases worth working through before you look
> **$\ce{CCl4}$** — four polar C–Cl bonds, tetrahedral, all outer
> atoms identical. The four dipoles point to the corners of a
> tetrahedron and sum to zero. Non-polar, and it does not mix with
> water.
>
> **$\ce{CHCl3}$** — swap one chlorine for a hydrogen and the
> cancellation is destroyed. The three C–Cl dipoles no longer have a
> fourth to balance them. Polar.
>
> **$\ce{NH3}$** — trigonal pyramidal, so the three N–H dipoles all
> have a component pointing the same way, up through the nitrogen, and
> the lone pair adds to it. Strongly polar.
>
> **$\ce{BF3}$ against $\ce{NF3}$** — both have three bonds to
> fluorine, and their polarities are completely different. Boron
> trifluoride is trigonal planar with no lone pair and cancels to zero.
> Nitrogen trifluoride is trigonal pyramidal and does not. Same outer
> atoms, same count, opposite answer. Shape is doing all the work.

## What polarity then predicts

This is not a classification exercise. Polarity is the first thing in
this course that lets you predict a property you have never measured.

- **Solubility.** "Like dissolves like" is a statement about dipoles.
  Polar and ionic substances dissolve in polar solvents because the
  attractions they lose to each other are replaced by attractions to the
  solvent. Non-polar substances have nothing to offer a water molecule
  that water molecules do not already offer each other.
- **Boiling point.** A polar molecule is held to its neighbours more
  tightly than a non-polar molecule of the same size, so more energy is
  needed to separate them. That is the beginning of the explanation for
  the data you collected in [[Boiling Points and Structure]], and
  [[Intermolecular Forces]] finishes it.
- **Which end reacts.** Later in the year, a reagent will attack the
  $\delta^+$ carbon of a carbonyl group rather than any other carbon in
  the molecule. It goes there because of a partial charge you can now
  predict from a drawing.

> [!warning] Polar molecule, non-polar molecule, ion — three things
> An ion has a **whole** charge and a molecule with a dipole has a
> **partial** one, and the difference is not a matter of degree. Sodium
> chloride does not dissolve because it is "very polar"; it dissolves
> because water molecules surround genuine, separate, charged particles.
> Writing $\delta^+$ rather than $+$ is the notation reminding you which
> situation you are in.

One last case, because it comes back in Unit 2. Two molecules can have
the same molecular formula, the same bonds, and different polarities:
1,2-dichloroethene has a form with both chlorines on the same side of
the double bond, which is polar, and a form with them on opposite
sides, which is not. They boil at different temperatures. That is a
preview of [[Isomers]], and it is the cleanest evidence there is that
shape is a physical property rather than a drawing convention.

Practise the whole chain — structure, shape, polarity, prediction — in
[[Shapes and Polarity Practice]], and put it to work in
[[The Property Prediction]].
