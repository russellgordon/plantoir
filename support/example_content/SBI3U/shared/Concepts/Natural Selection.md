Darwin's mechanism is four observations and one conclusion, and it is
worth stating in that order.

1. Organisms produce more offspring than can survive.
2. Individuals in a population vary.
3. Some of that variation is heritable.
4. Some variants survive and reproduce more than others.

**Therefore** the heritable variants that helped will be more common in
the next generation.

## What it is not

- It is not "survival of the fittest" if "fittest" means strongest.
  Fitness means reproductive success, and it is often about avoiding
  notice, or being tolerable to a pollinator.
- It is not directed. Nothing tries to evolve. Mutation is random with
  respect to need; selection is not random at all, which is the part
  people miss.
- It does not act on individuals. Individuals do not evolve; populations
  do.

## Darwin did not do this alone

The four observations above are usually filed under one name, and the
history is more interesting than that. Each piece arrived from somebody
working on a different problem.

| Who | What they contributed | What they got wrong, or did not do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Charles Lyell | Geology showing the Earth is very old and changes by slow processes still operating today — which is what makes gradual change possible at all | Did not apply the reasoning to living things for many years, and resisted Darwin's conclusion when it came |
| Thomas Malthus | The population argument: populations grow faster than their food supply, so most offspring must die. Observation 1 comes straight from him | Was writing about human economics and poverty policy, not biology at all |
| Jean-Baptiste Lamarck | Proposed, decades before Darwin, that species change over time rather than being fixed — a genuinely radical claim at the time | His mechanism, inheritance of characteristics acquired in a lifetime, is wrong: the giraffe does not stretch its neck and pass that on |
| Alfred Russel Wallace | Arrived at natural selection independently, from fieldwork in the Malay Archipelago; his 1858 letter to Darwin is what prompted publication | Is frequently left out of the story entirely, which is a fault of the telling rather than of the science |
| Charles Darwin | Assembled the observations into a mechanism and spent twenty years assembling the evidence for it | Had no idea how heredity worked — Mendel's work in [[Mendelian Genetics]] was unknown to him, which left a real gap |
| Stephen Jay Gould and Niles Eldredge | Punctuated equilibrium (1972): the fossil record shows long stable periods broken by rapid change, rather than steady gradual change throughout | Was widely misreported as a refutation of Darwin; it is an argument about *tempo*, inside the same mechanism |

Two things are worth taking from that table. **Lamarck was wrong about
the mechanism and right about the big claim** — species change — and
being usefully wrong in public is how the question got asked at all.
And **the theory kept being revised after Darwin died**: genetics
supplied the heredity he lacked, and Gould and Eldredge argued about the
rate. A theory that has been repeatedly amended is not a weak theory;
it is what a scientific explanation looks like when it is being used.

## The classic case, carefully

Peppered moths in industrial Britain: the dark form became common where
soot darkened the trees, and rare again after clean air legislation. The
story has been challenged and re-tested repeatedly, and it survived —
which makes it a better example than the tidy version usually told.
