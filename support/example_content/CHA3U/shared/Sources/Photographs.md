A photograph feels like proof, and that feeling is the problem. What it
proves is narrow and precise: that these things were arranged this way, in
front of this lens, at this moment, because somebody stood there and decided
to press the shutter. Everything else — what it means, what happened next,
what is outside the frame — is argument, and it is yours to make.

## Six things to establish before you interpret

**Who made it, and for whom.** Federal agencies, railroads, newspapers,
missions, reform societies and companies all employed photographers to
document their own programmes. A commissioned image is evidence about the
commissioner as much as about the subject.

**What is outside the frame.** Framing is the first act of interpretation
and it happened before you arrived.

**Whether the subject consented, or was told.** People photographed in
institutions, camps, tenements, work sites and schools were rarely in a
position to refuse. That does not make the image unusable; it changes what
it is evidence of.

**Whether it was posed.** Long exposures, group arrangements and repeat
shots from slightly different angles are all detectable, and a posed image
is not a fake — it is a statement about how people wished to be seen.

**The caption's author.** Captions are added, sometimes decades later, often
by a cataloguer. Never quote a caption as if the photographer wrote it.

**The file it lives in.** The collection and reference number tell you why
the image survived. Images survive in the records of the organisation that
paid for them, which is why the poor, the incarcerated and the colonised are
photographed far more often than they are read.

> [!example]- Two captions, one negative (click to expand)
> The same photograph of a family outside a house can be catalogued as
> evidence of a relief programme's success, as evidence of rural poverty, or
> as a portrait of a named family. The negative does not change; the
> argument attached to it does, and each version was made by somebody with a
> purpose. When you find a photograph reproduced in a textbook, go back to
> the repository record and compare the two captions. The difference between
> them is a small, publishable finding about
> [[Interpretation and Historiography]] — and it is exactly the kind of
> "tools, strategies, and approaches appropriate for historical inquiry" the
> curriculum has in mind.

## Using one in an argument

State what the image shows in flat language first — number of people, what
they are doing, what is behind them — before any adjective. Then say what it
can establish, and what it cannot. A photograph almost never establishes a
cause; it is very good on conditions, appearances, and the presence of
particular people in a particular place.

The National Archives has digitised large parts of its photographic
holdings, browsable by topic and date range, and invites the public to
transcribe records through its Citizen Archivist programme — the fastest way
to learn how a caption gets written is to write one. See
[[Where the Records Live]] for addresses, and
[[Reading a Primary Source]] for the annotation routine to run first.
