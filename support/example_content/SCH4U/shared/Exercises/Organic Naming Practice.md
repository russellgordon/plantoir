Organic names are not vocabulary. They are **instructions for drawing a
structure**, and a correct name is one that a stranger can follow to
exactly one molecule and no other. That is the whole design goal, and
every rule below exists to close off an ambiguity.

The procedure, in the order the rules are applied:

1. **Find the longest continuous carbon chain** containing the principal
   functional group. That chain names the compound; everything hanging
   off it is a substituent.
2. **Number the chain** so that the principal group — or, if there is
   none, the first substituent — gets the **lowest** possible locant.
3. **Name the substituents alphabetically**, each with its locant, with
   prefixes *di-*, *tri-*, *tetra-* for repeats. Those prefixes are
   ignored when alphabetising.
4. **Check that your name regenerates your structure.** Draw it back
   from the name you wrote. This step takes fifteen seconds and catches
   most errors.

Keep [[Functional Groups at a Glance]] open beside you.

**1.** Define *saturated hydrocarbon*, *unsaturated hydrocarbon*,
*structural isomer*, and *stereoisomer*. Then classify each of these as
saturated or unsaturated: ethane $\ce{C2H6}$, ethene
$\ce{C2H4}$, propane $\ce{C3H8}$, cyclohexane
$\ce{C6H12}$.

> [!success]- Answer 1
> **Saturated hydrocarbon**: a compound of carbon and hydrogen only, in
> which every carbon–carbon bond is a single bond. "Saturated" means
> carrying as many hydrogens as its skeleton can hold.
>
> **Unsaturated hydrocarbon**: one containing at least one carbon–carbon
> double or triple bond. Those bonds are sites where more atoms can be
> added, which is why unsaturated compounds react in ways saturated ones
> do not.
>
> **Structural isomer**: same molecular formula, different *connectivity*
> — the atoms are joined together in a different order.
>
> **Stereoisomer**: same molecular formula **and** the same
> connectivity, differing only in the arrangement of the atoms in
> space. The joining diagram is identical; the three-dimensional object
> is not.
>
> | Compound | Formula | Classification |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | Ethane | $\ce{C2H6}$ | Saturated |
> | Ethene | $\ce{C2H4}$ | Unsaturated — one C=C |
> | Propane | $\ce{C3H8}$ | Saturated |
> | Cyclohexane | $\ce{C6H12}$ | **Saturated** |
>
> **The last one is the trap and it is worth the whole question.** A
> quick test people learn is that an acyclic alkane fits
> $\ce{C_{n}H_{2n+2}}$, so a compound with fewer hydrogens than
> that must be unsaturated. Cyclohexane has 12 hydrogens where
> $2(6) + 2 = 14$ would be expected, so the test flags it — and the
> test is wrong here.
>
> Cyclohexane is short of two hydrogens because it is a **ring**, not
> because it has a double bond. Every carbon–carbon bond in it is
> single, so it is saturated. The formula test detects "something
> reduced the hydrogen count", and a ring and a double bond both do
> that. It cannot tell you which, and the difference is chemical: the
> double bond in ethene will add bromine across itself and the ring in
> cyclohexane will not.

**2.** Name $\ce{CH3CH2CH}(\ce{CH3})\ce{CH2CH3}$.
Then draw and name all five structural isomers of
$\ce{C6H14}$.

> [!success]- Answer 2
> The longest continuous chain is **five** carbons — walk along the
> written formula and count the carbons in the backbone, ignoring the
> $\ce{(CH3)}$ in brackets, which hangs off. Five is pentane. The
> methyl group sits on the middle carbon, and the middle carbon is C3
> whichever end you start from.
>
> **3-methylpentane**, molecular formula $\ce{C6H14}$.
>
> The five isomers of $\ce{C6H14}$:
>
> | Name | Longest chain | Substituents |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | Hexane | 6 | none |
> | 2-methylpentane | 5 | methyl at C2 |
> | 3-methylpentane | 5 | methyl at C3 |
> | 2,2-dimethylbutane | 4 | two methyls, both at C2 |
> | 2,3-dimethylbutane | 4 | methyl at C2, methyl at C3 |
>
> Two checks worth making every time you produce a list like this.
>
> **Every isomer must have the formula you started with.** Count
> 2,3-dimethylbutane: four carbons in the chain plus two in the methyls
> is six; the hydrogens come to fourteen.
> $\ce{C6H14}$, correct.
>
> **No two entries may be the same molecule under different names.** It
> is easy to "find" a sixth isomer by writing 4-methylpentane, but
> numbering that chain from the other end gives 2-methylpentane, which
> is already on the list. Whenever you think you have found an extra
> one, renumber it from both ends before you get attached to it.

**3.** Name $\ce{CH3CH2CH=CHCH3}$. Why is the
locant part of the name at all?

> [!success]- Answer 3
> Five carbons in the chain, one carbon–carbon double bond, so the stem
> is **pent-** and the ending is **-ene**.
>
> Number from both ends and take the lower locant for the double bond.
> From the left the double bond starts at carbon 3; from the right it
> starts at carbon 2. Two is lower.
>
> **Pent-2-ene**, molecular formula $\ce{C5H10}$.
>
> The locant is there because **"pentene" names three different
> substances.** Pent-1-ene and pent-2-ene have the same formula, are
> genuinely different compounds with different boiling points, and react
> differently — the position of the double bond decides where a reagent
> adds. A name without the number does not identify a substance, and
> identifying exactly one substance is the entire job of the naming
> system.
>
> The older style writes this as 2-pentene, and you will meet both.
> Modern IUPAC puts the locant next to the part of the name it refers
> to, which becomes much clearer once compounds carry two or three
> numbers each.

**4.** Draw structural formulas for **2,3-dimethylbutane** and
**3-ethyl-2-methylhexane**, and give the molecular formula of each.

> [!success]- Answer 4
> **2,3-dimethylbutane**: a four-carbon chain, with a methyl on C2 and a
> methyl on C3.
>
> $\ce{CH3CH}(\ce{CH3})\ce{CH}(\ce{CH3})\ce{CH3}$
>
> Carbons: 4 in the chain plus 2 in the methyls, so 6.
> Hydrogens: $3 + 1 + 3 + 1 + 3 + 3 = 14$.
> **$\ce{C6H14}$** — and it is on the list in question 2,
> as it should be.
>
> **3-ethyl-2-methylhexane**: a six-carbon chain, ethyl on C3, methyl on
> C2.
>
> $\ce{CH3CH}(\ce{CH3})\ce{CH}(\ce{CH2CH3})\ce{CH2CH2CH3}$
>
> Carbons: 6 in the chain, 2 in the ethyl, 1 in the methyl, so 9.
> Hydrogens: 20. **$\ce{C9H20}$**, which fits
> $\ce{C_{n}H_{2n+2}}$ for $n = 9$, so it is a saturated acyclic
> alkane and the count is self-consistent.
>
> **Two things this name is quietly telling you.** *Ethyl* comes before
> *methyl* because substituents are listed alphabetically, not in
> locant order — which is why the numbers in the name run 3 then 2 and
> that is not a mistake. And the chain was numbered from the end that
> gave the substituents $\{2, 3\}$ rather than $\{4, 5\}$, because the
> rule compares the whole set of locants and takes the lower one at the
> first point of difference.

**5.** Name each of these, and say which class of compound it belongs
to.
*(a)* $\ce{CH3CH2CH2OH}$
*(b)* $\ce{CH3CH}(\ce{OH})\ce{CH3}$
*(c)* $\ce{CH3CH2CHO}$
*(d)* $\ce{CH3COCH3}$
*(e)* $\ce{CH3CH2COOH}$
*(f)* $\ce{CH3CH2OCH2CH3}$
*(g)* $\ce{CH3CH2NH2}$
*(h)* $\ce{CH3CONH2}$

> [!success]- Answer 5
> | | Structure | Name | Class |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | (a) | $\ce{CH3CH2CH2OH}$ | Propan-1-ol | Alcohol |
> | (b) | $\ce{CH3CH}(\ce{OH})\ce{CH3}$ | Propan-2-ol | Alcohol |
> | (c) | $\ce{CH3CH2CHO}$ | Propanal | Aldehyde |
> | (d) | $\ce{CH3COCH3}$ | Propanone | Ketone |
> | (e) | $\ce{CH3CH2COOH}$ | Propanoic acid | Carboxylic acid |
> | (f) | $\ce{CH3CH2OCH2CH3}$ | Ethoxyethane | Ether |
> | (g) | $\ce{CH3CH2NH2}$ | Ethanamine | Amine |
> | (h) | $\ce{CH3CONH2}$ | Ethanamide | Amide |
>
> **(a) and (b) need their locants** because propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol
> are different substances with different boiling points and different
> chemistry. **(c) and (d) do not**, and the reason is worth
> understanding rather than memorising: an aldehyde's carbonyl can only
> ever be at the end of a chain, so "propanal" has nowhere else to put
> it; a ketone's carbonyl can never be at the end, so in a three-carbon
> chain there is only one place left. With four carbons the ketone
> starts needing a number again.
>
> **(c) and (d) are structural isomers** — both are
> $\ce{C3H6O}$, joined up differently, and they behave
> completely differently. That pair is exactly the one
> [[Identifying an Unknown Organic Compound]] asks you to separate at
> the bench.
>
> **(f) is named as an ether** by treating the smaller alkyl group and
> its oxygen as a substituent — *ethoxy* — on the larger chain. When
> both sides are the same, as here, you will also see the older name
> "diethyl ether", and both are understood.

**6.** How many structural isomers have the molecular formula
$\ce{C4H10O}$? Name them, and sort them by class.

> [!success]- Answer 6
> **Seven** — four alcohols and three ethers.
>
> The alcohols, from the two carbon skeletons available to four carbons:
>
> | Skeleton | Position of $-\ce{OH}$ | Name |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | Straight chain | C1 | Butan-1-ol |
> | Straight chain | C2 | Butan-2-ol |
> | Branched | End carbon | 2-methylpropan-1-ol |
> | Branched | Central carbon | 2-methylpropan-2-ol |
>
> The ethers, by splitting four carbons across an oxygen:
>
> | Split | Name |
> | --- | --- |
> | 1 and 3, straight | 1-methoxypropane |
> | 1 and 3, branched | 2-methoxypropane |
> | 2 and 2 | Ethoxyethane |
>
> **How to be sure you have them all.** Work systematically rather than
> by inspiration: first list the carbon skeletons — for four carbons
> there are two, straight and branched — then place the oxygen in every
> distinct position on each. Distinct is the operative word: putting the
> $-\ce{OH}$ on C3 of butane gives butan-2-ol renumbered, not a new
> compound.
>
> **The chemistry across that list is not uniform**, which is the reason
> the question is worth doing. The four alcohols all hydrogen bond and
> are all reasonably soluble in water. The three ethers have no O–H, so
> they cannot donate a hydrogen bond to each other at all, and they boil
> far lower and dissolve far less. **Same formula, two very different
> substances-worth of behaviour**, and only the connectivity separates
> them.

**7.** Name the ester formed from butan-1-ol and ethanoic acid. Then
work out which alcohol and which acid would give **ethyl butanoate**,
and compare the two molecular formulas.

> [!success]- Answer 7
> An ester's name is built in two halves and they come from opposite
> reactants:
>
> - **The first word is the alkyl group from the alcohol.**
> - **The second word is the acid's stem with the ending -oate.**
>
> **Butan-1-ol plus ethanoic acid gives butyl ethanoate.** Butan-1-ol
> supplies a four-carbon *butyl* group; ethanoic acid supplies the
> two-carbon *ethanoate* part.
>
> $\ce{CH3COOH + C4H9OH <=> CH3COOC4H9 + H2O}$
>
> **Ethyl butanoate** is the other way round: *ethyl* comes from
> **ethanol**, and *butanoate* comes from **butanoic acid**.
>
> Both esters are $\ce{C6H12O2}$. They contain the
> same atoms in the same numbers, they are made from different starting
> materials, and they are **different substances** — which you can
> confirm without any equipment at all, because they do not smell the
> same.
>
> The reaction arrow is a double one — `<=>` — on purpose.
> Esterification does not run to completion; it settles at an
> equilibrium with water and the starting materials still present, which
> is why the industrial version removes the water as it forms. That
> reasoning is Unit 4's, applied to Unit 2's chemistry, and the two
> units are more connected than the table of contents suggests.

**8.** Four names from a study group. Each one is wrong. Say why, and
give the correct name.
*(a) 3-methylbutane*
*(b) 1-methylpropane*
*(c) pent-4-ene*
*(d) 2-ethylpropane*

> [!success]- Answer 8
> **(a) 3-methylbutane — the numbering runs the wrong way.** A
> four-carbon chain with a methyl on it can be numbered from either end.
> One direction puts the methyl at C3, the other puts it at C2, and the
> rule takes the lower locant.
>
> Correct: **2-methylbutane**. Note that the student's structure was
> right — only the label was wrong, and the same molecule was going to
> appear twice on their isomer list as a result. That is the practical
> cost of skipping the numbering rule: not a lost mark on one name, but
> a miscount everywhere the name is used.
>
> **(b) 1-methylpropane — a substituent at the end of a chain is not a
> substituent.** Draw it:
> $\ce{CH3-CH2-CH2}$ with a methyl on the first
> carbon. Now count the longest continuous chain in what you have drawn.
> It is four carbons, in a straight line.
>
> Correct: **butane**. The error is at rule 1 rather than rule 2. There
> is no "1-methyl" or "3-methylpropane" anywhere in organic chemistry,
> because a methyl on a terminal carbon is always just a longer chain,
> and if a name of that shape ever appears in your work it means you did
> not find the longest chain.
>
> **(c) pent-4-ene — same numbering failure as (a).** Numbering from the
> other end puts the double bond at C1.
>
> Correct: **pent-1-ene**. A useful check for alkenes: in a chain of $n$
> carbons the double bond's locant can never exceed $n - 1$, and it
> should never exceed roughly half of $n$, because past the middle you
> would have numbered from the other end.
>
> **(d) 2-ethylpropane — the longest chain was missed.** Draw it: a
> three-carbon chain with a two-carbon group on the middle carbon. Now
> trace the longest continuous path through that skeleton. Start at one
> end of the ethyl group, go through the middle carbon, and out along
> the propane chain: that is **four** carbons, not three.
>
> Correct: **2-methylbutane**, $\ce{C5H12}$ — which is the
> same compound as the corrected version of (a), by a different wrong
> route.
>
> **The pattern in all four.** Every error is a rule applied out of
> order or not at all, and every one of them is caught by step 4 of the
> procedure at the top of this page: **draw the structure back from your
> name and see whether you get what you started with.** Do that on
> "2-ethylpropane" and you get a molecule whose longest chain is four,
> which contradicts the "propane" in the name you just wrote. The check
> is fifteen seconds and it finds all of these.
