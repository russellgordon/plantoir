A technician holds keys to other people's machines and other people's
information. The rules around that are not paperwork; they are the
reason anybody lets you near the equipment.

## Software licences, and what they permit

Buying software rarely means owning it. It means holding a licence to
use it in stated ways.

| Licence | What you may do | What catches people out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Proprietary, per-device** | Install on the stated number of machines | Re-imaging twenty machines from one licensed install is twenty violations |
| **Proprietary, per-user or subscription** | One named person, often on several devices | Sharing an account is usually a breach even when it is technically possible |
| **Volume or education licences** | Wide installation under conditions | The conditions are real: education licences often forbid commercial use |
| **Free and open source** (GPL, MIT, Apache) | Use, modify, and redistribute | The GPL requires you to pass on the source of derived work — free does not mean unconditional |
| **Freeware / shareware** | Use at no cost, sometimes limited | "Free for personal use" is not free for the shop |

Copyright applies automatically to software, documents, images, and
music — the Copyright Act protects them whether or not a notice is
printed. Patents cover inventions and can cover techniques. Installing
unlicensed software on a client's machine exposes the client, not just
you, which is why the licence record belongs in the service
documentation.

## Acceptable-use policies

Every organisation that lets people use its computers writes one. A
usable policy states:

- **Who it applies to** and on what equipment, including personal
  devices on the network.
- **What the systems are for**, and what personal use is tolerated.
- **What is prohibited** — inappropriate content, harassment,
  circumventing security, installing unlicensed software.
- **What is monitored**, and by whom. A policy that permits monitoring
  without saying so is a policy nobody can consent to.
- **How data is protected**, including personal information about
  others.
- **What happens when it is breached**, in proportion.

Your school has one, and you agreed to it. Read it once as a
technician rather than as a student — you will be writing the
equivalent for a client in [[The Client Build]].

## Privacy, which is somebody else's

Working on a machine gives you access to files you have no business
reading. The professional standard is simple and absolute: you open
what the job requires and nothing else, you do not copy anything, and
you do not discuss what you saw. If a repair uncovers something
illegal, you stop and escalate — you do not investigate.

Ontario's privacy legislation, and the federal law covering commercial
activity, both rest on the same principle: personal information is
collected for a stated purpose, kept only as long as needed, protected
appropriately, and not used for anything else.

> A customer's machine is in for a drive replacement. Cloning the drive
> gives you a complete copy of their data on your bench. What do you do
> with that copy when the job is finished, and when exactly does it
> stop being yours to hold? Bring your answer to
