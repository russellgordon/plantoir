An argument with evidence, arranged so a reader can follow it. At this
level the five-paragraph scaffold comes down.

## What it needs

1. **A thesis with a mechanism**, per [[Thesis and Argument]].
2. **Body paragraphs that each prove one move of the argument** — not one
   "point" each, one *move*.
3. **Evidence quoted short and analysed long.**
4. **The strongest counter-reading, engaged**, not mentioned and
   dismissed.
5. **A conclusion that says what follows** from having been right.

## Paragraph shape

Claim → context → evidence → analysis (two to four sentences) → link. The
analysis is where the marks are, and it is the part that gets squeezed
when a student is short of time. Plan so that it is not.

## Introductions that work at this level

No dictionary definitions and no "since the dawn of time". Open with the
specific observation that made you write the essay:

> Banquo hears the same prophecy and does nothing. Every account of
> *Macbeth* that treats the witches as the cause has to explain him
> away, and the play makes that impossible.

## Common structural failures

| Failure | Symptom | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Organised by the text | Paragraphs follow act order rather than argument order | Reorder by move; the text will still be there |
| Evidence stacking | Three quotations in a paragraph, one sentence of analysis | Cut two quotations |
| The undefended pivot | "However, some may argue…" then nothing | Engage it for a full paragraph or delete the sentence |
| The summary conclusion | Repeats the introduction in different words | Ask "so what follows?" and write that |

## Before you submit

- [ ] Read it aloud, whole.
- [ ] Check each quotation against the text.
- [ ] Cover the body and reread the thesis: does the essay prove the
      claim you made?
- [ ] Check citations against [[Citing Sources]].
