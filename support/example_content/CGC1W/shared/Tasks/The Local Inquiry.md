**The culminating task. Four class periods at the end of Unit 4, ending
with a presentation to a real audience.** Groups of three or four — with
one concept written and marked on your own.

One geographic question about the place you actually live, investigated
properly and answered in public.

## The four days

| Day | What it is for |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Scope: the question, the evidence you will need, the audience |
| 2 | Evidence: a site visit — the shoreline, the woodlot, the farm, or a street — plus data |
| 3 | Analysis: the concepts of geographic thinking applied to what you found |
| 4 | The presentation, to your audience, with questions taken |

## The audience is real

A municipal planner, a conservation authority staff member, a councillor,
a farmer, a community association, or the school's own administration.
Your teacher arranges the invitation; you write to them yourself, and you
send them your question in advance.

## What you deliver

- **The inquiry question**, and why it matters here.
- **Evidence you gathered yourself** — field observation, a survey, an
  interview, or your own mapping — alongside published data.
- **An analysis** that uses the four concepts explicitly: what is
  significant about where this is, what patterns and trends you found,
  what is connected to what, and whose perspective changes the answer.
  **One concept each**, written alone and signed, on day 3. In a four
  that is one apiece; in a three, one of you takes two and says which.
  You speak to your own concept on day 4.
- **One practice from somewhere else.** A planning or design practice
  used in another community — in Canada or elsewhere — that grew out of
  how the people there actually live, and an honest judgement about
  whether it would transfer here. [[Designing With Culture in Mind]] is
  where to start; the examples compared in class before this task
  launched are fair game.
- **A conclusion** with a recommendation, its cost, and what you would
  need to be more certain.
- **A one-page leave-behind** your audience keeps.

## How it is marked

| Criterion | What earns the marks |
| --- | --- |
| The question | Specific, local, answerable, and worth asking |
| Method | Evidence gathered systematically, with its limits admitted |
| Geographic thinking | The four concepts used to reason, not named as decoration |
| The presentation | Twelve minutes a busy adult would find worth their time |
| Learning from elsewhere | One real practice named, with where it comes from and what about that place made it fit — and a judgement about whether it survives the move here |
| The exchange | The letter told your audience what they were coming to; on the day, their questions get answers about your evidence rather than apologies for it |
| Your own concept | The section you wrote and signed, and what you said about it at the day 3 checkpoint |

**No part of this is a group mark.** The rows above are judged for each
of you — from the concept section you wrote, from what you can account
for at the day 3 checkpoint, and from what you did in the room. Four
people on one inquiry can finish with four different marks, and usually
do.

> [!tip] The best questions come from something you noticed
> A drain that always floods. A field that became houses. A shoreline
> path that ends abruptly. Start from something real you have seen, and
> the research will have somewhere to go.

[[The Concepts of Geographic Thinking]] is the analytical spine, and
[[Fieldwork Safety and Ethics]] governs the site visit — including how
you behave on land that belongs to somebody.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

This one is part of the final thirty per cent and it is done in groups,
so the individual evidence is not optional. Day 4 of 4 — Unit 4, Day 21
— is not the place to gather it: you are hosting guests and you will
hear almost nothing.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 19, the evidence day, on site or in the lab
  Watch whether the conditions of the evidence get written down as it is
  gathered — where they stood, when, for how long, what the weather was,
  how many they counted and over what period. On Day 20 the analysis
  will state its limits, because the criteria ask for it; a group can
  write those limits from memory and it reads the same. Only today shows
  you whether the limits were recorded or reconstructed. Treat this as
  corroboration of the method claim, which is worth more than it sounds:
  it is the difference between a figure somebody could repeat and one
  nobody can.
  The agenda says "recording sources as you go", which is about
  citations; this is about the conditions of the evidence they generate
  themselves, which nobody has told them to write down today.
  Going well: the four headers on the page before the first count —
  date, time, location, weather — the same discipline as the first week.
  Stuck: a photograph, a number in a group chat, and nothing else.
  Record: group name and a tick. You are off-site; keep it to that.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 20, at the checkpoint conference already on that
agenda
  Sit with a group for eight minutes and hear each member on their own
  concept, two minutes apiece, while the rest of the class works on the
  analysis. Seven groups fits the period; twenty-five separate
  conferences does not.
  Ask each student about the concept they took: "Which of the other
  three does your part lean on hardest, and where does the join show?"
  A strong answer points at a place in the analysis where two concepts
  had to be used together, and can say which came first. That is A1.5's
  synthesising half — the concepts reasoned WITH rather than named as
  four headings, which is the failure this task attracts — and a group
  that divided the work and never spoke cannot answer it at all.
  Then: "If you asked this same question about the whole municipality
  instead of this block, which of your findings would survive?"
  A strong answer separates a finding that is scale-dependent from one
  that is not, and says why — the strongest evidence you will get all
  year that a student understands what scale does to an answer.
  Record: one line per STUDENT. The mark on this task is individual and
  this conference is where you can defend it.

The product evidence is the analysis, the leave-behind and the
presentation on Day 21.
