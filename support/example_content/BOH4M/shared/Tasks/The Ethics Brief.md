**Due: the last day of Unit 1.** Individual. About 1,000 words, in memo
form.

One company. One ethical issue it actually faced. Your recommendation to
its management.

## The brief

You are writing to a decision-maker who has ten minutes. Use the memo
shape from [[Writing for Business]]: recommendation first, then the
reasoning, then what it costs, then what happens next.

## What it must contain

1. **The issue, factually.** What happened, when, who was involved. No
   adjectives you cannot support.
2. **A stakeholder analysis.** Everyone affected, what each wanted, and
   where those wants collided.
3. **The company's own record.** What they said, what they did, and what
   it cost them — see the evidence test on
   [[The Responsible Company]].
4. **The dilemma, stated honestly.** If there is an obvious right answer,
   you have chosen an easy issue.
5. **Your recommendation**, and the cost you are accepting by making it.

## How it is marked

| Criterion | What earns the marks |
| --- | --- |
| Reasoning | You weigh competing obligations rather than declaring a side |
| Evidence | Sourced, dated, and distinguishing claims from facts |
| Writing | The memo conventions, used properly; business vocabulary, used precisely |
| Judgement | The recommendation is one a manager could act on this month |

> [!tip] Name the cost
> The single most common way to lose marks here is a recommendation with
> no downside. Every real ethical choice costs somebody something — money,
> speed, a relationship, a job. Say what, and say who pays.

Sources are cited the way [[Citing Business Sources]] sets out.

  for.
  Going well: a document open that the company did not publish, being

  Record: a tick, a dash or a question mark beside each name on the day

TALK — Unit 1, Day 17, the conferences already on that agenda
  Do not ask what the recommendation costs. Both the agenda and the task
  page print that, and they will arrive with the answer.
  Ask: "Who inside the company would have argued against you at the time,
  and what was their strongest point?" A strong answer gives an argument
  a reasonable person could hold — pay the fine, it is cheaper; the plant
  is the town's only employer — rather than a straw man. That is A3.1

  strategies and decision making), and the memo will not show it, because
  the memo only contains the objections the student decided to answer.

  company says about itself would it have had to stop saying?" A strong
  answer quotes something from the company's own website or report and
  explains the contradiction. That is A3.2 again, heard rather than
  cited.
  Record: one line per student, in the margin of the day plan, right
  then.
