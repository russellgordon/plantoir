The four moves are on [[Research Writing]]: support, extend, complicate,
disagree.

**1.** A critic writes: *"Monkey Beach refuses the conventions of the
coming-of-age novel."* Use it to **extend** rather than to agree.

> [!success]- Answer 1
> *The refusal is real, but it is selective: the novel keeps the form's
> first movement — a child learning the rules of her world — and
> discards only its resolution. What Robinson refuses is not the coming
> of age but the arrival.*

**2.** Use the same claim to **complicate** your own argument.

> [!success]- Answer 2
> *This complicates my reading: if the novel keeps the form's opening
> and abandons its ending, then calling the structure "grief-shaped" is
> too simple — the shape is inherited before it is broken.*

**3.** A source you found is a student essay on a revision site. What do
you do with it?

> [!success]- Answer 3
> Not cite it. Use it, if at all, to find the primary evidence it points
> at, then read that yourself. An unrefereed summary is not a secondary
> source; it is somebody else's homework.

**4.** Rewrite to remove the source stacking: *Critic A argues X. Critic
B agrees. Critic C also notes X. This shows X.*

> [!success]- Answer 4
> *Critic A's account of the novel's orality has become the standard
> reading, and it explains the interview passages well. It explains the
> dream sequences less well, and those are where the novel's method is
> most visible.*
> One source, used for something, with your argument driving. In your
> own writing "Critic A" is a name and a page number — inventing one
> that sounds plausible is the exact habit this page is against.

**5.** You cannot remember whether a sentence in your notes is yours or a
critic's. What now?

> [!success]- Answer 5
> Do not use it. Close the notes, write the idea from memory in your own
> words, and if it turns out to be the critic's, cite them. The habit of
> drafting with sources closed prevents this entirely — see
> [[Research Writing]].
