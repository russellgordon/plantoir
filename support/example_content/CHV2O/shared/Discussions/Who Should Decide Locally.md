**The question:** when a decision affects one neighbourhood, one town, or
one nation's territory, who should make it — the people there, or a
larger government answerable to everyone?

The Canadian answer is complicated on purpose. Municipalities exist
because a province created them and can change what they may do.
Provinces hold powers Ottawa cannot take. Indigenous governments hold
authority that comes from neither.[^s35] Every arrangement encodes a
different answer to the same question.

[^s35]: Section 35 of the *Constitution Act, 1982* recognises and affirms
existing Aboriginal and treaty rights. Those rights are not delegated by
a province or by Parliament, which is why an Indigenous government is not
a fourth kind of municipality — see [[Indigenous Governance]].

## Prepare

Bring one decision made locally that you think should have been made
higher up, and one made higher up that you think should have been local.
Read [[Who Decides What, and Where]] first.

## Positions that genuinely conflict

**The people affected should decide.** They know the street, the school,
the river. Distant governments make decisions about places they have
never visited, and the record of that is not good.

**Local decisions export their costs.** A municipality that refuses
housing does not make the need disappear; it moves it. A town that
permits a discharge upstream affects a town it does not represent. When
harms cross a boundary, so should the decision.

**Local majorities can be the problem.** Minority rights have often been
protected by higher governments and courts precisely because a local
majority would not protect them. "Let the community decide" has a
specific history in Canada, and it is worth knowing before using the
phrase.

**Consultation is not the same as authority.** Being asked and being able
to decide are different, and a great many "local input" processes are the
first without the second. Whether that counts as local decision-making is
part of the argument.

**For Indigenous governments the question is different in kind.**
Jurisdiction here is not devolved by a province, and treating it as a
scale question — small government, big government — misdescribes the
relationship. Get this one right before you generalise: it is not
one more point on the same spectrum.

## Push it further

Take one live question — where housing goes, where a landfill goes, what
a school teaches, whether a road is tolled — and say which level should
decide it and why. Then take the strongest case against your own answer,
and say what would make you change it.

Bring your position into [[The Issue Brief]] if it holds up.
