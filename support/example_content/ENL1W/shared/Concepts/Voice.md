Voice is the thing that makes two people describing the same afternoon
sound nothing alike. It is not slang, and it is not a personality you put
on: it is the accumulation of choices a writer keeps making.

## What voice is made of

- **Sentence length, and the mix of it.** All long sentences sound
  academic. All short ones sound breathless. The mix is where a voice
  lives.
- **Word choice, especially the plain ones.** *Grabbed*, *took*,
  *snatched* — same event, three different speakers.
- **What gets noticed.** A narrator who describes shoes and never faces
  has told you who they are.
- **Distance.** How close the writing sits to the reader: *one might
  argue* versus *look*.
- **Rhythm.** Read it aloud; a voice you can hear is a voice you have.

## In your own writing

Voice is not something to add at the end. It comes from making decisions
instead of taking defaults:

| Default | A decision |
| --- | --- |
| "In today's society, many people believe…" | "Most of us believe…" |
| "It was very cold" | "It was cold enough that the door stuck" |
| "The author uses imagery to convey…" | "Joe writes the classroom as a place where a language goes missing" |

## Register: the other half

Voice is yours; **register** is the level of formality a situation asks
for. Both are real skills, and knowing when to switch is the point. The
voice in a group chat, a job application, and a personal essay should be
recognisably the same person and unmistakably different registers.

That is exactly what [[B1.5|adapting word choice for an audience]] and
[[D2.3|establishing an identifiable voice]] ask you to be able to do, and
it is worth noticing that nobody thinks a lawyer talking to their friends
is being unprofessional.

> [!tip] The read-aloud test
> Read your paragraph to somebody. If they say "that sounds like you",
> you have a voice. If they say "that sounds like an essay", you have a
> costume.
