Until 1982, changing Canada's constitution meant asking the British
Parliament to do it. Patriation ended that. On 17 April 1982 the
*Constitution Act, 1982* was proclaimed, bringing the constitution home with
a domestic amending formula, a charter of rights, and section 35's
recognition of existing Aboriginal and treaty rights. It is the biggest
constitutional change since Confederation, and Quebec did not agree to it.

## How the deal was reached, and who was not in the room

After a long federal-provincial fight, the compromise came together at a
late-night meeting in November 1981 from which Quebec's delegation was
absent. Quebec's National Assembly refused to endorse the result. Legally the
amendment bound Quebec anyway; politically it left a wound that shaped the
next twenty years. In English Canada the meeting is called the Kitchen
Accord; in Quebec, *la nuit des longs couteaux*. When two names for one event
carry opposite judgements, that is a fact about the event. Meanwhile
Indigenous organizations lobbied hard, in Canada and in London, and section
35 exists because of that campaign; women's organizations lobbied to
strengthen section 28. Neither group was handed anything.

> [!success]- Three features of the Charter to know precisely (click to open)
> **Section 15**, the equality rights section, did **not** come into force
> with the rest of the Charter. It took effect on **17 April 1985**, three
> years later, and the delay was deliberate: governments were given time to
> find and amend laws that would fail it. **Section 33**, the notwithstanding
> clause, lets Parliament or a legislature declare that a law operates
> despite certain Charter rights; the declaration expires after five years
> unless renewed. It was the price of provincial agreement in 1981, and
> provinces have invoked it more often in the past decade than in the
> Charter's first thirty years. **Section 28** guarantees Charter rights
> equally to male and female persons.

## The two attempts to bring Quebec in

The Meech Lake Accord of 1987 would have recognised Quebec as a distinct
society and made other changes to federal powers. It required unanimous
provincial approval within three years and did not get it. In Manitoba,
Elijah Harper — a Cree member of the legislature — withheld the consent
needed to move it forward, on the ground that a constitutional round which
had again ignored Indigenous peoples should not pass. It died in 1990. The
Charlottetown Accord of 1992 was broader, included self-government, and was
put to a national referendum. Canadians voted it down, opposed from opposite
directions at once — as too much for Quebec, and as not enough.

## 1995, and the rules afterwards

The second Quebec referendum, in 1995, was decided by **50.58 per cent to
49.42 per cent**. Do not round that. A country came within roughly one voter
in a hundred of breaking apart, and the bitter aftermath — over the
question's wording, over campaign spending, over a premier's concession
speech — belongs to the event. Ottawa then asked the Supreme Court whether
Quebec could secede unilaterally. The Court said no, and also said a clear
majority on a clear question would create a duty on the rest of Canada to
negotiate. Parliament wrote that into the *Clarity Act*; Quebec's National
Assembly passed its own law rejecting it. Neither has been tested.
