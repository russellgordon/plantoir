Advertising is the most persuasive writing most people read every day, and
almost nobody stops to notice how it works. Today you will, in a small
group of three or four.

## Before the activity

Each group brings one real, current advertisement to class — a magazine
spread, a poster from a bus shelter, a video ad someone screenshots or
links to, a page from a retailer's website. It has to be something you
actually found this week, not a famous example everyone already knows by
heart. A fragrance ad, a car ad, a fast-food ad, a back-to-school clothing
ad — the category does not matter. What matters is that it is real,
current, and yours.

If your group cannot find one, borrow a print flyer from the school office
or a magazine from the library — do not fall back on describing an ad from
memory. Half of what you are analysing is *how it looks*, and memory does
not preserve layout, colour, or font.

## Techniques to look for

Work through the table below with your actual ad in front of you. You will
not find every technique in every ad — note which ones are there and skip
the rest.

| Technique | What it looks like | Ask your group |
|---|---|---|
| Aspirational imagery | The people, places, or lifestyle shown are better-looking, richer, or happier than an ordinary day | Who is this ad telling us we could become? |
| Bandwagon | Language or images suggesting "everyone" already uses this | What crowd is the ad putting you inside, or leaving you outside of? |
| Emotional appeal | Music, colour, or a story designed to make you feel something before you think anything | What feeling hits first — and does it match the product? |
| Authority or expertise | A uniform, a lab coat, statistics, an expert's endorsement | Is the authority actually relevant to this product, or just borrowed? |
| Scarcity or urgency | "Limited time," "while supplies last," a countdown | What happens to your judgement when a deadline is added? |
| Vague comparison | "More," "better," "new and improved" — with no stated comparison point | More than what, exactly? The ad will not say. |

## Running the activity

1. As a group, identify every technique from the table that appears in
   your ad. Mark where, specifically — a phrase, an image, a camera angle.
2. Choose the single technique doing the most work in this ad — the one
   that would make the ad noticeably weaker if it were removed. Agree on
   this as a group before moving on.
3. Draft two or three sentences naming that technique, describing exactly
   where it appears, and explaining who the ad is trying to persuade and
   what it wants them to feel or do.
4. Be ready to hold up your ad and read your sentences to another group.
   Listening groups should be able to point to the same spot in the ad you
   are describing.
