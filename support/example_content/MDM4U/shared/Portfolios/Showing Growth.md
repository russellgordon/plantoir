By mid-course, your [[Math Journal]] stops being only a diary and
becomes something rarer: **evidence**. The person who wrote your
week-one entries no longer quite exists — and because that person
wrote honestly, you can prove it. Showing growth means putting your
week-one self and your now-self side by side and letting the
difference speak. It is the same move this course asks of every
claim: do not assert the change, *show the data*.

## Growth is a claim plus evidence

"I got better at math" is a claim, worth what any claim is worth:
nothing yet. Growth writing pairs every claim with evidence from your
own record — and the strongest evidence is the gap between two
entries:

> In the first week I wrote that probability was "a fraction you
> guess and then defend". Last week, working on my investigation, I
> caught myself refusing to report a difference between two groups
> because one of them had eleven people in it — I could not tell the
> difference from noise, and I said so in the write-up instead of
> hiding it. I would not have noticed the group size at the start of the course,
> and I definitely would not have admitted it.

The claim is *uncertainty became something I handle rather than
avoid*. The evidence is two moments, months apart, both on the record
— and nobody can argue with that, including the part of you that
suspects you have not changed. It is the standard [[How Marks Work]]
applies everywhere: what can you point to?

## How to cite yourself

Treat your earlier entries the way a report treats sources — which is
exactly what [[Citing Your Sources]] asks of the data in your
investigation, applied to the one source that is you:

- **Date it** — "In my second-week entry…" — the distance matters.
- **Quote a phrase, not a page.** Your words from the time carry more
  truth than your summary of them now. "I said 180 and the answer was
  23", from [[Your First Entry]], says more than "I used to have poor
  intuition."
- **Then show the now.** A recent moment, just as specific, that the
  earlier self could not have managed — or would not have noticed.

> [!tip] Growth is not always getting more answers right
> Some growth is confidence traded for care. Some is a new noticing:
> a bar chart whose axis starts at 96 and you *seeing* it before you
> read the numbers, a survey question whose wording you rewrote
> before collecting a single response. Some of it is negative
> results — a dataset you rejected for a reason you can name is
> genuine progress, even though it produced nothing to hand in. New
> noticing is growth, and your early entries prove it was not always
> there.
