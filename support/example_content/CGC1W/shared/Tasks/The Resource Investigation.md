**Launched mid-Unit 2, due at the end of it.** Pairs — with the answer
written and marked on your own.

One Canadian resource, followed from where it sits in the ground, the
water, or the forest to the argument about whether we should be taking
it.

## The inquiry

You write the question yourself, and that is the first thing marked. A
good one is answerable, specific, and genuinely open — see
[[The Geographic Inquiry Process]].

| Weak question | Stronger version |
| --- | --- |
| Is mining bad? | Should the proposed mine at X proceed, and on whose terms? |
| Why is water important? | Who decides how the water in this watershed is allocated, and how well does that work? |

## What you produce

- **The resource**, classified — renewable, non-renewable, or flow —
  and where it is found, with a map showing why it is there.
- **How it is taken and moved**: extraction or harvesting method, the
  route to market, and what the method costs environmentally.
- **The argument**, from at least three perspectives that genuinely
  disagree — including, where the resource sits on treaty land or
  traditional territory, the position of the First Nation, Métis, or
  Inuit communities involved, in their own published words.
- **Your answer** to your question, with the strongest objection to it
  answered — **written alone, signed, in the working period after the
  sustainability seminar.** Your partner writes their own. The two need
  not agree, and it is more interesting when they do not.

## How it is marked

- **The question** — worth answering, and answered.
- **Perspectives** — genuinely different positions, fairly represented,
  each sourced. Not three versions of your own view.
- **Geography** — the physical facts of where the resource is do real
  work in your argument.
- **Care** — Indigenous rights and claims are described accurately, from
  sources connected to those communities.
- **Your own answer** — signed, and the piece your mark rests on most.
  Nothing here is a common pair mark. The question, the map and the
  perspectives are built together, and every row above is judged for
  each of you from what you wrote, what I watched you do, and what you
  can account for when I ask. Two people on one investigation can
  finish with two different marks.

> [!warning] Three perspectives is a minimum, not a formula
> Industry, government, and "environmentalists" is the lazy set. Workers,
> neighbouring communities, downstream water users, and the people who
> would lose a job if it stopped are all real positions.

See [[Land, Treaty, and Title]] before you write the third bullet, and
[[Arguing About Sustainability]] before you write the fourth.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

Pairs, and the answer is marked individually, so the conversation is
also where the individual evidence comes from. Ask each partner alone.

OBSERVE — Unit 2, Day 13, the working period, while the resource maps
are being built
  Watch the moment two map layers get put on top of each other — a
  deposit or a licence area over a treaty area, a claim under
  negotiation, or a nation's own published territory map. Do they check
  the source, the date and the scale of each layer, and say what the
  line actually represents, before they conclude anything about whose
  land it is on? An overlay built at two different zooms produces a
  confident false statement, and the finished map gives you no way to
  tell.
  The rule is already on the Using a Web GIS page — one layer, one
  source, one date — so this is not a trick question. It is whether a
  rule they have read gets used when nobody reminds them, which is a
  different thing and is only visible here.
  Going well: someone reads the source and year of a layer out loud, or
  says "this boundary is the claim, not the reserve".
  Stuck: two screenshots stacked, with the conclusion already typed.
  Record: pair names, one column, and which layer they questioned.

TALK — Unit 2, Day 15, in the working period after the sustainability
seminar, while they write their own answers
  Ask: "What was your question before this one, and what made you
  change it?"
  A strong answer names what the earlier question could not be answered
  with — evidence nobody publishes, an answer already settled, a scale
  too big to check. That is A1.1: the page marks the question they
  ended with, and this is the only place the choosing of it is visible.
  Then: "Move this deposit forty kilometres further from the nearest
  road. Is it taken out the same way?"
  A strong answer changes the METHOD, not just the cost — a different
  machine, a winter road, a camp, or nobody bothering at all. That is
  C1.3's interrelationship between accessibility and the mode of
  extraction, which the page's Geography criterion asks them to write
  about but never asks them to vary. On a pair task it is also how you
  find out which half of the argument each student actually owns.
  Record: one line per STUDENT, not per pair.

The product evidence is the investigation handed in on Day 16.
