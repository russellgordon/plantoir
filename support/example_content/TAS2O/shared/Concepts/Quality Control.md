You have been doing quality control since the first week — you just
called it a proof. Every name checked against the recording, every page
read by somebody who did not write it, every published page loaded on
a second device: those are quality checks, and a newsroom runs on them.
The shop runs on the same idea, with a ruler where the newsroom has a
red pen.

## A standard, a check, a record

Quality control has three parts wherever it happens. First a
**performance standard** — what "good enough" means, written down
before the work, preferably as something that can be measured. Then a
**check** against that standard, done the same way every time. Then a
**record** of what the check found, so a failure teaches the next
attempt rather than just the person standing there.

| | In the newsroom | In the shop |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The standard** | House style: names checked twice, quotes exact, every photo captioned | A dimension with a tolerance — 6.5 mm, give or take 0.2; a sign readable at 5 m |
| **The check** | The proof, the fact-check, the second read | Measuring the first part off the machine; testing it with the person who will use it |
| **Pass or fail** | A story either meets the standard or goes back | A part either fits the gauge or does not |
| **The record** | The corrections log | The settings sheet, the test log |

Industry calls checking the very first part of a run **first-article
inspection**: before a factory makes a thousand of anything, it
measures one, completely, against the drawing. A **go / no-go gauge**
is the fast version — a test piece the part must fit (go) and must
not fit (no-go), so a check takes seconds and needs no arithmetic. Our
class tolerance coupon is a go / no-go gauge you made yourselves.

## Qualitative and quantitative

Some standards are numbers: a clamp that holds a 71.5 mm phone, a print
that finishes in under thirty minutes and uses less than fifteen grams.
Some are judgements: a sign whose user can find the room without
asking, a rig a crew actually reaches for on a shoot. Good criteria
include both, and say how each will be checked — a number without a
measuring method is a wish, and a judgement without a named judge is
an opinion. Write them before you make the thing, so they cannot be
bent afterwards to fit what you managed ([[Planning a Production]] has
the same rule for stories).

## Analysing a test

A test is only worth running if you look at what it tells you:

1. Measure or test **against the standard you wrote**, not against
   what you hoped.
2. Record the result exactly — the number, the distance, what the user
   said in their own words.
3. Where it failed, find the **cause**, not just the symptom: the hole
   was small *because* printed holes shrink, not "because it was
   wrong".
4. Change the design to remove the cause, and test again. That loop is
   the whole of [[The Rig]] and most of [[The Sign Shop]].

Many companies go further and organise their whole business around
written quality standards — the ISO 9001 family is the best known — so
that every product is checked the same way, whoever is on shift.
