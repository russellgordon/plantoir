Short passages, read hard. The method is on [[Reading Like a Writer]].

**1.** *The lights buzzed. Somewhere down the corridor a locker closed,
and then nothing for a long time.* — What is the mood, and which three
choices build it?

> [!success]- Answer 1
> Unease. Built by: *buzzed* (agitated, not neutral); the locker heard
> but not seen, which puts us in the dark with the narrator; and *nothing
> for a long time*, which is a sentence about absence — the writer is
> using silence as an event.

**2.** *She said she was fine. She said it again at the door.* — What
does the repetition tell you that the sentences do not say?

> [!success]- Answer 2
> That she is not fine. The reader infers from the repetition and from
> the fact that the narrator noticed and counted. Neither is stated,
> which is why it works.

**3.** *He was the kind of teacher who remembered your name at the start of the course
and used it in July.* — What is the effect of the dates?

> [!success]- Answer 3
> Specificity does the praising. "Kind" or "caring" would be an opinion;
> September-to-July is evidence, and it lets the reader do the judging —
> which they trust more than being told.

**4.** *The land does not belong to us. That was the first thing she
taught me, and the last thing I understood.* — What is the turn, and how
is it made?

> [!success]- Answer 4
> The turn is *and the last thing I understood*, which converts a
> statement about ownership into a story about the speaker's slowness.
> The parallel structure (*first thing … last thing*) is the machinery.

**5.** Two sentences describe the same room. Which is doing more work,
and why? (a) *The room was small and untidy.* (b) *There was one chair,
and the coats were on it.*

> [!success]- Answer 5
> (b). Same information, delivered as an image a reader can see. The
> reader concludes "small and untidy" themselves, which is both more
> vivid and more convincing. Show, then let them judge.

**6.** Take any four lines from the text we are reading now. Mark the
word you would stress if you read it aloud, and say what changes if you
stress a different one.

> [!success]- Answer 6
> No fixed answer. The point: stress *is* interpretation. Two readings of
> the same line can be two arguments about it, which is exactly what
> [[Reading Aloud]] means by preparation.
