Civics is not a course about being a good citizen. It is a course about
how public decisions are actually made in Canada, who has the power to
make them, and what you can do when you think one of them is wrong.

It is short — a half credit, running across the second half of the semester —
so almost every period does something.

## A normal period

1. **A real thing.** A bill, a budget line, a council minute, a court
   decision, a news item from this week. You meet it before it is
   explained.
2. **A question about it.** Usually in threes: who decided this, under
   what authority, and who else wanted something different? "How do you
   know?" is the standard follow-up here, and it is not an accusation.
3. **A position, in writing.** You commit to a view, with the evidence
   attached, and you say what would change your mind.

Naming the concept comes afterwards. That is why the pages in
**Concepts** assume you have already met the idea in a class.

## What the course asks of you

- **Check before you assert.** Every civic claim in this room needs a
  source somebody else could find. [[Judging Civic Information]] is the
  method.
- **State the other side at its strongest.** It is a marked criterion in
  four of the six assessed tasks, not a courtesy.
- **Do the reading.** Periods start from a document you were meant to
  have seen; arriving without it means catching up on what everyone else
  is already arguing about.
- **Keep the notebook.** [[Your Civic Notebook]] is where the culminating
  project comes from, and it cannot be reconstructed at the end of the course.

## The shape of the term

Three units of fourteen periods. **How Decisions Get Made** — democratic
values, the orders of government, Indigenous governance, and how a law is
passed. **Rights, and Who Holds Them** — the constitutional documents,
what limits a right, responsibilities, and who is left out. **Doing
Something About It** — one issue, one real action, carried out and
reported on.

The term ends with [[The Civic Action Project]], which is an action taken
in the world outside this building, and then a two-and-a-half-hour
[[The Issue Examination]] on sources you have not seen.

> [!note] Political disagreement is expected here, and it is managed
> We will study questions on which your classmates, and their families,
> genuinely disagree. [[Our Working Agreement]] sets out how we argue
> about those — read it in the first week, not the week you need it.

## Connecting to your community: youth service and leadership

Civic participation extends beyond classroom analysis into active service. In
Ontario, all secondary students complete forty hours of community involvement to
graduate. This course helps you identify meaningful service and leadership
opportunities in your community:

- Serving on municipal youth advisory committees or school board student
  senates.
- Volunteering with local food banks, newcomer settlement agencies, and youth
  advocacy groups.
- Participating in environmental conservation projects and community cleanups.
- Working as trained poll workers during municipal, provincial, or federal
  elections.

Connecting your coursework to real community service builds practical leadership
and civic commitment that lasts well beyond Grade 10. See
[[Service and Contribution]].

## If you fall behind

Say so early. In a ten-week course there is no quiet stretch to catch up
in, and a missed week early on is a real problem by the end of the course.
[[Getting Help]] lists the ways to reach me, and [[Help Sessions]] has
the times I am in the room.
