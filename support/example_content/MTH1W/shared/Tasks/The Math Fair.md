## What you are making

The Math Fair is the culminating task and the whole of your final
evaluation — thirty per cent of the credit, as [[How Marks Work]]
explains. It reaches back across every unit on purpose: nothing on your
table has to come from the last month, and most of it should not.

Your exhibit has two panels and a statement.

**The story panel.** Research one concept — a number concept, an
algebraic concept, or a geometric concept or measurement system. Which
of the three you pick decides which of this task's expectations your
exhibit answers, so choose the one you actually want to spend a
fortnight with, and
tell the story of its development and use in a specific culture or
community, and why it still matters today. Zero alone crossed three
cultures on its way to Europe; every measurement system is a story
about what somebody needed to count. If you take a geometric concept or
a measurement system, say where it shows up in real work — a trade, a
career, another subject.

**The problem panel.** Re-solve your own best problem from the course,
better — a new method, a clearer representation, an extension — and show
both solutions side by side, with what changed in you between them.

**The growth statement**, built from your [[Math Journal]] —
[[Showing Growth]] shows how to mine it, and the statement you write
here becomes the seed of your [[Final Reflection]].

At the fair you present twice over: a visitor-facing display that stands
alone for a stranger, and a defended conversation with visitors and me,
where you explain your choices.

## How to work

1. In the launch period, choose your concept and your problem, and say
   both aloud to one other person — hearing yourself choose is half the
   choosing.
2. In the research studio, go past the first search result. The
   interesting history is rarely on page one, and two sources that
   disagree are worth more to you than five that repeat each other.
3. Re-solve first, compare after. Do not let the old solution steer the
   new one; the point is the route you can now see and could not then.
4. Reread your journal start to finish and pull three dated, specific
   moments of growth — claims need evidence, even claims about
   yourself.
5. Write the display for a stranger — [[Writing About Math]] is the
   guide. In the last studio period, judge your table against the
   criteria below using [[Judging Your Own Work]], write down what you
   will change, and then rehearse the conversation with a peer playing a
   sceptical visitor. The setup period on fair morning is when the
   changes get made.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like at your table |
| --- | --- |
| Stands alone | A stranger follows the exhibit without you narrating |
| A story with a place in it | The concept is traced to a named culture or community, not to "ancient times" |
| Still relevant | The panel says where the concept lives now, and in whose hands |
| Honest sourcing | Research is credited; the problem work is genuinely yours |
| A second solution worth having | The re-solve is really different, and both are shown side by side |
| Evidenced growth | The statement cites specific, dated journal entries |
| Defended, not recited | Questions get reasons, not a replayed script |

## Reflect

The growth statement is this task's written reflection; the defended
conversation is its spoken one. Together they answer: what can you see
now that you could not see at the start of the course, and how do you know?

> [!success]- If you cannot choose (click to expand)
> For the problem panel, flip through your journal for the entry with
> the most crossings-out — that struggle is your candidate. For the
> story panel, start with a number, a shape, or a way of measuring that
> your own family or community uses, and trace it backwards.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

Fair day is the one day of the course when nothing EXTRA can be
gathered. The exhibit and the defended conversation ARE what is being
judged, and the class page books that conversation as two minutes at
every table for exactly that reason — protect it, because hosting the
room will otherwise eat it. Everything below therefore comes out of the
studio periods, where there is still time for it to change somebody's
exhibit.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 13, the research studio
  Watch for the difference between a student hunting a story and a
  student hunting a page. The panel credits its sources, but a list of
  three is easy to assemble after the fact, so it cannot tell you which
  of them were ever read against each other. A student who has met two
  accounts that disagree, and says which one they believe, is doing the
  research their chosen expectation asks for; a student who paraphrased
  the first result and cited the rest has a panel that looks identical.
  Going well: two sources open at once, and a note about where they part
  company.
  Stuck: one page found in the first five minutes and rewritten in
  different words for the rest of the period.
  Record: a tick and the concept beside each name, one line each.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 13, in the same research studio, on your second pass
  Day 16 looks like the obvious slot, and it is not: that period also
  collects the design task and hears every group's spoken defence. Day
  13 is better anyway, because an answer given while the reading is
  still open can still change the exhibit.
  Ask: "Whose problem was this concept solving, and what did people do
  before they had it?"
  Then: "Where did the idea travel to after that, and who changed it on
  the way?"
  A strong first answer puts the concept into somebody's hands with a
  need attached, which is what B1.1, C1.1 and E1.1 each mean by
  development and use — whichever of the three this student's concept
  falls under. A panel of names and dates can be assembled without any
  of it. A strong second answer knows the story does not stop where
  their table stops, which is the difference between researching a
  concept and copying an entry about one.
  Record: a tick per student when the need lands in somebody's own
  terms, and a second when the story keeps travelling past their table.
  Anyone with neither tick by the end of the period is the student to
  reach before Day 16.

The product evidence is the exhibit and the defended conversation on
Day 17.
