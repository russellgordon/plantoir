Ontario requires forty hours of community involvement to graduate, and the
requirement produces a predictable reaction: find forty hours, get them
signed, never think about it again. This page is an argument for treating
it as something other than a form to fill.

## What counts as a contribution to the common good

The common good is what a community holds together — the things nobody
owns individually and everybody depends on. A contribution is any action
that adds to it. That covers a wider range than volunteering:

| Kind of action | What it adds | Scale it works at |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direct service | Meals served, tutoring done, a river cleaned | Immediate, local, measurable |
| Organising | More people doing the above, sustained after you leave | Local to national |
| Advocacy | A rule changed, so the need falls | Wherever the rule is made |
| Documentation | A problem nobody could previously prove | Anywhere the evidence travels |
| Everyday reliability | Jury duty, honest testimony, showing up | Invisible until it stops |

Notice that the middle rows scale and the first does not. Serving meals
feeds the people in front of you tonight; changing the rule that produced
the need can feed people you will never meet. Neither is the superior
choice — advocacy without service is often people arguing about a problem
they have never seen — but knowing which one you are doing is the
difference between contributing and being busy.

## Individuals and organisations, and the difference

Named individuals get the credit in most accounts of civic contribution.
The organisation is usually what made the contribution possible and
usually what outlives it — a community association with a phone number
and a bank account can still act in ten years, and a person cannot
guarantee that.

When you assess someone's civic significance, ask three things: what
changed, how many people it reached, and whether it lasted after they
stopped. The third question is where most reputations lose altitude, and
where organisations gain it.

## Where to find real service

- **Your school** — peer tutoring, student council, clubs that actually
  run something, the events that need setup crews.
- **Municipal committees** — many Ontario municipalities have advisory
  committees with youth seats, and they are chronically undersubscribed.
- **Community organisations** — food banks, libraries, newcomer
  settlement services, animal shelters, environmental groups, sports
  leagues that need coaches and officials.
- **Election work** — Elections Canada, Elections Ontario and municipal
  clerks hire and train poll workers, and the age requirement is lower
  than most students expect.
- **Advocacy organisations** — including ones your family disagrees with;
  you are allowed to choose.

Leadership in any of these is not a title. It is being the person who
knows what happens next and tells everyone else, which is available to
you in your first week and does not require an election.

## Where civic education leads: careers and pathways

The inquiry and communication skills you practise in this course are directly
applicable in a wide range of professional fields:

- **Public administration and municipal governance**: Policy analysts,
  legislative assistants, municipal clerks, urban planners, and program officers
  who design and implement government services.
- **Law, justice, and human rights**: Lawyers, paralegals, legal aid workers,
  mediators, and human rights tribunal officers who interpret legislation and
  advocate for clients.
- **Community leadership and non-profits**: Directors, community organisers, and
  volunteer coordinators managing social service agencies and charities.
- **Journalism and public affairs**: Reporters and editors who investigate
  government decisions, analyse public records, and inform the public.
- **Labour and stakeholder relations**: Union representatives and corporate
  compliance officers who negotiate collective agreements and ensure regulatory
  adherence.

Log what you do in [[Your Civic Notebook]] as you go — hours, what you
actually did, and what you would change. [[The Civic Action Project]] will
ask for exactly that, and reconstructing it at the end of the course is much
harder than writing it down in the first week.
