## What you are making

A technician's log of your semester: one short entry after every bench
day, written in the last minutes of class while your hands still
remember what they did — [[How the Lab Runs]] reserves that time on
purpose, because an entry written the night before collection is a
reconstruction and always reads like one. Ten honest minutes beats
thirty performed ones. Entries are **private**: I read them, other
students never do, and nothing is quoted anywhere without your
explicit okay.

If you kept this journal in Grade 10, the routine is unchanged. The
contents are not.

## What every entry carries

Four prompts, in whatever order helps, plus two things that are new
this year and are not optional.

- **What I built** — the facts, briefly: which circuit, which board,
  where your bench got to before tools-away.
- **What I predicted** — the number you committed to *before* you
  measured, with its unit and the reasoning behind it. Even when it
  was wrong. Especially when it was wrong.
- **What the meter said** — the measurement, with its unit and the
  conditions: supply voltage, which two points the probes were on,
  what range the meter was set to.
- **What fought back** — the specific fault in its own words. Name
  the component and the symptom: "the LED lit dimly and the resistor
  was too hot to hold", not "it was being weird".
- **What I learned** — the fix and *why* it worked, or the idea that
  finally clicked and what did the clicking.
- **What I would try next** — one concrete move for the next bench
  day. Not "get better at soldering": something you could actually do.

Plus a **sketch**. Every entry that involves a circuit gets a small
hand-drawn schematic of what you actually wired — not what the lab
sheet said, what *you* built. It takes ninety seconds, it is the
fastest way to find a wiring error, and it is the only record of your
build that survives the breadboard being stripped. Photographs of your
own bench are welcome any time and do not replace the sketch; a photo
shows where the wires were, a schematic shows what you meant.

## The numbers rule

A journal entry that contains no quantity is a diary entry. Every
measurement gets three things: the value, the unit, and the condition
under which it was taken. "3.2 V across the LED at 15 mA, supply at
5.0 V" is a fact somebody else could reproduce. "The voltage was
about three" is a memory of a fact.

This is the same standard [[Writing About Technology]] applies to
every written thing in this course, and it is not a school
convention — it is what makes a service log worth keeping. A
technician reading your entry six months from now, including you,
needs to know whether the reading was taken with the motor running.

## Success criteria and collection

The journal is collected at the end of each unit and read against
these criteria. That reading comes back to you as comments, not as a
percentage — the entries that carry marks are the milestone ones a
task's own criteria ask for, which [[How Marks Work]] explains.
[[Journal Checklist]] turns the criteria below into a self-check to
run before you hand the journal in.

| Quality | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Regularity | An entry for every bench day, written close to it |
| Honesty | Real trouble recorded, not a highlight reel |
| Precision | Values with units and conditions; faults named and located |
| Evidence | A sketch or a photo of your own work in most entries |
| Growth | "Try next" items reappear later as things actually tried |
