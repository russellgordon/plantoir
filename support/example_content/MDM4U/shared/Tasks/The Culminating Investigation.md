## What you are making

Every other task in this course hands you the question. This one does
not. You choose something you actually want to know, find or collect
data that could answer it, analyse that data with the tools this
course gives you, and defend a conclusion in writing and out loud in
front of the class.

It is the largest single piece of work in the course, and it is
deliberately not a final-week project. It is launched in Unit 2, it
collects a milestone every few classes, and by the time the analysis
starts you should already be bored of your own data set. That is the
plan working.

Your question may be one substantial problem, or two smaller ones —
one about probability and one about statistics. Both routes are
allowed and both are marked the same way.

## The milestone schedule

| Checkpoint | What is due |
| --- | --- |
| Unit 2, Day 13 | Two candidate questions, with variables and possible sources |
| Unit 2, Day 14 | One question approved, in a sentence, with a named source |
| Unit 2, Day 17 | The plan: population, variables, method, size, expected bias |
| Unit 3, Day 8 | Collection under way — a third of the data in hand |
| Unit 3, Day 16 | Data collected, organized, and fully cited |
| Unit 4, Day 10 | Analysis worked, conclusion drafted, one limitation named |
| Unit 4, Day 15 | The written report handed in |
| Unit 4, Days 16–17 | Presented and critiqued at the symposium |

Miss a milestone and the work does not disappear — it lands on top of
the next one, which is exactly how projects fail. Come and see me
before the checkpoint, not after.

## Choosing a question you can actually answer

The single most common way this project goes wrong is a magnificent
question with no reachable data. "Does social media cause anxiety in
teenagers?" is a research programme, not a semester project. "Do
students in this school who report more than three hours of screen
time on a school night sleep fewer hours?" is answerable, with a
survey you could run, and it is *more* interesting because you might
be wrong about it.

Three honest tests, before you commit:

1. **Can you name the data?** Not "there must be statistics on this" —
   an actual data set, from an actual place. Start at
   [[Where to Find Real Data]] and check what the columns really are
   using [[Reading a Data Dictionary]].
2. **Can you get it before the collection deadline?** A survey of your
   own school is realistic; a survey of a city is not. Secondary data
   from Statistics Canada is realistic; data a company keeps private
   is not.
3. **Could the answer come out either way?** If you already know the
   answer, you have chosen a topic, not a question.

[[Choosing a Data Set]] walks through this in more detail, and I would
rather approve a small, sharp question than admire a large, doomed
one.

## What a defensible report contains

- **The question**, stated precisely enough that somebody else could
  test whether you answered it.
- **Background**: what is already known, and where you looked.
- **The plan**: population, variables, sampling method, sample size,
  and the sources of bias you anticipated — in the form you agreed to
  at the approval checkpoint.
- **The data**: how you [[E1.3|gathered and organized]] it, cleaned
  honestly, with every source cited per [[Citing Your Sources]].
- **The analysis**: numerical and graphical summaries, each chosen for
  a reason you can state — a probability model, a normal model, a
  regression, a correlation, an expected value, as the question
  demands. Two are required whatever your question: a **measure of
  spread** as well as a measure of centre, because a mean alone hides
  the story; and, where you have two attributes, a **scatter plot with
  the relationship named** — linear or non-linear, strong or weak,
  positive or negative — before any line is fitted to it. If your
  variable is continuous, the interval width of your histogram is a
  decision you state and defend.
- **The conclusion**: what the data supports, in a sentence the data
  can carry.
- **The limitations**: sample size, bias, missing groups, confounding
  variables. This section is not an apology; it is the part that
  proves you understood your own study.
- **Follow-up**: the next question, and what you would do differently.

## How it is assessed

Per [[How Marks Work]], the strength of the argument is the product.
An investigation with a small sample and a carefully limited
conclusion outscores one with impressive data and a claim the data
never earned. Two failures are fatal in a way nothing else is: data
that cannot be traced to its source, and a conclusion stated with more
confidence than the evidence supports.

The report is the heaviest single piece of the seventy per cent — the
semester's work — and it is assessed from the milestones as much as
from the document, which is why missing one costs more than it looks
like it should. The *defence* of it is separate, and belongs to the
thirty: see [[The Data Symposium]].

**If you work as a pair, you are still marked as yourself.** There is
no shared mark. Divide the report by section and sign each one, keep
your own dated decisions in your own journal, and answer for the whole
study on your own at the conferences — on Unit 2, Day 16, at the
ten-minute feedback conference on Unit 4, Day 9, and at the last
conference on Unit 4, Day 15. Each of you presents alone at the
symposium.

Every investigation gets ten minutes of feedback from me on Unit 4,
Day 9. Days 11 to 13 are the periods that exist so you can act on it —
limitations, conclusion, and graphs, in that order — and Day 15 is the
last assembly before it comes in. Run [[Judging Your Own Work]]
against the criteria table below on Day 13, while there is still a
working period left to use.

Your [[Math Journal]] runs alongside the project — the question you
abandoned and why, the day the data turned out to be messier than the
plan, the analysis that surprised you. Those entries are not marked in
themselves — but the report cannot honestly say why you chose this
source and rejected that one unless somebody wrote it down at the
time, and only your journal did. [[Showing Growth]] explains how to
use it once it exists.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like in your work |
| --- | --- |
| A question worth asking | Precise, answerable, and genuinely open |
| A plan that anticipated trouble | Bias and limits named before collection |
| Traceable data | Every number leads back to a citable source |
| Analysis chosen, not sprayed | Each summary justified by the question |
| A conclusion the data carries | Claims sized to the evidence behind them |
| Limits owned | Weaknesses named by you, before anyone asks |

> [!tip]- If your data turns out to be worse than you hoped
> This happens to real researchers constantly, and it is not a reason
> to start over in the last week. A smaller honest analysis of flawed
> data — with the flaws documented and their effect on the conclusion
> argued — is worth far more than a rescue attempt with invented
> numbers. Fabricated or "adjusted" data ends the project, so bring
> the problem to me the day you find it and we will resize the
> question together.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 8, the working period, before the clinic tables
open
  The thing to watch is the order of two acts: looking at the data,
  and naming a technique. Some students plot it, sort it, or scroll
  the whole column first, and the technique falls out of what they
  see. Others name a technique — regression, because there are two
  columns; the normal model, because it was the last thing taught —
  and then feed the data into it. Both arrive at a summary with a
  justifying sentence beside it, so the report cannot separate them,
  and no row of the criteria table asks. This is the difference
  between E1.4 done and E1.4 performed.
  Going well: a graph, a sort, or a scan of the whole column happens
  before any technique is named out loud.
  Stuck: the technique is named first, and the data is reshaped until
  it will accept it.
  Record: three columns in your day plan, looked, chose first,
  neither. The middle column is who to sit with on Day 9.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 9, at the ten-minute feedback conference already on
that agenda
  Open with: "Show me a summary you computed and then abandoned, and
  tell me why it went." Most students have one and have never been
  asked about it; the ones who have none have usually computed exactly
  what they were told to.
  Then: "Suppose you had twice as much data. Which part of your
  conclusion would change, and which part would not move at all?"
  A strong answer separates precision from bias without being given
  the words — twice the data narrows the spread and does absolutely
  nothing to a frame that never reached a whole group, so the
  limitation that survives doubling is the one that matters. That is
  E1.5's "evaluate the strength of the evidence", and a report can
  list sample size and bias in a limitations paragraph without the
  author being able to tell you which of them more data would fix.
  Record: one line per student on the conference list, plus the name
  of the abandoned summary. Anyone who cannot name one goes on Day
  11's list.

The product evidence is the report handed in on Unit 4, Day 15.
