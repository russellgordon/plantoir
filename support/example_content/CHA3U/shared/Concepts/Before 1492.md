The land that later became the United States was not empty, not uniform, and
not waiting. Hundreds of nations lived on it, speaking unrelated languages
and organizing themselves in ways that had little in common beyond the fact
that each was worked out for the place it was in. If you take one idea from
this page, take that one: the differences between these nations are the
evidence, not the noise.

## Environment first, then everything else

Ask what a place gave people and what it withheld, and much of the rest
follows — what was eaten, what was built, whether communities moved with the
seasons or stayed put, and what could be traded away.

| Region | How people lived before contact |
| --- | --- |
| Eastern Woodlands | Corn, beans and squash grown together; longhouse villages |
| Missouri River plains | Mandan, Hidatsa and Arikara earthlodge towns and farms |
| Southwest | Ancestral Puebloan stone towns; Hohokam canals in the desert |
| Mississippi valley | Mound-building centres, of which Cahokia was the largest |
| Northwest Coast | Salmon runs and cedar; wealth stored and given away |

Cahokia deserves a second look. Archaeologists describe a planned city of
earthen mounds and plazas across the river from present-day St. Louis, built
and occupied for generations before 1492 and abandoned before any European
saw it. Population estimates for it vary widely and honest accounts say so.
What is not in doubt is the scale of the labour, which means organization,
which means politics.

## Government, belief, and who decided

The Haudenosaunee — the people English speakers later called Iroquois —
bound five nations together under the Great Law of Peace: Mohawk, Oneida,
Onondaga, Cayuga and Seneca, joined much later by the Tuscarora. Clan
mothers chose and could remove the leaders who sat in council. That is a
constitutional arrangement, and it was working before any European document
in North America existed.

Elsewhere the arrangements were different again — hereditary house leaders
on the Northwest Coast, village councils on the Missouri, kin networks
across the Southwest. Spiritual life was not a separate department of these
societies; obligations to the land, to animals and to ancestors were the
same obligations that governed planting, hunting and trade. Knowledge passed
by oral tradition, which is a method of record-keeping with its own rules of
accuracy, not an absence of records.

When you compare two nations here, compare like with like and say what you
are comparing on. That is what [[Historical Perspective]] asks of you, and
it is the habit that makes [[What Did the Frontier Mean|What Did the Frontier Mean?]] answerable.
