Every task page in this course prints what earns the marks before you write
a word. Most people read that table once, on launch day, and never open it
again — then hand in an argument that misses two rows they could have fixed
in a work period. This page is that work period, used properly.

We run it together the first time, on a paragraph belonging to nobody, so
that the move is familiar before it is your own essay under the light.

## The routine

1. Put the task page's criteria table beside your draft.
2. Take the rows one at a time. Write **yes**, **not yet**, or **partly**
   against each — and one line of evidence: the paragraph, the footnote,
   the sentence a reader would point at. "Yes" with nothing beside it is a
   hope, not a judgement.
3. Name your weakest row. Not the quickest to repair — the weakest.
4. Write one sentence saying what you will change, and in which period.
5. Change it, in the time that follows. That is why this never happens on a
   hand-in day: a verdict you have no time to act on is only anxiety with a
   table attached.

Steps 2 and 3 are the whole exercise. Saying which row is weakest is harder
than any of the fixing, because it means writing down the thing you were
hoping the reader would skim past.

> [!example]- One row, judged honestly
> From [[The Union Divided]]: *"Causation — causes ranked and weighed, and
> the ranking defended."*
>
> **Partly.** I rank four causes and I defend the first one. The second and
> third are in the order I found them, not in an order I argued for, and
> nowhere do I say what standard put them there. Weakest row.
> **Change:** write the criteria sentence into the introduction, then
> reorder the middle two if the criteria do not put them where they are —
> in the rest of this period, which is exactly what it is for.
>
> That paragraph is worth more than "looks fine" written against all five
> rows, and it took two minutes.

## Historians do this to each other

Peer reading against the criteria happens in this course too — a partner
reading your brief before [[The Long Argument]] is delivered, for instance.
Their reading is useful and it is not a mark: what a classmate says about
your work, and what you say about it yourself, are outside the mark
entirely. See [[How Marks Work]]. That is what makes both worth doing
honestly — an accurate verdict here can only help you, because it cannot
cost you anything.

## Where the record goes

Copy your weakest row and your one-sentence change into
[[Your Inquiry Notebook]], dated. Over a term those lines become the most
useful pages in the notebook: they are the only place your own reasoning
about your own work is written down, and they are what
[[Skills You Are Building]] asks you to draw on when it wants an example
rather than a claim.

One thing worth doing at the end: read your judgement aloud to the person
beside you. You will hear the rows where you were being generous before
they say anything.
