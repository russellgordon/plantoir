Between 1939 and 1945 Canadian civilians were rationed, recruited,
relocated, taxed, censored and put to work. The obvious conclusion is that
the war changed the country. The useful conclusion is narrower: some of
those changes lasted, some reversed the moment the shooting stopped, and a
few things that look like change were only the war making an existing
arrangement visible.

## The question

**Which wartime changes at home survived the war, and for whom?**

Three dates, not two. Compare 1939 with 1945, and then compare 1945 with
1950. The second comparison is where the answer actually lives.

## The evidence set

- [ ] Employment and wage records for women in industry, 1939 through 1946
- [ ] Wartime recruitment material aimed at women, including the Bren Gun Girl
- [ ] Ration books, price controls, and the notices announcing their end
- [ ] Records of the internment and dispossession of Japanese Canadians
- [ ] Farm labour arrangements, and who was moved to do the work
- [ ] Veterans' benefits, and who was and was not eligible for them
- [ ] The family allowance, introduced as the war ended

Those boxes are printed, not interactive — nothing is saved when you tap
one. Copy the list into your own notes and tick it there.

## The reasoning the class does

Take one line of evidence each and report back with two figures or two
quotations: the wartime state of affairs, and the state of affairs five
years later. Then sort the class's findings into three piles — **kept**,
**reversed**, and **revealed**.

The third pile is the interesting one. Wartime industry did not create
women's paid work; it changed which paid work was open, and how loudly it
was praised. The internment did not invent the legal power to remove people
without charge; it used a power the *War Measures Act* already carried,
against a community that had been discriminated against for decades. Naming
something as "revealed" rather than "changed" is a real historical claim,
and you will have to defend it.

Finally, ask the "for whom" question of every pile. A veteran, a woman
leaving a factory, a Japanese Canadian family whose property had been sold
by the state, and a First Nations soldier returning to a reserve did not
leave 1945 with the same country.

Work the method in [[Continuity and Change]], read the period in
[[The War at Home]] and
[[Indigenous Peoples and the Second World War]], and take the judgement into
[[The Wartime Decision]].
