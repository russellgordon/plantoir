## The situation

Every few months something is announced as the battery that changes
everything. Some of those announcements are real chemistry at an early
stage; some are a press release. You now know enough electrochemistry to
tell the difference, and this task is where you prove it.

Two pages, for a reader who took Grade 10 science and no further.

## What must be in it

**1. The technology.** One electrochemical energy technology: a
lithium-ion cell, a sodium-ion cell, a hydrogen fuel cell, a flow
battery, a lead-acid battery, or another you can source properly.

**2. The chemistry, written out.** The half equations at each electrode,
the overall cell reaction, and the standard cell potential worked from
[[Reading a Reduction Potential Table]] where the values exist. Balanced
by a named method — [[Redox Bookkeeping]] has both.

**3. What it is genuinely good at**, in numbers: energy per kilogram,
how many cycles before it degrades, how quickly it charges, what it
costs, and what temperature range it tolerates. Sourced, with the source
named where you use it.

**4. What it is bad at**, in the same units. A report with no weaknesses
in it has not been researched.

**5. The viability verdict.** For ONE specific job — a phone, a car, a
grid store for a wind farm, a remote community's overnight supply —
could this technology actually do it? Answer with the numbers from
part 3, not with enthusiasm.

**6. Health and safety, honestly.** What is hazardous about this
chemistry, in manufacture, in use, in failure, and at end of life.
Corrosion belongs here too: [[Corrosion and Electrolysis]] is the same
electrochemistry running where nobody wanted it — in pipes, in hulls,
in reinforcing steel — and the mitigations are electrochemical as well.

## Which half of this is yours

Parts 1 to 4 are the pair's. You choose one technology between you,
balance one set of equations, and gather one table of numbers, and the
two of you may hand in the same version of all of it.

**Parts 5 and 6 are written separately, by each of you, and they are
what your own mark rests on.** Pick different jobs for the verdict if
you can — one of you sizing a phone, the other a grid store — because
the same numbers reach opposite conclusions depending on what is being
asked of them, and finding that out is most of the point of the task.
Two identical verdicts are one verdict handed in twice.

## The working periods

| Day | What it is for |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Technology chosen; half equations written and balanced |
| 2 | The numbers, from sources you can name |
| 3 | The verdict and the safety section, challenged by another pair |

## How this is assessed

| Quality | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Chemistry correct | Half equations balanced; potentials read correctly |
| Researched | Real numbers with real sources, not adjectives |
| Balanced | Strengths and weaknesses in the same units |
| Verdict is defended | A specific job, answered from the numbers |
| Safety is specific | Named hazards at named stages, with mitigations |
| Written for the reader | Two pages a non-chemist finishes and understands |

> [!question]- If the numbers disagree between sources
> They will. Report the range and say who claims what — a manufacturer's
> figure and an independent test are different kinds of evidence, and
> noticing that is worth more marks than picking one and hiding the
> other. [[What Counts as Evidence]] is the argument this rests on.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 5, Day 6, the working period where the half equations are
written
  Watch for: whether the method they name is the method they use. The
  page asks for a named method and nearly every pair will write one
  down; a balanced equation cannot tell you which one produced it, and
  "half-reaction method" written above a set of coefficients arrived at
  by adjustment is the commonest thing on the page.
  Going well: the method said out loud before a pen moves; oxidation
  numbers written above every atom, including the ones that do not
  change; electrons counted and the two halves scaled to match.
  Stuck: coefficients nudged until both sides balance, with the
  electrons added last to make the count come out; a half equation
  lifted from a source with the states left off.
  Record: two lists in your day plan — used the method they named, and
  did not. That is one half of F2.3; the other half, having more than
  one method available, is what the practice set on Day 3 and the
  examination are for.

TALK — Unit 5, Day 7, at the two minutes with each pair already on that
agenda
  Ask: "Name the number that would have to change before your verdict
  flipped, and say how far it would have to move."
  Then: "Suppose one of these cells fails in front of somebody. Not what
  breaks — what does the person standing beside it see, and what should
  they do in the next ten seconds?"

  Record: a circle in your day plan against any pair whose verdict still
