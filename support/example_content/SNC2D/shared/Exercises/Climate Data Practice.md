Some of these are arithmetic and some are judgement, and the judgement
ones are harder. Where a question gives you numbers, they are **invented
for practice** and labelled as such — the real ones live in the
published records, and part of the point of this page is that you should
go and get them rather than trust a number in a worksheet.

**1.** Define **albedo**, **anthropogenic**, **hydrosphere**, and **heat
sink**, and then use all four correctly in a single connected paragraph
about sea ice.

> [!success]- Answer 1
> - **Albedo** — the fraction of the light falling on a surface that
>   the surface reflects rather than absorbs.
> - **Anthropogenic** — caused by human activity, as opposed to arising
>   from natural processes.
> - **Hydrosphere** — all the water on the planet: oceans, lakes,
>   rivers, groundwater, and ice.
> - **Heat sink** — something that absorbs and stores a large amount of
>   thermal energy while changing temperature relatively little.
>
> A paragraph that uses all four:
>
> > Sea ice has a high albedo, so most of the sunlight reaching it is
> > reflected straight back out. When ice is lost, the darker ocean
> > underneath is exposed, and open water has a low albedo — it absorbs
> > most of what lands on it. Because the ocean is an enormous heat
> > sink, that absorbed energy is stored in the hydrosphere rather than
> > raising the temperature quickly, which is part of why the ocean
> > responds more slowly than the air above it. Where the ice loss is
> > driven by anthropogenic warming, the albedo change is not the
> > original cause, but it adds to the warming that produced it.
>
> The last sentence is the one worth writing carefully. Cause and
> consequence get tangled here, and saying which is which is most of
> the skill.

**2.** Rank these surfaces from highest albedo to lowest, and give the
mechanism: fresh snow, a coniferous forest, open ocean, sea ice.

> [!success]- Answer 2
> Fresh snow, then sea ice, then coniferous forest, then open ocean.
>
> The mechanism is simply how much light comes back out. Fresh snow is
> bright and white and returns most of what falls on it. Sea ice is
> also bright but less so, especially where it is thin, wet, or dusty.
> A dark conifer canopy absorbs a large fraction, and the structure of
> the canopy traps light that bounces between needles instead of
> escaping. Open ocean is the darkest of the four and absorbs almost
> everything reaching it, particularly when the sun is high.
>
> The consequence worth stating: swapping a high-albedo surface for a
> low-albedo one puts more of the same incoming sunlight into the
> system. Nothing about the sun changed.

**3.** Sort these into natural influences on climate and anthropogenic
ones: large volcanic eruptions, combustion of fossil fuels, changes in
Earth's orbit, deforestation, variation in the sun's output, cement
production. Then say why the sorting is not the end of the argument.

> [!success]- Answer 3
> **Natural:** large volcanic eruptions, changes in Earth's orbit,
> variation in the sun's output.
>
> **Anthropogenic:** combustion of fossil fuels, deforestation, cement
> production.
>
> **Why sorting is not enough:** knowing that a natural influence
> *exists* tells you nothing about whether it is responsible for a
> particular change. Natural influences have not stopped operating, and
> the scientific question is not "natural or human?" but "how much of
> the observed change does each one account for, over this period?"
>
> That question is answered by looking at **timing, size, and
> direction**. An influence that would push temperature the wrong way,
> or that operates over tens of thousands of years when the change took
> a century, cannot be the explanation for that change no matter how
> real it is. Naming a possible cause is not the same as showing it did
> the work.

**4.** Match each proxy record to what it preserves, and state one
limitation of each: tree rings, ice cores, lake sediment cores.

> [!success]- Answer 4
> **Tree rings** preserve a year-by-year record of growing conditions,
> one ring per year, so the dating can be extremely precise.
> *Limitation:* ring width responds to more than one thing — moisture,
> temperature, light, disease, competition — so separating a
> temperature signal from the rest requires care, and the record only
> reaches back as far as the oldest available wood.
>
> **Ice cores** preserve layers of snowfall, with actual samples of
> ancient air trapped in bubbles, plus chemical indicators related to
> past temperature. *Limitation:* they exist only where ice has
> persisted, so the geography is restricted, and the deepest layers are
> compressed, which blurs the time resolution.
>
> **Lake sediment cores** preserve what settled out year by year —
> pollen, ash, mineral grains — which speaks to the vegetation and
> conditions around the lake. *Limitation:* the layers can be disturbed
> by burrowing organisms, currents, or slumping, and the link from
> pollen to climate runs through the plants, which is an extra step
> where things can go wrong.
>
> The pattern across all three: a proxy is an **indirect** measurement,
> and every indirect measurement carries an assumption about the link
> between what was recorded and what you want to know. Saying that
> assumption out loud is the difference between using a proxy and
> quoting one.

**5.** *(Invented numbers, real pattern.)* Two stations at a similar
latitude in Ontario. One is on a Great Lakes shoreline; one is well
inland.

| Station | January mean (°C) | July mean (°C) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shoreline | $-4$ | 21 |
| Inland | $-11$ | 22 |

Calculate the annual range for each and explain the difference.

> [!success]- Answer 5
> Shoreline range: $21 - (-4) = 25\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
>
> Inland range: $22 - (-11) = 33\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
>
> The shoreline station has a range about $8\ ^\circ\text{C}$ smaller,
> and almost all of that difference is in winter rather than summer.
>
> **The mechanism is heat capacity.** A large body of water is a heat
> sink: it absorbs a great deal of energy in summer for a modest rise
> in temperature, and gives that energy back slowly through autumn and
> early winter, which keeps the air over and downwind of it warmer than
> it would otherwise be. Land has nothing like that capacity — it heats
> and cools quickly, so inland winters run colder and summers a touch
> warmer.
>
> This is the same physics your class measured on the bench when water,
> sand, and soil went under the same lamp in
> [[Modelling the Greenhouse Effect]], scaled up by a lake. It is also
> why "what latitude are you at?" is not enough to classify a climate —
> what is upwind of you matters too.

**6.** *(Invented numbers, real lesson.)* A student records annual
temperature anomalies, in °C, for two consecutive five-year periods.
Period 1: 0.12, 0.31, 0.05, 0.22, 0.40. Period 2: 0.38, 0.55, 0.29,
0.61, 0.47. Find the mean of each period and the difference. Then say
why the student should not report the warmest single year as evidence
of anything.

> [!success]- Answer 6
> **Period 1 mean:**
> $\frac{0.12 + 0.31 + 0.05 + 0.22 + 0.40}{5} = \frac{1.10}{5} = 0.22\ ^\circ\text{C}$
>
> **Period 2 mean:**
> $\frac{0.38 + 0.55 + 0.29 + 0.61 + 0.47}{5} = \frac{2.30}{5} = 0.46\ ^\circ\text{C}$
>
> **Difference:** $0.46 - 0.22 = 0.24\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
>
> Now the important comparison. Within period 1 alone, the years run
> from 0.05 to 0.40 — a spread of $0.35\ ^\circ\text{C}$, which is
> **larger than the difference between the two periods**. Year-to-year
> variation is bigger than the signal, so any single year is consistent
> with almost any story you like.
>
> That is exactly why climate is reported as averages over long periods
> rather than as individual years, and it is why picking the highest
> value in a series and calling it evidence is a technique rather than
> an argument. See [[Reading a Graph Honestly]].

**7.** A website headline says *"New study shows scientists were
wrong."* The article links to "the study". The link goes to a blog
post. The blog post links to a second blog post, which links back to
the first article. Nothing in the chain names a dataset, an author, or
a journal. What has gone wrong, and what would you do instead?

> [!success]- Answer 7
> **What has gone wrong:** the chain is circular. Each item borrows
> authority from the next, and at no point does anything touch a
> measurement. A claim can be repeated any number of times without
> becoming better supported — repetition is not corroboration, and
> "sources" that only cite each other are one source wearing several
> coats.
>
> Notice also what the chain is **missing**: no dataset, no named
> author with a stated affiliation, no journal, no date on the work
> itself, and no statement of what was measured or how.
>
> **What to do instead:** go up the chain until you reach something
> that reports its own measurements, and if the chain runs out before
> then, the claim has no support and you drop it. Then check it against
> a source of the right kind — the published records and reports of
> **Environment and Climate Change Canada**, the datasets of **NOAA**,
> or an **IPCC** assessment report, which summarises the published
> literature rather than resting on any one study. Read what the
> authors themselves say about the uncertainty, because that is the
> part the headline always drops.
>
> A useful habit: before quoting any number, be able to answer "who
> measured this, with what, and over what period?" If you cannot, the
> number does not go in your work.

**8.** Two claims. *(a) "It was the coldest February here in years, so
global warming is not happening."* *(b) "This graph starts in an
unusually warm year and shows no warming since, so the trend has
stopped."* Respond to each.

> [!success]- Answer 8
> **(a)** This confuses weather with climate. A trend is a statement
> about the distribution of conditions over decades and over the whole
> planet; one cold month in one place is a single draw from that
> distribution. A warming climate still produces cold months, just
> fewer of them and less extreme on average.
>
> The test that keeps you honest: would you accept the mirror-image
> argument? If a record-warm week is not proof of warming — and it is
> not — then a record-cold week is not disproof. Use the same standard
> in both directions or you are not applying a standard at all.
>
> **(b)** This is cherry-picking the start point. Choosing to begin at
> an unusually warm year makes the following stretch look flat while
> every number on the graph stays perfectly correct. That is what makes
> it effective and what makes it dishonest.
>
> The check is mechanical: **move the start point a few years each way,
> and do the same at the end.** If the story survives both, it is a
> trend. If it collapses, you were looking at where somebody chose to
> put the edge of the picture. And ask *why* the record was cut where
> it was — a series that stops early hides in exactly the same way as
> one that starts late.

**9.** Classify the climate of **your own region**, using a named system
rather than an impression — the **Ecoregions of Canada** framework and the
**Köppen–Geiger** classification are the two we use, and both publish the
criteria they classify on. State which system you used, which class your
region falls into, and the two or three measured quantities that decision
rests on. Then set your region beside **one other in Canada** and **one
outside it**, and say what is different and what is doing the differing.

> [!success]- Answer 9
> The answer is a method, not a place name, so check yours against these
> four moves rather than against a specific class.
>
> **Name the system first.** "Humid continental" means something precise
> under Köppen–Geiger — thresholds on the coldest month, the warmest
> month, and how the precipitation is spread through the year. "Cold and
> snowy" means nothing anybody can check.
>
> **Quote the quantities that decide it.** Long-term monthly means for
> temperature and precipitation, from the station nearest you, over a
> stated normal period. A classification without its numbers is a label
> you copied.
>
> **Compare on the quantity, not on the feeling.** A place in southern
> Ontario and a place on the coast of British Columbia can sit at nearly
> the same latitude and land in different classes, and the reason is
> almost always something structural: an ocean alongside, a mountain
> range upwind, a large lake, or an elevation difference. Name the
> mechanism, and remember that water moderates a climate for the same
> reason it warmed and cooled slowest on your bench in
> [[Modelling the Greenhouse Effect]].
>
> **Say what the classification cannot tell you.** It describes a
> long-term average, so it says nothing about any particular year, and a
> region can shift class over decades without a single day feeling
> different at the time.

Reference: [[Natural and Human Influences on Climate]],

will need all of this for [[The Climate Brief]].
