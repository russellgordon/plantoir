> [!abstract] At a glance
> Groups of four or five · six weeks, with a circle meeting every third
> class · one reading log and one written response · assessed on
> Knowledge, Thinking, and Communication

## The task

Choose one novel, read it with a group, and end up with something to say
about it that could not have been said by somebody who read a summary.

The four books are on [[Reading/index|the reading page]]:
[[The Marrow Thieves]], [[Indian Horse]], [[Fatty Legs]], and
[[The Breadwinner]]. Read the first page of each before you choose.

## How a circle runs

Your group sets the schedule in the first meeting and holds itself to it.
Each meeting, everybody arrives with:

- **One passage** you marked, and the reason. "It stuck and I do not know
  why" is a legitimate reason to start from.
- **One question** you cannot answer alone.
- **Your reading log** up to date.

Meetings are twenty minutes and they are not a quiz. I sit in on each
group twice and say nothing for the first ten minutes.

## The reading log

One entry per meeting, in your [[Reading and Writing Journal]]: what you
read, one passage with your note on it, one question, and one sentence
about what somebody else in the group said that you had not thought of.

That last line is the one that matters. A circle where nobody records
being changed by anybody is four people reading in the same room.

The entries that count are the ones written **in the meeting, in class**,
while the conversation is still in the room. Anything you add at home is
yours and is worth doing; it is not what I mark.

## The written response

Eight hundred words, at the end, on a question your group could not
settle. Not a book report — an argument, built the way
[[Writing About Texts]] describes: claim, evidence, explanation.

You may write on a question from your book's page, or one your group
invented. Invented ones are usually better and I will help you sharpen it.

## How it is marked

| Category | What I am looking for |
| --- | --- |
| Knowledge | You know the book — the whole book, including the parts that were work |
| Thinking | The response argues something, with evidence, and handles the strongest objection |
| Communication | A reader who has not read the novel can follow you; quotations are integrated, not dropped |

There is no group mark here and there never will be. Your circle sets the
schedule together and argues together, but the two things I evaluate —
your reading log and your written response — are yours alone, and the
mark is mine to determine from evidence. What your circle thinks of your
contribution is not part of it. See [[How Marks Work]].

> [!warning] The failure mode of literature circles
> One person reads and four people nod. It is obvious from the meetings
> within ten minutes, it is unfair to the reader, and it wastes six weeks
> for everybody else. Your log is how you show it did not happen here.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

A circle is four or five people and one conversation, and the mark
belongs to each of them on their own. Both prompts below are built to
pull them apart again: write down people, never circles.

OBSERVE — Unit 2, Days 4, 7, 11, 15 and 18: the five circle meetings, two
  circles a meeting
  One meeting at one circle is four or five students, so this is a
  unit-long sweep rather than an afternoon. Two circles a meeting gets you round
  every circle twice, which is what this task page promises them anyway.
  Day 4's agenda spells out the ten silent minutes; take the same ten at
  the other four.
  Watch for: whether a passage is OPENED — the book turned to the page
  and read from — or described from memory with the book shut. Then:
  whether anybody's question is taken up by somebody else, or five people
  ask five questions in turn and none of them is answered.
  Going well: someone says "wait, go back" and the circle goes back. A
  question that gets a second question rather than an opinion.
  Stuck: five turns taken, five views offered, nobody's sentence changed
  by anybody else's.
  The reading log will tell you what each student says somebody else
  changed for them — so this is corroboration rather than news, and that
  is exactly what makes it worth ten minutes: you are checking whether
  the sentence written that day matches what happened in front of you.
  Record: your circle list with two tick columns, "opened a passage" and
  "took up somebody else's question", and the date. Nine or ten students
  a meeting is the whole class twice over by Day 18.
  That is B1.1 watched — paraphrasing, asking open-ended questions,
  making relevant responses and reflecting on what has been said. It is a
  listening expectation, and an eight-hundred-word response cannot show
  you listening at all.

TALK — Unit 2, Day 19, the question-sharpening already on that agenda,
  one student at a time
  Ask: "Point me at the page where you first suspected this, and the page
  where you were sure."
  Then: "If your book had stopped three chapters earlier, what would the
  ending have meant instead?"
  A strong first answer finds two places unaided and says what happened
  between them: that is C3.2, local and global inference from explicit
  and implicit evidence, and the written response shows the conclusion
  without showing whether the student can still find it in the book. A
  strong second answer holds the whole book at once and swaps one part
  out — C2.6, synthesis and conclusions — and it is the question a
  summary cannot survive, which is the thing this task is most often
  faked with.
  Record: one line per STUDENT, never per circle, in the same list.

The product evidence is the reading log and the written response, handed
in on Day 22.
