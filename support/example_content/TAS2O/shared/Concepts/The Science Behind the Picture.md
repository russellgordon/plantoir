Every choice on a camera or a mixer is a physics decision somebody
already made for you. Knowing the science behind them is what turns the
settings from superstition into control.

## Light, lenses, and why a photograph looks the way it does

A lens bends light so that rays from one point in the world meet again
at one point on the sensor. Three consequences follow, and they explain
most of what a photograph does:

| What you change | The physics | What you see |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Focal length** | A longer lens has a narrower angle of view | Distant subjects fill the frame; backgrounds appear compressed and closer |
| **Aperture** | A wider opening admits more light and has a shallower depth of field | The background falls out of focus; less of the scene is sharp |
| **Shutter time** | The sensor collects light for longer | Motion blurs; a shorter time freezes it |
| **Sensor size** | A larger sensor gathers more light per pixel | Less noise in a dark gym |

**Depth of field** is not a style setting; it is geometry. Only one
distance is truly in focus, and the range that *looks* acceptably sharp
widens as the aperture narrows and as the subject gets further away.

## Colour, and why white is not white

Light sources differ in **colour temperature**, measured in kelvin:
candlelight around 2000 K, indoor bulbs 2700–3000 K, midday daylight
about 5500 K, shade higher still. Your eye adapts and stops noticing;
the camera does not. **White balance** tells the camera what the light
was, so that a sheet of paper comes out white rather than orange or
blue.

Mixed lighting is the everyday version of this problem: a gym with
daylight from high windows and sodium lamps overhead has two colour
temperatures at once, and no single white balance fixes both.

## Sound is a wave, and rooms interfere with it

Sound is a pressure wave. Frequency (in hertz) is pitch; amplitude is
loudness, measured in decibels on a **logarithmic** scale — which is
why 60 dB is not "twice" 30 dB but a thousand times the power.

Three room effects decide whether your audio is usable:

- **Reverberation**: sound reflecting off hard surfaces and arriving
  late. It is why a tiled hallway is the worst interview location in
  any school.
- **The inverse square law**: doubling the microphone's distance from
  a speaker cuts the sound power to a quarter. This is the single most
  effective audio fix available to you, and it is free.
- **Background noise**: ventilation, projectors, corridors. Record ten
  seconds of it deliberately — "room tone" — because your editor will
  need it.

## Screens, print, and why the colours differ

A screen **adds** light: red, green, and blue emitters combine toward
white. Ink **subtracts** it: cyan, magenta, yellow, and black absorb
wavelengths, and the paper supplies what is left. That is why a colour
that glows on a monitor can print muddy, and why
[[The Front Page]] gets a proof before it goes to the printer.

> [!tip] The one to carry into every shoot
> If it is too dark, the physics offers you exactly three moves: more
> light, a wider aperture, or a longer shutter. Every one of them costs
> you something — control, depth of field, or sharpness. Choosing which
> to spend is the job.
