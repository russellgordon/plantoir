Every landscape is a record of the processes that made it, and most of
them are still running. Reading one means moving from what you can see to
what must have happened — an inference, held with the confidence the
evidence actually supports.

## The sequence in southern Ontario

Ancient bedrock, laid down or intruded hundreds of millions of years
ago, sets what is available to erode. Continental ice sheets then
advanced and retreated repeatedly through the most recent glaciation,
scraping, dragging and finally dumping their load. Meltwater sorted some
of that load and cut channels far larger than the streams now occupying
them. Since the ice left, rivers, waves, frost and vegetation have been
adjusting the result — the phase you are standing in.

Glacial evidence is the most legible layer. Till plains and drumlins
record ice moving over ground; moraines record where an ice margin
stalled; eskers and outwash record meltwater; erratics record transport
of a rock far from its own bedrock. A boulder of Shield granite sitting
on limestone did not walk there.

## A field routine

Work down this list in order, in your notebook, before you interpret
anything:

- [ ] Relief — how much height change, over what distance
- [ ] Slope form — straight, convex, concave, terraced, benched
- [ ] Surface material — bedrock, till, sand, silt, organic
- [ ] Drainage — where water would go, and where it clearly has gone
- [ ] Vegetation — what grows, and what the pattern of it follows
- [ ] Human marks — cuts, fills, drains, walls, fences, roads

> [!tip] Tick it here, carry it there
> Tick the boxes as you read the landscape; your browser keeps the ticks
> on this computer. In the field, the copy in your field notebook is the
> one that is any use.

Then ask the two questions that turn observation into geography: what is
the process, and is it still operating? A gully with bare, fresh sides is
active. The same gully floored with grass and leaf litter is not, and the
difference is visible in ninety seconds.

Landforms change on timescales from a storm to an ice age, so a landscape
holds evidence at several speeds at once — the point developed in
[[Weathering, Erosion, and Deposition]] and stretched to geologic time in
[[Plate Tectonics and What It Builds]]. You will do this for real in
[[The Woodlot Study]] and write it up as [[The Process Study]].
