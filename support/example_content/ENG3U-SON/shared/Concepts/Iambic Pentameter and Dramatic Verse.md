Some of [[Hamlet]]'s characters speak in a steady, almost musical rhythm.
Others — usually the comic characters, the servants, anyone speaking
casually — just talk in ordinary prose. That is not decoration.
Shakespeare uses the switch between verse and prose to tell you something
about who is speaking, and how seriously the moment is meant to land.
This page gives you the vocabulary to hear it.

## Iambic pentameter

An **iamb** is a unit of rhythm — a "foot" — made of one unstressed
syllable followed by one stressed syllable: da-**DUM**. **Pentameter**
means five of those feet in a line, which gives ten syllables total,
alternating unstressed and stressed:

*to BE, or NOT to BE, that IS the QUES-tion*

Read it aloud, leaning into the capitalised syllables, and you can hear
the pattern holding the line together — five beats, steady, almost like a
heartbeat.

## Why it matters when the pattern breaks

Shakespeare does not write a play entirely in perfect iambic pentameter
and leave it at that. He uses the pattern as a baseline, and then breaks
it deliberately: a short line, an extra unstressed syllable, a line split
between two speakers. A broken line is usually a signal — of
interruption, of emotional strain, of a character who cannot hold the
composed rhythm a formal moment calls for. Once you can hear the regular
pattern, the irregular ones start to stand out on their own.

## Scanning a line: the steps

Use the following to work through a line of dialogue on the page. This is
a process to work through in your notebook, not something you tick off on
the site — read it top to bottom before you start marking a line.

- [ ] Read the line aloud once, at a natural pace, before marking anything.
- [ ] Count the syllables. Ten usually means straightforward pentameter.
- [ ] Mark which syllables naturally take stress when spoken aloud.
- [ ] Check whether the stresses fall in an unstressed-stressed pattern.
- [ ] If the pattern breaks, ask what in the line's content might explain
      the break — interruption, strong emotion, a name that will not fit.

## Where this connects

Verse and prose sit alongside meaning throughout the play: watch for who
speaks in verse and who does not, and watch especially for a character
who usually speaks in verse dropping into prose — that shift is worth
pausing on.
