Point a narrow beam at a mirror and it comes off somewhere. The
question is whether "somewhere" is predictable, and if it is, what the
rule is expressed in terms of.

## What you are trying to find out

Two claims are on the table today and you are going to test both.

1. **There is a relationship between where a ray arrives and where it
   leaves**, and it can be written as an equation between two measured
   angles.
2. **The image you see in a mirror has definite characteristics** —
   somewhere it is, some size, some orientation — and those can be
   predicted before you look, then checked.

The second claim is the harder one, because "the image is behind the
mirror" is a strange thing to say about a place where no light ever
goes.

## What you have to work with

- A plane mirror and a stand, or a mirror strip on a block
- A ray box with a single-slit mask, or the low-power laser at the
  demonstration station
- Protractor, ruler, **set square**, sharp pencil, plain paper
- Two long pins and a cork board, for locating the image
- A concave and a convex mirror, and a small candle-shaped lamp on a
  stand, at the far bench

Decide and record before you begin:

- **Which angles you will test, and how many.** Three points can be
  joined by a line whatever the relationship is. Decide how many you
  need to distinguish "proportional" from "equal", and spread them out
  — small angles hide the disagreements that large angles expose.
- **How you will construct the normal.** This is the single largest
  source of error in the whole investigation and it is entirely under
  your control.
- **How you will locate the image** in the plane mirror, given that you
  cannot put a screen where it appears to be.

> [!note]- Two ways to draw a normal, and only one is any use
> **By eye.** Fast, and wrong by a degree or two, and — this is the
> part that matters — wrong in the *same direction* for every single
> reading you take. An error that repeats itself is not averaged away
> by more trials, and it grows most obvious at large angles.
>
> **With a set square**, standing on the mirror line at the exact point
> where the ray strikes. Slower, and correct. Mark the strike point
> first, then the normal, then the incident and reflected rays.
>
> If your results drift steadily worse as the angle grows, come back
> and read this again. The pattern is a fingerprint.

## Safety

> [!danger] Light sources and mirror edges
> - **Never aim a laser at any person, at any part of any person, or
>   along a line where an eye could be.** Only the class-2 pointer at
>   the supervised station, only at bench level, and only with the beam
>   ending on a card. You do not look into it, and you do not look at
>   the bright spot where it lands on a mirror.
> - **A mirror sends a beam somewhere you were not expecting.** Before
>   switching on, look at where the reflected beam will go and put a
>   card there. Take off watches, rings, and anything shiny on your
>   wrists — a reflection off a watch face travels the width of a room.
> - **Ray box lamps get hot.** The housing, the slit mask, and the
>   filament stay hot after switch-off. Move the box by its base, and
>   keep paper from resting against the lamp housing.
> - Mirror strips and glass blocks **chip**. Handle by the edges, check
>   for chips before use, and report a chipped or cracked one rather
>   than working with it.
> - Pins are sharp and end up point-upward on a bench. Count them out
>   and count them back into the container before you leave.
> - Lights in the room will be dimmed. Bags under benches, stools
>   pushed in, and nobody moving quickly.

## The prediction you write first

In your journal, before the first measurement:

1. **An equation.** Write the relationship you expect between the angle
   of incidence and the angle of reflection, using symbols, and say
   which line each angle is measured from. If you write it from the
   mirror surface rather than the normal, keep it — comparing the two
   conventions afterwards is worth doing.
2. **Four characteristics of the image in a plane mirror**: where it
   is, how big, which way up, and whether you could catch it on a
   screen. Commit to all four.
3. **Concave and convex**, qualitatively: for an object well away from
   a concave mirror, and for the same object in front of a convex one,
   predict orientation and relative size.

## What to collect

Angles, measured from the normal, in degrees:

| Trial | $\theta_i$ (°) | $\theta_r$ (°) | $\theta_r - \theta_i$ (°) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | | | |
| 2 | | | |
| 3 | | | |
| 4 | | | |
| 5 | | | |

Then plot $\theta_r$ on the vertical axis against $\theta_i$ on the
horizontal, and draw the best-fit line your data actually supports.
Report the **slope** and the **intercept**, both with units where units
apply. A relationship of the form

$$\theta_r = \theta_i$$

predicts a slope of exactly one and an intercept of exactly zero, and
those are two separate things your graph can check independently. Do
not force the line through the origin; an intercept you did not expect
is information about your normal.

Image location in the plane mirror: record the object distance and the
image distance you found, in centimetres, with the resolution of your
ruler stated. For the curved mirrors, record what you observed against
what you predicted, characteristic by characteristic.

Ray diagrams to the conventions in [[Drawing Scientific Diagrams]] —
normals dashed and perpendicular, arrowheads on every ray, and virtual
rays dashed because the light does not really go there.

## What to bring to the consolidation discussion

- Your graph, its slope, and its intercept.
- The largest disagreement in your table, in degrees, next to the
  smallest angle your protractor can resolve. Only one of those two
  numbers is an error.
- Whether your disagreements are **random** — scattered both ways — or
  **systematic** — leaning one way and growing with angle. Say which,
  and what each would mean.
- Your four predicted image characteristics, with a tick or a cross
  against each.

## What you should not claim

- **A protractor read to the nearest degree cannot support a claim
  about half a degree.** Quote your uncertainty and compare your
  disagreement against it before calling anything a discrepancy.
- **A beam has width.** You measured to the centre of a band of light
  a millimetre or two across, and where you judged that centre to be is
  part of your result.
- **One mirror is not all surfaces.** Your data says nothing yet about
  a rough wall, water, or polished metal. That the rule generalises is
  a hypothesis you have not tested.
- **Equal angles is not the whole law.** You should also be able to say
  that the incident ray, the reflected ray, and the normal all lie in
  one plane — and you should notice that your flat sheet of paper made
  that impossible to test, because it assumed it.
