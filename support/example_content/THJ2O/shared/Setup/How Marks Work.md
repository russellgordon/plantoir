You are marked on what you can grow and build, on the safe practices you
demonstrate, and on how well you can account for your choices — not on how
much you already knew about plants at the start of the course.

## The seventy and the thirty

Every Grade 10 credit in Ontario is built the same way. **Seventy per cent**
of your mark comes from work spread across the whole semester, and it leans
towards your **most recent and most consistent** work rather than averaging
the start of the course against the end of it. That matters in a practical
course more than most: the joint you cut in week three is not the joint you
can cut in week fifteen, and the mark should say so.

The seventy is the assessed tasks and the records behind them. Roughly
in the order you meet them: [[The Plant Identification File]] and
[[The Growing Trial]]; [[The Safety Ticket]] and [[The Shop Build]];
[[The Propagation Bench]], [[The Production Run]], and
[[The Pest and Disease Report]]; and then [[The Site Project]], which is
worth several of the smaller ones on its own because it runs for eight
periods and asks for everything at once. They are not equally weighted.
Ask me for the exact split any time you want it. It is not a secret; it
is just not the number that changes what you do on Monday.

**Thirty per cent** is the final evaluation, at the end:
[[The Evidence Portfolio]], plus a practical demonstration in the
examination period where you set up a task, choose and check the tools,
work safely, and explain what you are doing while you do it. It is not
an essay, and it is not a fifth unit test — it reaches back across the
whole semester on purpose.

## Four kinds of thinking, not one

Every task asks for more than one of these, and the balance shifts with
the task. A bed that looks right is never the whole of it.

| What is being judged | What it means here |
| --- | --- |
| What you know | Species, parts, processes, tools, hazards, the law |
| How you think | Diagnosing a sick plant, finding the bottleneck, judging a source |
| How you communicate | Drawings, records, plant lists, a handover somebody can follow |
| Where you apply it | Doing it on a site nobody walked you through first |

The fourth is why [[The Site Project]] exists. Anyone can repeat the job
we did together on the bench; the course is asking whether you can
survey a real bank, plan for it, and build something that is still there
in five years.

## Three kinds of evidence, and two of them are not paper

**What you make** is the obvious one: the file, the trial, the object,
the batch, the planting, the records. **What I watch you do** is the
second, and in a shop-and-site course it is enormous — how you hold the
tool, the order you work in, what you do with a cutting that wilts,
whether you wait for the blade. None of that survives into the finished
job, which is exactly why I am walking around with a clipboard while you
work.

**What you tell me** is the third. The conferences on the class pages
are not progress checks; they are evidence in their own right. Some of
what you understand will only ever show up when you say it out loud
beside the thing you are working on.

A student whose build is behind can still give me the best two minutes
of the period, and those two minutes count. That is not a consolation
prize. It is one of the three sources the mark is built from.

## Safety is not a deduction

This is the part that surprises people. **A safety failure is not marked
down. It is a stop-work.** The job halts, we deal with it, and you
demonstrate the skill safely before being signed off —
[[Our Working Agreement]]. That is how a real site runs.

Safety still carries marks, and that is not a contradiction: knowing the
hazards, selecting the protection, running the pre-start check, and
keeping the records are curriculum expectations in this course, assessed
through [[The Safety Ticket]]. What is marked is the practice, not how
careful a person you seem.

## What earns a high mark here

- **The work stands up.** A square joint, a planting that establishes, a
  bed that drains. Outdoor work is marked by whether it survives.
- **You can account for your choices** — why that fastener, spacing,
  species, or method. See
  [[Design and Problem Solving in the Green Industries]].
- **Your numbers are right**, checked before you order rather than after.
- **The record is current and honest**, including what went wrong —
  [[Writing a Work Record]] and [[The Evidence File]].
- **Your own part is visible.** Several tasks are done in pairs or
  groups, and every one of them names the piece that is yours alone —
  your calculations, your section of the work, your log, your answers at
  the conference. There is no shared mark in this course.

Practical skills have to be demonstrated, so missed periods get made up
rather than averaged away. Tell me early and we will find the time.

## What is not in your mark

How you work is reported separately, on the same report card, as **E,
G, S or N** — six habits: responsibility, organization, independent
work, collaboration, initiative, and self-regulation. They matter,
employers ask about them, and they do not move your percentage. The mark
is about the work; that column is about the worker, and mixing the two
tells you less about both.

There is one exception here, and it is written into the curriculum
rather than invented by me. This course is required to teach the
Essential Skills and the work habits the green industries actually run
on, so when you set those out with evidence in
[[The Evidence Portfolio]] — using [[Your Skills Record]] — that is
marked like anything else in the curriculum. Read the expectation
closely, though, because it is narrower than it looks: it asks you to
show that you understand **which** skills and habits this industry runs
on, and **why** each one matters in this work. Being organised is not
worth marks. Nor is your own rating of how organised you are. Knowing
what organisation is FOR on a site, and being able to say it, is.

Your own judgement of your work, and your classmates', are not part of
your mark either. You will judge your own work against the criteria
often — see [[Judging Your Own Work]] — because it is the fastest way to
get better, and a partner reading your entry back to you is worth having
for the same reason. Neither becomes a number. The mark is mine to
determine, from evidence.

Records are marked from what you write **in class, at the bench or on
the site, on the day the work happened** — which is why every working
period ends with cleaning, storing, and recording, and why the ten
minutes for it belongs to you. Tidying an entry up afterwards is fine
and is not the thing I am marking.

The "before our next class" list is not marked either. It holds three
kinds of thing and no fourth: reading or practice that gets you ready
for the next period; a check that something done in class actually got
written down; and a reminder to bring in or hand in a piece of work the
class periods were for. Doing the practice is never itself worth marks.
And that list never stands in for the periods: every assessed task on
this course is built in class, with me in the room and the tools to
hand — check the schedule and you will find the days. What you add to a
file or a log between classes is your own collecting and tidying.

Every task page lists its criteria before you start, in plain words. If
a mark ever surprises you, ask: those criteria are the whole story, and
[[Getting Help]] lists the ways to reach me.
