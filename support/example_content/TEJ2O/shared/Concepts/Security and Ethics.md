Somewhere in [[Install an Operating System]] you chose a password,
probably without ceremony. That moment was security — the real thing,
not the movie version. Most of protecting a system happens at exactly
that level.

## Three doors into a system

A bench machine can be entered three ways: through what someone knows
(a password), through a flaw in its software (which updates exist to
close), or through its hands-on presence (whoever physically holds a
machine can, with time, get at its data). Real protection covers all
three doors, which is why strong passwords, prompt updates, and a
locked room are one habit, not three. The same thinking, scaled up,
is how the network you joined in [[Build a Small Network]] decides
who may see what.

## Habits that hold the doors

Use a passphrase long enough to be yours alone, and never lend it —
a shared password has no owner. Apply updates when offered; each one
closes doors already known to attackers. Lock the screen when you
step away, even at the bench, even for a minute. And treat other
people's data on a shared machine the way you would want yours
treated — the school's acceptable-use policy is the written form of
that, the same way [[Our Classroom Norms]] is for the room.

> [!warning] Skill is not permission
> This course teaches you how systems open. Knowing how a door works
> is never a licence to walk through it. Accessing an account,
> machine, or network you were not given is a breach of trust first,
> a policy violation second, and in many cases a crime third — and
> "just to see if I could" has never once made it otherwise.

## Your own identity is a system too

Machines are not the only thing with a way in. Information about a
person — name, birthday, address, the school you attend, the answers
to security questions — is worth money to somebody, and identity theft
is assembled out of small true details rather than stolen in one
piece. So: check what a privacy policy says a service will do with what
you give it before you give it; assume anything sent over a network can
be read unless it was encrypted; and treat a request for personal
details, however friendly, the way a technician treats an unlabelled
cable. Harassment that follows a person from account to account is the
same failure seen from the other side — it works because the details
were available. Report it rather than arguing with it — to the
platform, to an adult you trust, or to both.

## The ethics of access

Break-ins are not the whole story. Ethical computer use also means
not passing off found text or code as your own, not copying software
or media you have no licence for, and not posting what demeans
another person. The costs of the hacking that gets celebrated are
carried by other people — lost data, corrupted records, broken trust.
Where exactly the line sits between security research and intrusion
is genuinely arguable, and [[Locked Down or Wide Open]] is the place
this course argues it.
