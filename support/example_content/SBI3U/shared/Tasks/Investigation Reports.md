> [!abstract] At a glance
> **Format:** five write-ups spread across the semester, each done at the
> bench in a period set aside for it. **Individually**, from data your
> group collected. **Assessed on:** Thinking and Investigation,
> Communication, and Knowledge where the biology has to be explained.

Five of this course's investigations are written up properly, and the
five write-ups are marked. They are the part of your seventy per cent
that arrives steadily rather than in four large pieces — which is what
lets a report card say something about your most recent and most
consistent work rather than about four afternoons.

## The five, and when they are written

| Write-up | Investigation | Written in class on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One | [[Microscopy of Microorganisms]] | Unit 1, Day 5 |
| Two | [[Comparative Anatomy]] | Unit 2, Day 2 |
| Three | [[A Monohybrid Cross]] | Unit 3, Day 11 |
| Four | [[Measuring Photosynthesis]] | Unit 5, Day 4 |
| Five | [[Transpiration]] | Unit 5, Day 8 |

Each investigation page carries its own question, method and safety
notes, and says what its analysis has to answer. The shape of the report
itself is on [[Writing a Lab Report]]; the drawing conventions are on
[[Biological Drawing]].

## Written in class, and why

Every one of these is written at the bench, in a period set aside for it,
with your data and your notes in front of you and me in the room. That is
deliberate. A report written at home three days later is a report about
what you can remember, and the thing worth marking — what you made of the
numbers while they were still strange — has gone by then.

Four of the five are written in the period straight after the
investigation. [[Transpiration]] is the exception: it is run on Unit 5,
Day 5 and written on Day 8, because one potometer is one condition and
the report needs the whole class's data pooled before there is anything
to compare.

It also means the help is available while you need it. Ask.

## Individually, from shared data

The bench work is done in pairs or threes and the raw data is the
group's. **The write-up is yours alone**, from the analysis onward. Two
people may hand in the same table; two people handing in the same
analysis have handed in one piece of work.

## Success criteria

The same six rows every time, so that by January you can run them from
memory. Use them with [[Judging Your Own Work]] before you hand a report
in — the criteria do not change, which is exactly what makes improvement
across five reports visible.

| Quality | What it looks like in your write-up |
| --- | --- |
| Observations kept clean | Tables and graphs, plus the note that the third leaf disc never floated. No interpretation, and nothing quietly dropped |
| Data recorded so somebody else could use it | Units on every column, the raw counts kept beside anything you calculated from them |
| Diagrams to the conventions | Title, magnification where it applies, ruled label lines that do not cross |
| Analysis that reads the numbers | The calculation shown once in full, the graph read for its trend and where it flattens, and the result set against what the biology predicted |
| Variation and error named honestly | How much the living material varied, where the method could have misled you, and one improvement that would actually reduce it |
| A conclusion the data allows | One paragraph answering the purpose, with the uncertainty in it — including "nothing I can claim" where that is the truth |

The fifth row is the one that separates a Grade 11 report from a Grade 10
one. "There may have been experimental error" is a sentence, not a
finding; naming the step that could have gone wrong, and saying how much
it could have moved the number, is a finding.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

These five are the steadiest evidence in the course, and they are also
the cheapest place to gather the other two kinds: ask on a writing day,
when the data is in front of them, and watch on a bench day, when the
data is being made. Unit 5 puts one of each back to back, which is why
both prompts below live there — Day 4 and Day 5, one period apart.

OBSERVE — Unit 5, Day 5, the potometer runs
  Watch for: what happens to a run that goes wrong. A bubble that races
  away, a leaky joint, a reading taken while somebody was still
  adjusting the clamp. The write-up shows you the numbers that survived,
  and a discarded run leaves no trace in it at all.
  Going well: the bad run written down and labelled as bad, and the
  apparatus reset before the next one.
  Stuck: a quiet restart, and four tidy readings that all agree.
  Record: one tick per bench — kept the bad run, or did not. It takes
  the length of one walk around the room, and it is the single best
  predictor of whose fifth row will be honest.

TALK — Unit 5, Day 4, while they write up Measuring Photosynthesis
  The agenda has already put "how repeatable was your rate, really?" in
  front of them, so that question is spent. Sit down with the graph and
  go underneath it.
  Ask: "Your slowest disc and your fastest one — how far apart are they,
  and is that gap bigger or smaller than the difference you are claiming
  between your light levels?"
  Then: "If you ran the whole thing again tomorrow, which of your numbers
  would come out closest, and which would not?"
  A strong answer treats the scatter as information about the method
  rather than as noise to apologise for, and can say which step it came
  from. That is A1.8 heard — evidence weighed against the prediction,
  with the sources of error named — and a report can contain a paragraph
  about uncertainty without any of that thinking having happened.
  Record: three columns — scatter explained, scatter apologised for, not
  noticed. Carry the third column into Day 8.

The product evidence is the five write-ups themselves, handed in at the
end of each writing period.
