**Built through Unit 1, handed in at the end of it.** Individual.

Twenty plants you can identify on sight, from our own woodlot, shoreline,
and school grounds — with the evidence that you actually looked.

## What goes in the file

For each plant:

| Field | What it needs |
| --- | --- |
| Common name | The one people actually use |
| Botanical name | Genus and species, spelled correctly |
| Group | Tree, shrub, herbaceous perennial, annual, grass, vine |
| Distinguishing features | What tells it apart from the plant beside it |
| Where you found it | Site, date, and roughly where |
| Evidence | Your own photograph or a pressed, labelled sample |

At least eight from the woodlot, four from the shoreline, and four from
the school grounds.

## What is marked

- **Accuracy** — the identification is right, and the features you name
  are the ones that actually distinguish it.
- **Vocabulary** — the correct terms for leaf arrangement, margin, bark,
  and habit, used properly.
- **Your own evidence** — photographs and samples you collected, dated.
- **Care** — nothing rare or protected was picked, and you took only
  what you needed.

> [!warning] Collect responsibly, and know what you are touching
> Take a photograph rather than a sample where you can. Never collect
> from a protected area or a private site without permission, and learn
> poison ivy and wild parsnip before the first woodlot visit — see
> [[Hazards on a Green Industry Site]].

[[What Makes a Plant a Plant]] and [[The Parts and What They Do]] are the
vocabulary; the file is what proves you can use it in the field.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 1, Day 5, identification in the woodlot
  Watch which end of the entry a student starts from. A1.1 is about
  plant GROUPS — tree, shrub, perennial, grass, vine — so the moment it
  is visible is when a student works out what KIND of thing is in front
  of them from the thing itself. Look the name up first and fill the
  group column in afterwards to match it, and Day 17 cannot tell the
  difference.
  Watch for: a hand on the twig — bud arrangement counted, bark felt,
  leaf turned — before anything is written down.
  Going well: three features noticed, then the guide opened to confirm.
  Stuck: photograph, name, next plant, in under thirty seconds.
  Record: on the class list clipped to your day plan, a stroke against
  anyone who worked from the plant and a circle against anyone who
  worked from a name. The circles are who to stand beside on Day 16.

TALK — Unit 1, Day 15, at the conferences on entries so far
  The file already asks in writing what distinguishes each plant, so
  they have that one prepared. Ask past it.
  Ask: "Which of your twenty would you get wrong in January, with the
  leaves off it?"
  Then: "Describe that one to me out loud, the way you would to a
  supplier on the phone who cannot see it."
  A strong answer reaches for bark, bud, and habit without groping, and
  does not fall back on "the pointy one". That is A4.1, which asks for
  the terminology used correctly in ORAL as well as written
  communication — and a written entry can be copied off a field guide in
  a way a spoken description cannot.
  Record: one line each on the conference sheet, and circle the term
  they reached for and missed.

The product evidence is the file handed in on Day 17.
