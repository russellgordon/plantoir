You met this before it had a name — the first [[News or Not]] round,
when the room argued about whether a new vending machine beats a
retiring caretaker of thirty years. That argument was the whole
discipline: **news judgement**, deciding what deserves your readers'
limited attention, and being able to say why.

## The seven tests

Editors everywhere weigh a story against roughly the same questions:

| Test | The question |
| --- | --- |
| Timeliness | Did it just happen — or is it about to? |
| Proximity | Is it close to OUR readers? |
| Impact | How many people does it actually affect? |
| Prominence | Are the people involved widely known here? |
| Conflict | Are interests genuinely pulling against each other? |
| Novelty | Is it a first, a last, a rarity? |
| Human interest | Does it make a reader feel something true? |

No story needs all seven — but a story with none of them is not a
story, however much its writer enjoyed it. The retiring caretaker
carries proximity, prominence, and human interest; that is why he
beats the vending machine.

> [!tip] The reader test beats every list
> "Would someone at this school stop scrolling for this?" is the seven
> tests compressed into one honest question — and it is the question
> behind every [[One-Minute Pitch]].

News values are also an ethical instrument: what we choose to cover
decides who gets seen. A newsroom that only covers championships
misses [[Whose Story Is It|most of the school's actual life]] — which
is why [[The Athletics Package|the athletics beat]] is never the only
one this newsroom walks.
