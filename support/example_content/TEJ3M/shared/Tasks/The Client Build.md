> [!abstract] At a glance
> Benches of two · six periods across Unit 4 · one working system, one
> client handover package · assessed on all four categories

## What you are making

A small business hires your bench. They need a workstation built to a
brief, a peripheral installed, a home-office network that reaches the
internet safely, and documentation good enough that the next
technician — who will not be you — can take over.

This is the task where everything from Unit 4 arrives at once, and it
is deliberately more like a job than like a school assignment: the
client changes their mind once, partway through, and you handle it.

## What you deliver

1. **The machine**, built to the brief in [[Build a Machine to Spec]],
   posting, updated, and benchmarked against each requirement.
2. **The operating system**, chosen with a written reason from
   [[Operating Systems]], installed, patched, and configured with
   separate administrator and daily-use accounts.
3. **A peripheral**, installed, driver-configured, and demonstrated
   working.
4. **The home-office setup**: the workstation, the peripheral, and the
   client's existing laptop sharing what they need to share and nothing
   else.
5. **The network drawing**, to the standard in
   [[Network Types and Topologies]] — every device, address, port, and
   run, drawn as built rather than as planned.
6. **The security section**, per [[Securing a Network]]: wireless
   protection, firewall rules and why, accounts, what is encrypted, and
   where the backups go.
7. **A maintenance plan**: what to run, how often, and how to restore —
   with one restore already tested and timed.
8. **The client paperwork**: a plain-language acceptable-use note for
   the household or business, and a licence record for every piece of
   software installed. [[Licensing, Policy, and Privacy]] is the
   reference.

## The handover

Another bench receives your package and, without speaking to you,
answers three service questions from it: what is the administrator
account, where does the backup go and how is it restored, and which
port on the switch serves the printer. If they cannot, the job is not
finished — however well the system runs.

## How it is marked

| Category | What I am looking for |
| --- | --- |
| Knowledge | Parts, settings, and services chosen correctly for the stated requirements |
| Thinking | Faults diagnosed in order and documented; the mid-project change absorbed without a rebuild |
| Application | It works, and each requirement has a measurement rather than an assurance |
| Communication | A handover package a stranger can act on, in the client's language rather than yours |

Two people share a bench and neither shares a mark. Yours comes from the
parts of the package you wrote and can account for — name yourself on
each section — from your own [[Tech Journal]] entries across the six
periods, and from the conference on the first day. If the receiving
bench cannot find something on Day 6, the question is whose section it
was in.

Judge the package against the criteria below before it leaves your
hands, not after: [[Judging Your Own Work]] sets out the routine, and
there is a period left afterwards to fix whatever it finds.

## Success criteria

The table above says which of the four categories a judgement falls
under; this one says what I will be looking at when I make it. Where
they seem to disagree, this is the one to work from — it is the one you
can check for yourself.

| Quality | What it looks like at your bench |
| --- | --- |
| A machine that meets the brief | Every stated requirement has a benchmark number beside it, not an assurance |
| Choices with reasons | The operating system, the parts, and the firmware settings each have a written why |
| A drawing of what exists | Devices, addresses, ports and runs match the cables in front of you |
| Security you can explain | Each rule names the thing it stops, not the box it was ticked in |
| A restore that has been done | The maintenance plan carries a real elapsed time from a real restore |
| Licences accounted for | Every installed piece of software has a licence line a client could show an auditor |
| A package a stranger can act on | Another bench answers the three service questions without asking you |

> [!tip] Where marks are usually lost
> Not on the build. On the documentation written at the end from
> memory, which is always thinner than the notes you would have had if
> you had written as you went. [[Documenting Your Build]] exists for
> exactly this, and the benches that use it finish earlier.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 19, the restore, in the body of the period
  (the last fifteen minutes are the self-check, so take this before it)
  The agenda asks for "one restore tested and timed", and the plan they
  hand in will carry a number of minutes. The number cannot tell you
  whether anything was ever actually broken. Watching is what
  corroborates a figure the product reports and cannot support on its
  own.
  Watch for: does something get destroyed on purpose before the restore
  runs, and is the thing checked afterwards to see it came back?
  Going well: a file or a partition is wrecked deliberately, somebody
  looks at a clock as the restore starts, and the bench opens the
  restored thing to confirm it works.
  Stuck: a backup is run, nothing is broken, nothing is restored, and
  the plan says "about twenty minutes".
  Record: one column on your day plan — broke it, or guessed. That is
  B2.2 seen being used rather than claimed.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 15, the conference already on that agenda
  Do not ask them what the licence types are. That table is printed on
  Licensing, Policy, and Privacy and was read one class before this
  conference; they will give it back to you.
  Ask: "Eighteen months from now somebody asks this client to prove
  every piece of software on that machine is licensed. What do they
  open, and what is in it?"
  Then: "Your acceptable-use note — say four lines that will be in it,
  and name who in that household has to agree to it before any of them
  mean anything."
  The first is D2.2's *comply* clause rather than its vocabulary: a
  strong answer describes an artefact — product, proof of purchase, seat
  count, date, and where the receipt actually lives — and knows it has
  to survive the technician leaving. The second is D2.1, which asks what
  an acceptable-use policy is made OF; the deliverable proves a note
  exists, and only the answer tells you whether they know why each line
  is in it or downloaded a template.
  Record: two ticks per bench, licence and policy, on the same sheet you
  are already carrying for the conference.

The product evidence is the working system and the handover package,
received by another bench and handed in on Day 20.
