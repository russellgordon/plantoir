Every task page carries its success criteria, published before you start.
Most people read them once, on the day the task is handed out, and never
again — and then hand in work that misses two rows they could have fixed in
twenty minutes. This page is the twenty minutes.

We do it together the first time, out loud, on a report nobody in the room
has to defend — so that the move is not new to you on the day it is your own
work being judged.

## The routine

1. Open the task page and put the criteria beside your work.
2. Go row by row. For each one write **yes**, **not yet**, or **partly** —
   and one sentence of evidence: the paragraph, the graph, the number a
   reader would land on. "Yes" with nothing beside it is a wish.
3. Find your weakest row. Not the easiest to fix — the weakest.
4. Write one sentence saying what you will change.
5. Change it, in the time that is left. This is why a self-check is never
   the last thing you do before handing something in — sometimes it is the
   period before, sometimes it is the first ten minutes of a period whose
   rest is yours to fix things in. A judgement you have no time to act on is
   only a worry.

Steps 2 and 3 are the whole exercise. Naming your weakest row honestly is
harder than the fixing, because it means saying out loud the thing you were
hoping nobody would look at.

> [!example]- One row, judged honestly
> From [[Lab Reports]]: *"Limits named honestly — the step that could have
> misled you, roughly how much it could have moved the result, and one
> change that would actually reduce it."*
>
> **Partly.** I wrote that the lamp warmed the water. I did not say by how
> much, and I measured it — 3 °C at the closest distance. I also did not say
> what I would change. Weakest row.
> **Change:** put the 3 °C in, say that it could account for part of the
> difference at 10 cm, and add that a heat shield between lamp and beaker
> would separate light from heat. Ten minutes.
>
> That entry is worth more than "looks fine" written against all seven rows,
> and it took less time to write.

## Where this fits

Your judgement of your own work is not part of your mark, and neither is a
classmate's — see [[How Marks Work]]. That is deliberate, and it cuts both
ways: nothing you write here can cost you anything, which is exactly what
makes it worth writing honestly.

What it changes is the work. A self-check that leads to one fix is the
cheapest improvement available in this course, and by January it is
something you do without being asked.

> [!tip] Read your judgement out loud to the person beside you
> It catches the rows where you were being generous. You will usually hear
> it yourself before they say anything — which is the point, and is also why
> nothing they say to you goes anywhere near either of your marks.
