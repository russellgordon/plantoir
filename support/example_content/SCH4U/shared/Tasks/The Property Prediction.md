You get a formula and nothing else. No name, no picture, no bottle, no
data sheet.

From that formula you are going to state, in advance, what the substance
melts at, what it dissolves in, whether it conducts, and whether you
could scratch it — and then find out how close you were. If structure
really does predict behaviour, this is the test of it, and the whole
unit has been building toward being able to attempt it.

## The situation

Somebody has to choose a material, and the choice has consequences.
A seal that has to survive a solvent. A window that has to stay
transparent and stop a bullet. A wire that has to carry current without
melting. A coating that must not dissolve in rain.

In every case the question is the same: **given what this substance is
made of and how it is held together, how will it behave?** Nobody has
time to test everything, so the first pass is always a prediction from
structure — and the value of a chemist in that room is being right often
enough to narrow the field.

You are going to do exactly that, once, carefully, with your reasoning
visible and your prediction locked before you check.

## Who it is for

Write your Part 1 as a **one-page property sheet** for somebody who has
to make a materials decision and who will not open a textbook. They know
what melting point and solubility mean. They do not know what a lattice
is, and they should not have to.

That audience constrains you in a useful way. Every structural term you
use has to earn its place by explaining something the reader cares
about — "it is a covalent network solid" is only worth writing if the
next clause says what that costs them.

## Part 1 — The prediction, before any source

You will be given a coded substance: a formula, and where relevant a
structural formula. **Yours is yours** — no two people in the class get
the same code, so there is nobody to compare answers with and this task
is entirely your own from the first line. Working from that alone, and
with every book and site closed, produce your prediction.

**a) The type of solid it forms**, chosen from the four, with the
reasoning that got you there:

| Type | What holds it together | What that usually means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ionic | Attraction between oppositely charged ions throughout a lattice | High melting point, brittle, conducts when molten or dissolved but not as a solid |
| Molecular | Intermolecular forces between discrete molecules | Low melting point, often soft, poor conductor in any state |
| Covalent network | Covalent bonds continuing through the whole structure | Very high melting point, very hard, usually a poor conductor |
| Metallic | Positive cores in a shared sea of delocalised electrons | Variable melting point, malleable, conducts as a solid |

**b) Five predicted properties**, each as a specific claim rather than a
word:

- **Melting point**, as a range in degrees Celsius. "High" is not a
  prediction. "Above 800 °C" is.
- **Solubility in water, and in a non-polar solvent.** Both, with the
  reason, which will be about polarity and about what the solvent would
  have to overcome.
- **Electrical conductivity as a solid**, and **when molten or
  dissolved**. State both. Where they differ, say why.
- **Hardness or brittleness**, with the mechanism — what happens inside
  the structure when you hit it.
- **One property you are genuinely unsure about**, named, with the two
  possibilities and what would settle it.

**c) Your confidence**, per property, in words. "Confident", "fairly
sure", "guessing". You will be marked on the **calibration** of this,
not on the confidence itself — being uncertain about the thing you got
wrong is worth more than being certain about everything.

Hand Part 1 in, dated, **before** you begin Part 2. That is not
ceremony. A prediction you can still edit is not a prediction.

## Part 2 — The check, and the gap

Now find the real values, from sources you can name.

For each of your five properties, give the published value beside your
predicted one, and then do the part that carries the marks: **account
for every disagreement.**

There are only a few honest ways a prediction can miss, and naming which
one applies is the analysis:

- **The structure was misread.** You classified it wrong at step (a),
  and everything downstream inherited the error.
- **The classification was right and the reasoning inside it was
  wrong.** The type was correct, but you expected the wrong end of the
  range for that type.
- **The property depends on something the formula does not show** —
  crystal form, purity, or which of several structures the substance
  adopts.
- **The published value is for different conditions** than the ones you
  were imagining. A "melting point" under pressure, or a solubility at a
  temperature you did not assume.

A prediction that was wrong and is explained is worth more than one that
was right and is not. Say so plainly where it applies.

> [!important] The optional bench check, and what it is worth
> If you want to test a property yourself rather than look it up — a
> conductivity check, a solubility comparison, a melting behaviour in a
> water bath — bring me a written procedure and I will tell you whether
> it can run.
>
> It is worth doing for one reason: **a measurement you made carries a
> known uncertainty and a published value carries somebody else's**.
> But do not let it displace Part 3, and do not design anything that
> needs heat above a water bath. See the safety rules below and in
> [[Safety in the Lab]].

## Part 3 — What structural knowledge has been used for

Choose **one** of the following, and evaluate it in about a page.

**Either a technology that reads structure**: magnetic resonance
imaging, infrared spectroscopy, X-ray crystallography, mass
spectrometry. What does it actually measure, what does that let people
know that they could not know otherwise, and who benefits?

**Or a material designed out of structural understanding**: an aramid
fibre in protective fabric, a nanomaterial, a superconductor, a
cyanoacrylate adhesive, a specialised polymer. What was the structural
idea that made it possible, what does it do well, and — this half is
not optional — **what does making it, using it, or disposing of it cost
the environment?**

What I want here is not a description. It is an **evaluation**: a
benefit named specifically, a cost named specifically, and your judgement
about the balance, with the uncertainty in that judgement stated.

## Sources

At least **three**, of at least **two different kinds**, cited in the
format in [[Writing About Chemistry]]. Print and electronic both count
and you should have some of each. What matters is that a reader can
open what you opened.

| Kind of source | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Reference data | A chemical data handbook, a safety data sheet, the course booklet |
| Institutional | A government agency, a standards body, a professional society |
| Scholarly | A peer-reviewed article, a university department's published material |
| Manufacturer | Technical documentation for a real product, read critically |

**A manufacturer describing its own product is a source with an
interest**, and saying so in one clause is the difference between using
a source and being used by one. Where two sources disagree, report both
and say which you trust and why. See [[What Counts as Evidence]].

> [!danger] If any part of your work goes near a bench
> Nothing in this task requires you to handle a chemical. If you choose
> the optional check, everything below applies, and your written
> procedure comes to me first.
>
> - **School reagents only, at the concentrations supplied**, and
>   **combine only what your approved procedure lists**. Substances that
>   are harmless apart can produce a toxic gas together and there is no
>   way to tell by looking.
> - **Solvent testing happens in the fume hood, with no flame or hot
>   plate anywhere in the room.** The organic solvents used for
>   solubility comparisons are flammable, and **the vapour is the
>   hazard, not the liquid** — it is heavier than air and travels along
>   a bench to an ignition source you did not know was there.
> - **Melting behaviour is investigated in a water bath and nowhere
>   else.** Hot glassware looks exactly like cold glassware.
> - **Eye protection from the first move to the last of the cleanup.**
>   Hair back, sleeves secured, closed-toe shoes.
> - **Never pipette by mouth. Waft, never sniff. Nothing is tasted**,
>   including a substance you are confident you have identified.
> - **Nothing returns to a stock bottle.** Waste goes to the labelled
>   container, not the sink.
> - **Know where the eyewash, shower, extinguisher, blanket, and spill
>   kit are before you start**, and **report every incident
>   immediately**, however small.

## What to hand in

**1. The property sheet (Part 1)**, dated and handed in before you
research anything. One page, written for the audience above.

**2. The comparison (Part 2)**, as a table with a column for your
prediction, a column for the published value, a column for the source,
and a column for your account of any gap.

**3. The evaluation (Part 3)**, about a page.

**4. A reference list**, complete and formatted consistently.

Before you hand in, check every one of these:

- [ ] Part 1 is dated before Part 2 and has not been edited since
- [ ] The solid type is argued from the formula, not asserted
- [ ] Every predicted property is a **specific claim**, with units
- [ ] Both conductivity states are addressed, not just one
- [ ] Confidence is stated per property
- [ ] Every gap between prediction and published value has an account
- [ ] Part 3 names a benefit **and** an environmental cost, both
      specifically
- [ ] Every figure is traceable to a source a reader can open
- [ ] Sources of at least two different kinds, with their interests noted
- [ ] The property sheet is readable by somebody who does not know what
      a lattice is

## How it will be judged

| What I am looking for | Level 3 sounds like | Level 4 sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Classifying the solid | The right type, with a reason | The right type, with the alternative considered and ruled out |
| Predicting properties | Specific claims for all five | Claims with ranges and units, each tied to a structural feature |
| Reasoning from structure | Structure is mentioned | Every property is derived from structure rather than recalled |
| Confidence | Stated | **Calibrated** — the uncertain ones are the ones that missed |
| Accounting for gaps | Differences are noted | Each difference is diagnosed, and the diagnosis is specific |
| Where the model stops | Mentioned | A named limit of structural prediction, with your own case as the evidence |
| Part 3 | A real technology or material, correctly described | A benefit and a cost, both specific, with a judgement and its uncertainty |
| Sources | Three appropriate sources, cited | Matched to the claims they support, with their interests noted |
| Writing for the audience | Clear and accurate | A non-chemist could act on it without asking a follow-up question |

## What sinks an otherwise good prediction

- **A prediction edited after the check.** It shows, always, because the
  edited version is right about the surprising thing and vague about the
  obvious one.
- **"High melting point."** Compared with what? Ice has a high melting
  point compared with liquid nitrogen. Give a number or a range.
- **Conductivity answered once.** Solid and molten or dissolved are two
  different states with two different answers, and the difference
  between them is the single most useful piece of evidence for
  distinguishing ionic from metallic.
- **Structure used as a label rather than an explanation.** "It is
  ionic, so it has a high melting point" is a lookup. "Each ion is
  attracted to several oppositely charged neighbours in every direction,
  so melting means overcoming all of those at once" is chemistry.
- **Part 3 written as an advertisement.** Every specialised material has
  a cost — in the energy it takes to make, the solvents used, the
  difficulty of recycling it, or what happens at the end of its life. A
  page with no cost in it has not been evaluated.
- **A figure with no source**, including one that sounds too specific to
  be invented. Especially that one.
