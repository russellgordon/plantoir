In [[Looking at Tissue]] you focused on real tissue and found that the cells
were not interchangeable blobs. They had shapes — long ones, flat ones, ones
packed edge to edge like paving stones — and the shapes repeated. That
repetition is the whole idea on this page. A cell's shape is not decoration.
An organism builds the shapes it needs by dividing cells and then switching
them into specialists, and once you can say how that happens you can explain
almost everything else in this unit.

## The cell cycle, and what mitosis actually is

Most of a cell's life is **interphase**, which is not a rest. The cell grows
and does its job (G1), copies every chromosome (S), then makes the proteins
and structures it will need to divide (G2). Only after all of that does the
dividing part begin.

Here is the distinction people lose marks on. **Mitosis is the division of the
nucleus, not of the cell.** The duplicated chromosomes condense, line up
across the middle, and are pulled to opposite ends by spindle fibres, so that
two identical nuclei form. The cell is still one cell.

**Cytokinesis** is the separate event that follows, and it is what actually
produces two cells: the cytoplasm and its organelles are divided between them.
An animal cell pinches inward — a ring of protein tightens around the middle
like a drawstring, making a cleavage furrow. A plant cell cannot pinch,
because a rigid cell wall will not deform that way, so it builds a **cell
plate** outward from the centre until it meets the existing wall.

Because the chromosomes were copied before they were separated, both daughter
cells carry the same instructions as the parent. That is precisely why mitosis
is the right tool for **growth and repair** — you want more of the same cell,
not a variation on it. Your skin replaces its outer layer continuously, a cut
closes, a broken bone knits, a plant's root pushes further into the soil, and
all of it is this cycle running.

## Why one set of instructions produces many kinds of cell

Every one of your body cells carries the same complete set of DNA. A muscle
cell is not issued a shorter manual than a neuron. What differs is which parts
of the manual are **switched on** — which genes are being expressed — and once
a cell commits to a pattern of expression, it builds itself accordingly and
mostly stays that way.

Unspecialised cells that can still divide and then commit are **stem cells**
in animals, and **meristematic** cells in plants, found at root and shoot tips.
Both keep dividing; some of the daughters stay unspecialised and some
differentiate. That is how a body can keep building new specialists without
running out of the ability to make more.

Structure follows function, concretely:

- A **red blood cell** is a biconcave disc, and in humans it discards its
  nucleus as it matures. Both changes make room for more haemoglobin, and the
  flattened shape puts every molecule of it close to the surface, so oxygen
  loads and unloads quickly.
- A **neuron** may run most of the length of your leg in a single cell,
  because a signal that has to cross a boundary between cells is slower than
  one travelling along the inside of one.
- A **muscle cell** is packed with overlapping protein filaments arranged so
  that they can slide past each other, which is what shortening *is*.
- A **root hair cell** extends one long, thin projection into the soil. Its
  job is absorption, and absorption depends on surface area.

Notice that in each case you can argue from the job to the shape. That is the
test of whether you have understood specialisation, and it is what
[[Cells and Systems Practice]] asks you to do.

## When the controls fail

The cell cycle is not automatic. It has **checkpoints** — points at which the
cell verifies that the DNA was copied correctly, that the chromosomes are
properly attached, that there is a reason to divide at all. A cell that fails
a check normally pauses to repair, or destroys itself.

Cancer is what happens when those controls are lost. The cell divides when it
should not, ignores the signals telling it to stop, does not self-destruct
despite damaged DNA, and its descendants inherit the same failure. A tumour is
the accumulated result, and the damage to the organism comes from a mass of
cells taking up space, consuming resources, and disrupting the organ they grew
in — and, if cells break away and establish elsewhere, doing that in several
places at once.

> [!warning] "Cancer cells divide fast" is only half right, and the wrong half
> Plenty of perfectly healthy cells divide very quickly — the lining of your
> small intestine is replaced every few days, and bone marrow produces blood
> cells constantly. They are not cancerous. Some cancers, meanwhile, divide
> quite slowly. The defining problem is **uncontrolled** division, not fast
> division. When you compare cancerous and non-cancerous cells, the honest
> claim from your images is about the *fraction of cells caught mid-division*
> and about whether anything appears to be stopping them — not about speed
> alone.
