You will write about art in every unit of this course, and one standard
runs through all of it. That standard is not how much you liked the
work — it is whether a reader who has never seen it can tell, from your
sentences, what is actually there.

## Describe before you judge

The first sentence most people write is a verdict: it is beautiful, it is
disturbing, it is boring. Verdicts are where writing ends. Describe first,
and the judgement usually changes.

- Not *the painting is chaotic* — **every edge in the upper half is broken
  or scraped, and there is no line long enough to follow.**
- Not *the sculpture feels heavy* — **the form widens as it goes down and
  meets the plinth with no visible gap.**

The second version is what the first was made of, and a reader can
disagree with it — [[Describing What You See]] is the long version.

## Name the element and its effect

Naming an element is only half a sentence; the other half is what it does
to you as you look. *The artist uses contrast* is the half. **The only pure
white is on the child's collar, so your eye goes there first and keeps
returning** is the whole thing. [[The Elements of Design]] and
[[The Principles of Design]] are the working vocabulary — use a term
because it is precise, not because it sounds expert.

## Evidence a claim with a specific passage

Every claim needs a passage attached — a part of the work a reader could
put a finger on. Here is a paragraph with the evidence marked.

> The work is about waiting rather than grief. ==The two figures face the
> same direction, and neither has been given a face; the only detail in
> the whole panel is the folded coat across the nearer one's arm.== Nobody
> is mourning. Somebody is expecting someone.

Take the highlighted clause out and it is only an opinion; leave it in and
the reader can check you. [[Interpreting a Work]] and
[[The Critical Analysis Process]] set the full method out.

## Intention against result

Two different sentences: what the artist appears to have been after, and
what the work actually does to a viewer. Keep them apart, including about
your own work — "I wanted it crowded, and it reads as busy instead" is
stronger than either half alone, and it is the move
[[Assessing Your Own Work]] teaches.

## Citing an image or a source

Name the maker, the title, the year, the medium, and where you saw it —
`Kenojuak Ashevak, The Enchanted Owl, 1960, stonecut and stencil, seen at
the gallery`. For anything found online, give the site and the date you
found it. Reference images used in your own work get credited in the
sketchbook the day you use them — [[Copyright, Ownership, and Credit]].

[[The Interpretation]] and [[Art and Society Study]] are graded on all of
this, [[The Class Critique]] is the spoken version, and the analysis in
[[The Portfolio and Reflection]] is where it lands.
