Editing and proofreading are two different jobs, done in a different order.
Editing asks whether your sentences say what you mean, as clearly and
effectively as possible. Proofreading asks whether the spelling,
punctuation, and grammar on the page are correct. Do them in that order —
polishing the grammar of a sentence you're about to cut wastes the one
resource you don't get back in an exam or a working period: time.

| Mechanic | What you're checking | Quick example |
|---|---|---|
| Spelling | Every word is spelled the way it's meant to be, including words spellcheck won't catch | *definitely*, not *definately*; *its* vs. *it's* |
| Vocabulary and word choice | The word you chose is the most precise one available, not just a word that's roughly right | *said* vs. *insisted* vs. *admitted* |
| Punctuation | Commas, semicolons, and quotation marks do what they're supposed to and nothing else | a comma splice fixed with a semicolon or a period |
| Grammar | Subject and verb agree, pronouns are unambiguous, tense stays consistent | *she and I were*, not *her and me was* |
| Sentence variety and flow | Sentences don't all sound the same, and one idea leads into the next | short sentence after three long ones, for emphasis |
| Proofreading process | You have an actual method for catching your own errors, not just a final skim | reading backward, sentence by sentence |

## Spelling

Spellcheck catches a misspelled word. It does not catch a *correctly
spelled wrong word* — *their* where you meant *there*, *effect* where you
meant *affect*. Those are the errors worth hunting for by hand, because
software will never flag them.

> [!fail] Weak
> Its clear that the narrator looses something she can't get back.

> [!success] Strong
> It's clear that the narrator loses something she can't get back.

Keep a running list, in your own notebook, of the specific words you
misspell more than once. Everyone has two or three — knowing yours is
faster than re-learning the rule from scratch every time.

## Vocabulary and word choice

A precise word does more work than a vague one, and it usually costs you
nothing extra to find it. "Said" is not wrong, but a scene where every
character only ever "says" everything has flattened all of them into the
same voice.

> [!fail] Vague
> The father said that his daughter was only a girl, which made her feel
> bad.

> [!success] Precise
> The father dismissed his daughter's competence with a single phrase,
> and she registered, for the first time, exactly what he thought she
> was worth.

*Dismissed* and *registered* carry meaning *said* and *made her feel bad*
don't — precision is not about using bigger words, it's about using the
word that actually matches what happened.

## Punctuation

Most punctuation errors in essay writing cluster around a small number of
repeat offenders:

- **The comma splice** — two complete sentences joined with only a comma.
  Fix it with a period, a semicolon, or a coordinating conjunction:
  *The narrator says nothing, she has already decided.* becomes *The
  narrator says nothing; she has already decided.*
- **Quotation punctuation** — the comma or period goes *inside* the
  closing quotation mark in Canadian usage: *"just a girl,"* not
  *"just a girl",*. [[Integrating Evidence - The Quote Sandwich\|Integrating Evidence: The Quote Sandwich]]
  covers where the citation itself goes.
- **The apostrophe** — *its* shows possession, *it's* means *it is*.
  Reversing these is the single most common punctuation error in student
  essays, and spellcheck will not catch it.

## Grammar

The two errors worth checking for specifically, every time, because
they're the ones that actually confuse a reader rather than just looking
informal:

- **Subject-verb agreement across a long subject.** The verb agrees with
  the subject, not with whatever noun happens to sit closest to it: *The
  choices the narrator makes reveal* her values — not *reveals*, even
  though "makes" is the nearer verb.
- **Tense consistency.** Literary analysis is written in the literary
  present ([[Writing About Literature]] covers why) — pick it and hold it.
  Switching between *the narrator learns* and *the narrator learned*
  mid-paragraph, with no reason for the shift, is one of the fastest ways
  to lose a reader's trust in your control of the sentence.

## Sentence variety and flow

Five sentences in a row of the same length and shape read as monotone,
even when every one of them is grammatically correct. Vary the length
deliberately, and use a short sentence right after a run of long ones to
land a point:

> [!success] Deliberate variety
> Saul plays because it is the one thing at St. Jerome's that still
> belongs to him, the one place where his body does something the
> institution didn't order it to do, the one hour of the day nobody is
> allowed to touch. Then they touch it anyway.
>
> The short sentence at the end works *because* of the three long clauses
> before it — the contrast is the whole point.

Transitions matter just as much as sentence length: a paragraph that
jumps from one idea to the next with no signal (*however*, *as a result*,
*by contrast*, or simply a sentence that echoes a word from the one before
it) makes your reader do the work of connecting your ideas for you.

## Proofreading: the process itself

Proofreading is not the same skill as writing, so it needs its own pass,
done differently than the writing itself:

- [ ] Read your work **backward**, sentence by sentence from the end —
      this breaks the flow that lets your brain autocorrect errors without
      you noticing them.
- [ ] Read it **out loud**, even quietly — your ear catches an awkward or
      missing word your eye slides past.
- [ ] Check one thing at a time. A pass hunting only for comma splices
      catches more of them than a single pass trying to catch everything
      at once.
- [ ] Leave time between drafting and proofreading, even fifteen minutes —
      distance is what lets you read the sentence you actually wrote
      instead of the one you meant to write.

This is a skill you build, the same way [[Judging Your Own Work]] describes
building self-assessment — modelled first, then practised on your own work
before every piece you hand in for a mark.
