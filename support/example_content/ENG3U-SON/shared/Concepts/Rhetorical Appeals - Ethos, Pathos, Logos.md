When Claudius stands before the Danish court and explains, in his very
first speech, why he married his brother's widow so soon after the
funeral, he is not just informing the court — he is working to persuade
it. So is Polonius, a few scenes later, sending Laertes off to France
with a speech of advice dressed up to sound wise. Both speeches use the
same three basic levers a persuasive speaker has always reached for.

## Three appeals

| Appeal | What it appeals to | How it shows up |
|---|---|---|
| **Ethos** | The speaker's credibility, authority, or character | Claudius opens by presenting himself as measured and grieving, not reckless — establishing that he is a man whose judgement should be trusted |
| **Pathos** | The audience's emotions | Claudius leans on shared grief for the late king before pivoting to his own decision, borrowing the court's sorrow to soften its judgement |
| **Logos** | Logic, reasoning, evidence | Polonius structures his advice to Laertes as a numbered set of maxims, giving it the shape of a reasoned argument even where the individual pieces of advice do not obviously connect |

A single speech rarely uses only one appeal. Claudius's opening speech
works because it braids ethos and pathos together — he is establishing
who he is *and* borrowing the room's grief in the same breath, before he
ever gets to what he actually wants the court to accept.

## Reading for appeals

When you are trying to identify which appeal is at work in a passage,
ask: is this asking me to trust the speaker (ethos), feel something
(pathos), or follow a chain of reasoning (logos)? Most persuasive speech
blends more than one, and naming which appeal is doing the heaviest
lifting at a given moment is more useful than trying to sort every
sentence into exactly one box.
