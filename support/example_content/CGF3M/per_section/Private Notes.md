## Before the first field day, every time

Read this list before the shoreline day, and again before the woodlot
and the farm. It is not a first-week formality — the sites differ and so
do the risks.

**Water.** Nobody enters the water, for any reason, on any day. Stay
back from the lip of any bank: undercut banks overhang water with
nothing beneath them and fail without warning under added weight. Treat
a fresh scarp or a leaning tree as a sign the bank is actively going.

**The woodlot.** Ankles and eyes. Roots and holes under leaf litter take
ankles; branches at face height take eyes, especially when students are
walking with their heads in a notebook. Head up while walking, stop to
write, two arms' length behind the person ahead. If a slope needs hands
to climb, we take a different route.

**Ticks.** This is the one to be genuinely organised about. Long
trousers tucked into socks, closed shoes, light colours, repellent
applied before we leave, and everyone stays on the path — ticks wait on
vegetation at the edges. A full-body check the same day is the
non-negotiable part: behind knees, waistband, hairline, armpits, groin.
Remove with fine-tipped tweezers, gripping as close to the skin as
possible and pulling straight out, without twisting or squeezing. The
bacterium generally needs the tick attached for roughly 24 to 36 hours,
which is exactly why prompt checking is the whole defence. Say that
sentence to them, because it is the one that makes them do it.

**Heat and sun.** Early-term fieldwork is often the hottest weather of
the term and the shoreline has no shade. Water, hats, sunscreen, and
tell them plainly to report dizziness, a headache or stopping sweating
*early* — heat illness gets worse quickly and is easy to treat at the
start.

**The farm.** A working farm is an industrial workplace that happens to
be outdoors. Machinery is large, quiet from behind, and driven by people
who cannot see students. Group discipline is the control here: stay
together, cross yards only when told, never approach a running machine,
stay out of buildings and pits we are not taken into, and follow the
farmer's instructions immediately and without negotiation. The farmer is
the person who knows what is dangerous today.

Collect medical conditions and allergies privately and in advance, and
check that anyone who needs medication has it on them rather than in a
bag left at the edge of the field.

## Two case studies that were substituted while this was written

The drought case is **the 2021 Prairie drought**, not Cape Town. It is
built from Statistics Canada's crop production release and the AAFC
Canadian Drought Monitor archive, which means students can watch the
event develop month by month and can check every figure themselves. It
also lets the heat dome sit inside the same summer.

The slow-onset case is **the Great Lakes water level cycle**, chosen
partly because it is the shore the class walks to. A metre and a half of
range over a century, no single event and no moment it begins, is a much
harder and more useful thing for students to argue about than a distant
example, and their own first-visit and final profiles are evidence in it.

## The conflicting figures are deliberate — do not tidy them

Several pages carry **two figures for the same quantity, with the reason
they differ**. The clearest is the 2023 wildfire area: NRCan's 14.6
million hectares of satellite-mapped burned *forest* against CIFFC's
17,203,625 ha of reported *fire perimeters*, which enclose lakes and
unburned islands. Both are correct; they answer different questions.

Reconciling two numbers is the teaching point on four of the six case
pages — also the 2013 Alberta flood's \$2.72 billion federal total
against roughly \$6 billion provincial, Fiona's three pressure readings,
and Tōhoku's two magnitudes and three run-up heights. If you reduce any
of these to one number you remove the lesson, and the class pages that
reach them are written around the comparison.

## Deliberately absent, because no figure could be traced

- **A damage cost for Tōhoku.** Figures circulate; none could be traced
  to a Japanese government publication, so the page says so.
- **Which body assigned Fukushima its INES Level 7 rating.** The page
  states that the rating was assigned in Japan rather than by the IAEA,
  and that which Japanese body made the decision is reported
  inconsistently and could not be confirmed. Left as unresolved.
- **Firefighter names.** Eight people working on wildfires in Canada
  died in 2023. No official consolidated list of names has been
  published, so none is given.
- **Any "1-in-100-year" label for the 2013 Alberta flood.** No return
  period for that event could be traced to a primary source, so the page
  does not assert one — the return-period discussion lives on
  [[Mapping a Hazard]] instead, where it is a general point.

If a student brings any of these in from elsewhere, that is a teachable
moment about sourcing rather than an error to correct quietly.

## The shape of the schedule, in case you need to cut

The four field days are Unit 1 Day 8 (shoreline), Unit 1 Day 16
(woodlot), Unit 3 Day 8 (farm) and Unit 4 Day 5 (shoreline again, with
the hazard question). Each has a preparation period immediately before it
and a debrief period immediately after, and the safety and kit pages are
linked from every preparation period rather than only from the first week.

If the timetable takes a day off you, take it from a work period rather
than from a debrief. The debriefs are where the field data becomes
geography; a work period can be recovered at a help session.
