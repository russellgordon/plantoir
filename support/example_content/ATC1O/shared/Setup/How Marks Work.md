You are marked on what you do with movement, and on how you think about
it — not on how much dance you had done before this course.

## The seventy and the thirty

Every Grade 9 credit in Ontario is built the same way. Seventy per cent of
your mark comes from the work you do as the course goes along, and it leans
towards your **most recent and most consistent** work rather than averaging
the start of the course against the end of it. The other thirty per cent
comes from the final evaluation at the end of the course, which is
deliberately built to reach back across the whole semester rather than test
a single unit.

**The seventy** is the seven tasks you show or hand in as we go, in the
order you meet them: [[The Movement Signature]], [[The Safe Dancer]],
[[The Phrase]], [[The Composition Study]], [[The Form Study]],
[[The Dance Review]], and the [[Dance and Community Task]]. They are not
equally weighted — the composition study asks for far more than the
phrase does, and the later ones carry more because they are the more
recent evidence. Ask me for the exact split whenever you want it. It is
not a secret; it is just not the useful thing to memorise.

**The thirty** is two things, in two very different rooms:
[[The Showing]], made by your group and performed for an invited
audience, and [[The Portfolio and Reflection]], submitted complete with
a reflective piece written in the examination period. A course that
spends 110 hours on your feet should not be settled by an hour of
writing, and it is not.

The journal entries that carry a mark are the ones you write **in the
studio, at a milestone** — after a showing, after a seminar, after the
day we watch the recording, wherever the class page asks for one. What
you add at home between classes is yours, it is worth doing, and it is
not something I mark. Practice is practice.

## Four kinds of thinking

| What is being judged | What it means in a studio |
| --- | --- |
| What you know | The elements, the terminology, how the body actually moves |
| How you think | Choosing a structure, solving a movement problem, judging a work |
| How you communicate | What the movement says without explanation, and how you write and talk about dance |
| Where you apply it | Making it work in a form, a task, or a room nobody rehearsed you for |

Every task asks for more than one of these, and the balance shifts from
task to task. The last row is why Unit 3 exists: it is easy to dance
well inside the vocabulary you built yourself, and much harder inside
somebody else's.

## Three kinds of evidence, and only one of them is a hand-in

**What you make** is the obvious one — solos, phrases, compositions,
reviews, programme notes, the portfolio. **What I watch you do** is the
second: how you generate material when nothing is working, what you
actually do with a note, how you are with a partner whose weight you are
taking. **What you tell me** is the third — the coaching conversations
in the working periods, where some of what you understand will show up
and nowhere else.

The second and third are evidence in their own right, not a warm-up for
the hand-in. If your work is unfinished on a bad week, the two minutes
we spend talking about it is often the strongest evidence in the room
that day. That is not a consolation prize. It is how the mark is meant
to work.

## What is not in your mark

How you work is reported separately, on the same report card, as **E, G,
S or N** — six habits: responsibility, organization, independent work,
collaboration, initiative, and self-regulation. They matter, we will
talk about them, and they do not move your percentage. Effort is not a
mark here, attendance is not a mark, and neither is how much you say in
a seminar.

There is one exception, and in dance it is a real one rather than a
loophole. This course is required to teach responsible practice, safe
and ethical practice, and collaboration during rehearsal — they are
expectations C3.1, C3.3 and C3.2 in the curriculum. Where a habit is
named in the curriculum it is marked like anything else in the
curriculum, and what is looked for is written down for you on
[[The Safe Dancer]] and [[The Showing]] rather than left to my
impression of you.

Your own judgement of your work, and your classmates', are not part of
your mark either. You will judge your own work against the criteria
often — see [[Judging Your Own Work]] — because it is the fastest way to
get better, and it cuts both ways: nothing you write there can cost you
anything.

## Why ten years of training does not start you ahead

Nothing above marks how much you could already do at the start of the
course. What is marked is the movement you generate, how you structure it,
whether the second version is better than the first, and whether you can say
what you chose and why. A trained dancer who repeats a combination they were
given three years ago meets almost none of that. A beginner who makes four
movements that are theirs and can defend the order they are in meets most of
it.

## If you are injured

Injured dancers are not excused, they are redeployed: notating,
outside-eye feedback, stagecraft, filming, rehearsal direction. Those
are real dance skills. We agree in advance, in writing, which rows of
the task's criteria your version of the work is judged against, and you
get the same note-and-fix time everybody else gets. Tell me early and we
will plan it.

> [!tip] The criteria live on the task
> Every task page carries what earns the marks for that piece of work,
> published on the day it launches rather than the week it is due. Read
> it then.
>
> Every task also has a point before it is marked where you get a note
> from me, and time afterwards to use it — sometimes the rest of that
> period, sometimes the whole of the next class. The class page for the
> day says which it is.
