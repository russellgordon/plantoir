On your desk today are two headlines about the same event, from two
different outlets, printed side by side. Neither one is lying, exactly —
and they will not leave you with the same impression of what happened. This
seminar is about how that's possible, and about who each version was
actually written for.

## The questions

1. Two headlines, two different words for the same event — what's the one
   word each headline chose that the other avoided, and what does that
   word do to how you read the rest of the story before you've read a
   single sentence of it?
2. Read past the headline into the first two paragraphs of each piece.
   What gets mentioned first in one, and left until paragraph six in the
   other — and does that ordering change what feels most important?
3. Would a reader who already agreed with one side of this story keep
   reading past a headline that challenged them? What does your answer
   tell you about who each outlet is actually writing for?
4. If you had only ever read one of these two accounts, what would you
   believe happened — and how confident should you actually be in that
   belief?

> [!success]- What a strong answer to question 3 sounds like
> "A reader who already agrees with the framing in headline A probably
> stops reading before headline B's version could change their mind — the
> two pieces aren't really arguing with each other, because their readers
> mostly aren't crossing over to see the disagreement. That means neither
> outlet is actually trying to persuade someone who disagrees with them.
> They're writing to confirm what their existing audience already expects,
> which is a different job than reporting the event itself."
>
> Notice this goes further than "the outlets have different audiences" —
> it explains what that difference actually does to how persuasion is
> working, or isn't, in either piece.

We'll put a name to what you're noticing today — bias, framing, implied
message — in [[Bias and Perspective in News Media]] in a few classes. For
now, the goal is to notice it working on you before you have the vocabulary
to file it away.
