You cannot install software on the school machines. Half the
internet is filtered. You do not have admin rights on the very
computer you are learning to administer. Annoying — and yet the
network quietly keeps a few thousand people's accounts, marks, and
personal files safe from each other and from the wider internet,
every single day. Security and access pull against each other in
every system ever built, and the tension never resolves; it only
gets managed. So the question worth arguing is not "rules: good or
bad?" but what all this protecting is actually *for* — and whether
the current trade is the right one.

Questions worth arguing about:

1. List what the school network protects: whose data, whose time,
   whose money. Now rank the list. Does the filtering we actually
   have match the ranking you actually made?
2. "I have nothing to hide" — does that survive the observation
   that you still lock your locker? What is the difference
   between hiding something and simply owning it?
3. Who should hold admin rights on a machine: the person who uses
   it, the person who fixes it, or the person legally responsible
   when it goes wrong? Those are three different people in this
   building.
4. An acceptable-use policy is a contract most people sign
   without reading. Read ours. Which clause surprised you — and
   which clause protects *you* rather than the school?
5. A technician with admin rights can read anything on the
   system. The rules say do not; the password says can. What
   actually stops them — and is that enough?

The stakes are personal: the same trade-offs run your phone, your
accounts, and every network you will ever be paid to maintain.
[[Security and Ethics]] carries the concepts; what you argue here
decides what kind of technician ends up holding the passwords.
