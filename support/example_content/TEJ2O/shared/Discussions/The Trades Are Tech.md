Ask who works in technology and the stock photo answers: someone
at a laptop in an office tower. But the electrician who wires a
server room, the technician who terminates the fibre, and the
installer who commissions a building's automation work closer to
the metal than most software developers ever do. The trades run on
real math — load calculations, signal budgets, code compliance —
and pay real money, often sooner and with less debt than the
university route, because apprentices earn while they learn. Yet
"good with your hands" still gets said like a consolation prize.
That prestige gap is steering people right now — possibly you.

Questions worth arguing about:

1. An apprentice electrician earns from year one and finishes
   licensed; a university student pays for four years first. Why
   does only one of these paths get called "settling" — and who
   benefits from the prestige gap?
2. List what a licensed electrician must actually know: the math,
   the code book, the physics of things that can kill you. Which
   parts of this course are already trades training in disguise?
3. "Robots will take the trades" versus "someone has to install
   and fix the robots" — which claim survives contact with the
   evidence? What would each need to be true?
4. The skilled trades remain heavily male in Canada while the
   work itself has no gender. What keeps the numbers stuck, and
   what would honestly move them — posters, pay, mentors, or
   something else?
5. Fifty years from now, which more likely still exists mostly
   unchanged: your favourite app, or the building's wiring? What
   does your answer say about which skills hold their value?

The pathways are concrete: [[Careers in Computer Technology]] maps
which doors open from secondary school, apprenticeship, college,
and university — and skills this course banks, from
[[Crimp and Test a Cable]] to reading a spec sheet, are the first
rungs of several of them.
