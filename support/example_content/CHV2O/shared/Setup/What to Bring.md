Short list, and the first item on it is the one that decides how the term
goes.

| Bring | Why |
| --- | --- |
| A notebook you keep for the whole course | This is [[Your Civic Notebook]] — issues, sources, and how your thinking moved. Paper is fine; one digital file is fine; five apps is not |
| Something to write with | You will mark up printed bills, budget pages and decisions most weeks, and annotating a document is how you read one |
| A device you can research and write on | The school has loaners. Everything we use runs in a browser and none of it needs an account |
| The reading, already done | Periods start from a document you were meant to have met |
| The current task page, read | [[The Issue Brief]] and every task after it state what earns the marks before you begin |

## About the reading

It is rarely long and it is never optional. A period built around a
budget page does not work if half the room is seeing it for the first
time — the discussion becomes a summary of what it says instead of an
argument about what it shows.

If you could not do the reading, come anyway and tell me at the start.
That is a much smaller problem than pretending.

## Following one issue all term

Choose one civic issue in the first week and follow it until the end of the course.
Local is better than national, because you can reach the people deciding
it. Save every article, minute, and document you find about it in one
place, dated.

By the time [[The Civic Action Project]] starts you will either have ten
weeks of material on a live issue, or you will have discovered it is the
wrong issue — and both of those are worth more than starting from nothing
at the end of the course.

## What you do not need

No textbook. No subscriptions. Everything this course reads is published
free by a government, a court, or a public body: parl.ca, ola.org,
ontario.ca, canada.ca, scc-csc.ca, CanLII, Elections Canada, and your own
municipality's website. If you find yourself behind a paywall, ask — there
is nearly always a public version of the same document.

## One thing not to bring

A position you are unwilling to test. The seminars in this course are
built on questions where the evidence and the values genuinely divide —
[[Who Should Decide Locally|Who Should Decide Locally?]] is the first — and arriving determined to
win wastes the period for everyone, including you.

Bring the second thing instead: a willingness to say "I had not thought
of that" out loud, in front of people. It is the single most useful habit
this course can leave you with.
