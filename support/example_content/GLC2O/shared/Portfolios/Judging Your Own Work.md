Every task page ends with what earns the marks, published before you
start. Most people read it once, on the day the task is launched, and
never again — and then hand in something that misses two lines they
could have fixed in fifteen minutes. This page is the fifteen minutes.

We do it together the first time, on somebody else's inventory, so the
move is not new to you when it is your own work being judged. By Unit 3
you run it without being asked, which is the point of teaching it at
all.

## The routine

1. Put the criteria beside your draft. Not in another tab — beside it.
2. Go line by line. For each one write **yes**, **not yet**, or
   **partly**, and one sentence of evidence: the paragraph, the row of
   the spreadsheet, the sentence a reader would land on. "Yes" with
   nothing pointed at is a wish.
3. Find your weakest line. Not the easiest to fix — the weakest.
4. Write one sentence saying what you will change, and when.
5. Change it. That is why this is never the last thing that happens
   here: it is either the first thing in a period, with the rest of that
   period yours, or it is a block with the fixing built into it. A
   judgement you have no time to act on is just a worry.

Steps 2 and 3 are the whole exercise. Naming your weakest line honestly
is harder than any of the fixing, because it means writing down the
thing you were hoping nobody would notice.

> [!example]- One line, judged honestly
> From [[The Skills Inventory Task]]: *"Evidence — every skill attached
> to a situation a third person could confirm."*
>
> **Partly.** Eight of my ten have a situation and a name beside them.
> Two say "group project" with no year and nobody who would remember
> it, and one of those is "leadership", which is the line I most want
> to keep. Weakest line.
> **Change:** either find the actual project and the person who ran it,
> or take leadership off and use the canteen shifts instead, which I
> can evidence. This period.
>
> That entry is worth more than "looks fine" written against all six
> criteria, and it took less time.

## The three lines people mark themselves generously on

Every term, in this course, it is the same three.

- **"A situation somebody else could confirm."** Not confirm in
  principle — confirm in practice. Say the person's name out loud. If
  you would not be comfortable with me phoning them, the line is an
  adjective wearing a story.
- **"Sourced."** Pick one figure at random out of your budget and time
  yourself getting back to where it came from. Anything over a minute
  means the source is not really attached, and a number you cannot
  re-find in Grade 12 is a number you will have to look up again anyway.
- **"Honest about the factors."** Read your explanation and ask which
  of the real reasons is missing. Money, transit, hours, family, who
  else works there. The flattering reasons are always the ones that
  make it onto the page first.

## Where this fits

Your judgement of your own work is not part of your mark, and neither is
a classmate's — see [[How Marks Work]]. That is deliberate and it cuts
both ways: nothing you write here can cost you anything, which is
exactly what makes it worth writing honestly.

What it changes is the work. A self-check that leads to one real fix is
the cheapest mark improvement available in a course this short, and it
is the same move you will make on your own before you send a real
application to a real employer, when nobody has scheduled a period for
it.

One last thing worth doing: read your judgement to the person beside
you. It catches the lines where you were being kind to yourself, and you
will usually hear it yourself before they say anything.
