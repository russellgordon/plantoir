Every task page in this course carries a table of success criteria,
published before you start. Most people read it once, on launch day, and
then hand in work that misses two rows they could have fixed in twenty
minutes. This page is the twenty minutes.

We do it together the first time, out loud, on somebody else's write-up,
so that the move is not new to you when the work being judged is your
own.

## The routine

1. Put the criteria table beside your work — the task page's table, or
   the six rows on [[Investigation Write-Ups]] if it is a lab write-up.
2. Go row by row. For each one write **yes**, **not yet**, or **partly**,
   and one sentence of evidence: the page, the paragraph, the graph, the
   thing a reader would actually see. "Yes" with nothing after it is a
   wish.
3. Find your weakest row. Not the easiest to fix — the weakest.
4. Write one sentence saying what you will change, and when.
5. Change it, in the rest of this period or in the next one. That is why
   this is never scheduled on a day something is due: a judgement you
   have no time to act on is just a worry.

Steps 2 and 3 are the whole exercise. Naming your weakest row honestly is
harder than any of the fixing, because it means writing down the thing
you were hoping nobody would look at.

> [!example]- One row, judged honestly
> From [[Investigation Write-Ups]], the uncertainty row: *"The dominant
> source named, with a number, and what that number does to the
> conclusion."*
>
> **Partly.** I wrote that the timing was the biggest uncertainty, which
> is true, but I never said how big. Ten revolutions timed by hand is
> about ±0.2 s out of 8.4 s, so about 2%, and my two speeds differ by 9%
> — so timing does not explain the gap and I have been pretending it
> might. Weakest row.
> **Change:** put the 2% in, then say plainly that the remaining 7% is
> not accounted for by timing. Next period.
>
> That entry is worth more than "looks fine" against all six rows, and
> it took less time to write.

## After a checkpoint

The end-of-unit checkpoints work the same way, with the physics standing
in for the criteria table. You mark your own; nothing about that goes
anywhere near your mark (see [[How Marks Work]]). What you write is a
**revision list**, and it has to be specific enough to act on:

- Not useful: "review circular motion".
- Useful: "I keep drawing an outward force. Redo questions 3 and 7 of
  [[Circular Motion Practice]] starting from the free-body diagram, and
  say out loud what supplies the centripetal force each time."

Bring the list to the next class. There is a period set aside for working
through it, and it is the cheapest mark improvement available in this
course.

## Why it is not part of your mark

Deliberately, and it cuts both ways: nothing you write here can cost you
anything, which is exactly what makes it worth writing honestly. What it
changes is the work, not the number.

> [!tip] Read it to the person beside you
> Say your judgement out loud. It catches the rows where you were being
> generous — usually you hear it yourself before they say anything.
