This course can answer the oldest question in math class without
dodging. Compound interest *is* an exponential function wearing
dollar signs — the mathematics of every savings account, student
loan, and credit card statement you will ever hold, and
[[Your Financial Future]] hands it to you with your own name on it.
Anything that cycles — tides, daylight, a heartbeat, the seat of a
Ferris wheel — is a sinusoidal function waiting for its parameters,
which is what [[The Tide Problem]] rehearses. And anything that
doubles — populations, rumours, debts left alone — grows the way
[[Double or Nothing]] makes visceral.

The second answer is about transfer: you may never state the domain
of a function after this course, but you will spend a lifetime modelling
messy situations, checking claims before trusting them, and arguing
from constraints. That training happens here whether the algebra
resurfaces or not.

Questions worth arguing about:

1. "When will I use this?" is sometimes an honest question and
   sometimes code for something else — frustration, being lost, a
   fair complaint. How could a classmate tell which they just heard?
2. A bank never shows you the exponential function inside your loan,
   yet it is in there compounding. Who benefits when the people
   signing cannot read the mathematics they are signing?
3. List three quantities from your own week that repeat on a cycle —
   secretly sinusoidal. Whose job is it to notice they are?
4. Nobody asks a basketball player when they will use wind sprints.
   What is different about mathematics — or is anything?
5. Suppose the honest answer for some topic really is "you,
   personally, may never use this directly". Does the transfer answer
   rescue it, or is that a dodge too? Defend your position.

The test of today's talk is what you do at [[Money Over Time]] and
[[Your Financial Future]] — places where the mathematics is not
dressed up as useful, but simply is.
