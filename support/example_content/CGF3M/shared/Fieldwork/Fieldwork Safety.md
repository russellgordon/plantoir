Read this before the first field day and again before each one after. It
is short because everything in it has actually gone wrong somewhere, to
somebody, doing exactly what we are going to do.

## Water and the shoreline edge

You do not enter the water. Not to reach a better sample, not to retrieve
equipment, not on a warm day. Wet rock, algae-covered cobble and clay
banks are far more slippery than they look, and a fall into cold water
takes your breath before it takes your strength.

Bank edges undercut. The ground you stand on may be overhanging water
with nothing beneath it, and it fails without warning under added weight.
Stay back from any lip, work with a partner, and treat a fresh scarp or a
leaning tree as a sign the bank is actively going.

## Uneven ground in the woodlot

Most woodlot injuries are ankles and eyes. Roots, holes and logs hidden
under leaf litter take ankles; low branches at face height take eyes,
particularly when you are looking down at a notebook. Head up while
walking, stop to write, and stay two arms' length behind the person ahead
so a released branch does not reach you. If a slope needs your hands to
climb, it needs a different route.

## Ticks and Lyme disease

Blacklegged ticks are established across much of southern Ontario, and
Public Health Ontario maps and updates estimated risk areas each year.
The bacterium that causes Lyme disease generally requires the tick to
stay attached for roughly 24 to 36 hours, so **finding a tick promptly is
the whole defence.**

- Long trousers, tucked into socks, and closed shoes. Light colours make
  a tick visible.
- Insect repellent on skin and clothing, applied before we leave.
- Stay on the path; ticks wait on vegetation at the edges.
- Full body check the same day — behind knees, waistband, hairline,
  armpits, groin.
- Remove one with fine-tipped tweezers, gripping as close to the skin as
  possible and pulling straight out without twisting or squeezing. Wash
  the area. Tell your family, and note the date.

## Sun, heat, and the length of a field day

September fieldwork is often the hottest weather of the term, and the
shoreline has no shade at all. Bring water and drink it before you are
thirsty; a wide-brimmed hat and sunscreen; and tell a teacher early if
you feel dizzy, stop sweating, or get a headache, because heat illness
gets worse quickly and is easy to treat at the start. See
[[Drought and Heat]] for why this hazard is more dangerous than it feels.

## Vehicles and machinery at the farm

A working farm is an industrial workplace that happens to be outdoors.
Tractors and machinery are large, quiet from behind, and driven by people
who cannot see you. Stay in the group, cross yards only when a teacher
says, never approach a running machine, keep out of buildings and pits we
are not taken into, and follow the farmer's instructions immediately and
without negotiation — they are the person who knows what is dangerous
today.

Tell a teacher about any allergy, asthma, or medical condition, and carry
what you need for it. The full expectations for conduct are in
[[Our Working Agreement]]; what to bring is in [[What to Bring]].
