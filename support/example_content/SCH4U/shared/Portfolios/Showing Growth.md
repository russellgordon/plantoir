Stop adding entries for one sitting and write one that looks backwards.

## Do this

1. Re-read [[Your First Entry]], including the sentence about what you
   think a scientific model is.
2. Find an early piece of work and a recent one on the **same skill** —
   two conclusions, two sets of limitations, two attempts to say what
   your numbers are worth.
3. Put them side by side and name three specific differences. Quote
   yourself in both directions.

Same skill is the hard part. A boiling point investigation and an
equilibrium disturbance are different jobs, and comparing them shows
nothing except that one came later. Comparing two **conclusions** — the
paragraph where you say what you found and how far you trust it — is a
fair comparison, because you wrote that paragraph both times.

## Not this

> "I have improved a lot and learned many things. My labs are much
> better than they were at the start and I really understand
> equilibrium now."

Nothing in that paragraph could be checked, argued with, or shown to be
wrong. A claim nobody could contradict is not evidence of growth — it is
a sentence about wanting to have grown.

## This

> My first conclusion, from [[Boiling Points and Structure]], was:
> "Water had the highest boiling point because hydrogen bonding is the
> strongest intermolecular force." One sentence. No measurement, no
> comparison, and the word "because" is carrying an explanation I had
> not actually tested — my four liquids differed in molar mass and in
> bonding at the same time, so my data could not have separated them
> even in principle.
>
> My conclusion from [[Disturbing an Equilibrium]] says: "Diluting the
> mixture by a factor of two dropped the absorbance from 0.412 to 0.176.
> Dilution on its own would have given 0.206, since absorbance is
> proportional to concentration. The extra fall of 0.030 is six times
> the ±0.005 repeatability we measured on the colorimeter, so it is a
> real effect and not the dilution itself: the system shifted toward the
> side with more dissolved particles, as
> [[Le Chatelier's Principle|Le Châtelier's Principle]] predicts. What the
> principle does not tell us is *how far* it shifted — for that we would need the
> equilibrium constant, and the direction is all this observation can
> support."
>
> Three differences. The second one gives numbers with a predicted
> comparison value, so a reader can see what "no effect" would have
> looked like. It separates two causes that produce the same visual
> result, which the first conclusion did not even notice were two
> causes. And it names the model **and its limit** in the last sentence,
> rather than using the model's name as the explanation.
>
> The change in my head: I used to think naming the right principle was
> the answer. I now think naming the principle is the beginning of the
> answer, and the answer is what the principle is and is not entitled to
> tell me.

## What I am reading for

That last paragraph. Three differences in the work, and then one
sentence about what changed in your thinking to produce them. The second
is worth more than the first, and it is also the part people leave out.

There is one growth story this course cares about above the others, and
it is the one in the example: moving from *using* a model to *bounding*
it. If your mid-semester comparison can show that shift with two pieces
of your own writing, you have shown the thing this course is for.

> [!question] A useful prompt if you are stuck
> What do you now do automatically that you had to be told to do in the
> first month? Writing the unit before the number. Recording the
> temperature without being asked. Checking a difference against the
> instrument's repeatability before calling it a difference. Asking what
> the model assumes about your set-up. Automatic is exactly the evidence
> — you stopped noticing you were doing it, which is why you have to go
> back and look rather than trying to remember.
