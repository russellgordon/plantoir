Somewhere in [[Portraits with Purpose]] you took two photographs of the
same person, seconds apart, and one of them was simply better. Nothing
about the subject changed. What changed was the frame — where things
sat inside the rectangle — and that is composition: deciding, before
the shutter fires, where the viewer's eye will go.

## Where the eye goes

The eye is predictable. It goes to faces first, then to the brightest
area, then wherever lines in the picture point. A photographer who
knows this stops hoping viewers will notice the right thing and starts
steering them to it. Every guideline below is just that steering,
written down.

## Three habits

**Rule of thirds.** Imagine the frame divided into thirds both ways,
like a tic-tac-toe board. Placing your subject on those lines — eyes on
the top line, a runner on a side line — feels more alive than dead
centre, and leaves room for the story around them.

**Leading lines.** Railings, lane markings, a line of lockers, the edge
of the court: real-world lines aim the eye. Position yourself so they
aim at your subject, not out of the frame.

**Fill the frame.** If a photograph is not working, you are probably
not close enough. Whatever does not add to the picture subtracts from
it — so move in until the subject is unmistakably the subject, and
check every edge for clutter before you shoot.

> [!tip] Zoom with your feet
> Almost every composition problem is solved by moving — closer, lower,
> around to the other side — rather than by touching the camera. The
> frame is chosen by where you stand.

These are guidelines, not laws; a photo that breaks one deliberately
can be the strongest on the page. Being able to say *why* it works is
the skill, and it is precisely what [[Photo Critique Practice]] and a
round of [[Which One Doesn't Belong]] train your eye to articulate.
