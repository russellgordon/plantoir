While the printer works through the queue, look at who made the
technologies this course uses. Some of the names are famous: Reginald
Fessenden, born in Quebec, among the first to send a human voice by
radio; Elsie MacGill, the first woman in Canada to earn an electrical
engineering degree, who oversaw the production of fighter aircraft in
Thunder Bay during the Second World War; the team that built the
Canadarm for the space shuttle; the Canadian filmmakers and engineers
behind IMAX. Some are not names at all, because the knowledge belonged
to a people rather than a patent holder. Inuit carved snow goggles with
narrow slits to cut the glare that causes snow blindness, and built
inuksuit — stone markers that could show a route or a good place to
fish, readable from far away, which is exactly what a sign is for.
Métis freighters built the Red River cart from wood and hide so it
could be repaired from what grew along the trail.[^1] Mi'kmaw Elder
Albert Marshall calls the habit of using Indigenous and Western ways of
knowing together *Etuaptmumk*, Two-Eyed Seeing: looking with the
strengths of each, and using both eyes at once.[^2]

Questions worth arguing about:

1. Why do we know Fessenden's name and not the name of whoever first
   cut a slit in a snow goggle? What does a patent record, and what
   does it leave out?
2. Elsie MacGill was the first woman in Canada to graduate in
   electrical engineering. What does a field lose when most of the
   people in it are alike — and do
   you see that in the shop corner of this room?
3. An inuksuk and your sign from [[The Sign Shop]] do the same job.
   Name two design decisions they share, and one they could never
   share.
4. The Red River cart was designed to be repaired, not replaced. Pick
   one object in this room that was designed the other way. Who
   benefits from that choice, and who pays for it?
5. Two-Eyed Seeing asks you to hold two ways of knowing at once. What
   would it look like on your crew's rig: whose knowledge of the
   problem did you use, and whose did you not think to ask for?
6. Is "Made in Canada" about where a thing was invented, where it is
   manufactured, or who it was made for? Argue for one.

Carry it back to practice: when your crew writes up [[The Rig]], name
whose knowledge you drew on — the user who explained the problem, the
maker whose model you remixed, the crew that found the right clearance
— the way [[Copyright and Fair Dealing]] asks you to credit a
photograph. Innovation is almost never one person; the record should
say so.

[^1]: The Canadian Encyclopedia has entries on the Red River cart and
    on the inuksuk, and both are a good first stop before you repeat
    anything on this page to someone else.
[^2]: Described by Elder Albert Marshall of Eskasoni, Unama'ki (Cape
    Breton), who brought the principle into the Integrative Science
    program at Cape Breton University in 2004.
