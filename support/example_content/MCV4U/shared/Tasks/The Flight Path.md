## What you are making

Your flight packet holds an airspeed your aircraft can hold, a wind
that does not care about your plans, and a destination bearing. Act
one is two-dimensional: find the **heading** that makes your air
vector plus the wind vector point along the desired track, and the
ground speed that results — arrows doing the algebra. Act two climbs
into three dimensions: the final approach is a **line in space**,
$\vec{r} = \vec{r}_0 + t\vec{m}$, and the runway lies in a **plane**.
You finish with the flight plan: heading and ground speed defended,
the approach line written in vector and parametric form, the
**touchdown point** where line meets plane — solved, then checked by
substitution — and the descent angle at which you cross it.

## Milestones

- [ ] The wind triangle drawn before any components appear: air
      vector, wind vector, ground vector, tip to tail per
      [[Adding and Scaling Vectors]]
- [ ] Heading and ground speed computed in components, then checked
      against the drawing's rough angles
- [ ] The approach line built from your packet's data in vector and
      parametric form, per [[Equations of Lines]]
- [ ] The touchdown point found, then verified by substituting it into
      the runway plane's scalar equation
- [ ] The descent angle argued with [[The Dot Product]] and the
      plane's normal

## How it is assessed

Per [[How Marks Work]], the reasoning is the product: a landing a
few metres off, with every step traceable and the error hunted down,
outranks perfect coordinates that appeared from nowhere. The flight
plan is presented at [[The Math Symposium]].

This task has no defence day of its own — it lands at the symposium — so
your individual question comes while you judge your own plan against the
criteria, on the last day of the write-up, when there is something worth
asking about. Each of you takes one, alone. The second piece that is
yours alone is your [[Math Journal]] entry on what the model ignores —
gusts, the wind's own third dimension, the pilot — written in class in
the period after the write-up is finished. Between them, the pair's flight plan and your own two
contributions are the whole of the evidence.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like in your work |
| --- | --- |
| Picture first | The wind triangle drawn before components |
| Arrows that add | The heading argued from a vector sum |
| Three forms fluent | The approach line written two ways, correctly |
| A checked landing | The intersection substituted back and verified |
| A stated angle | The descent angle traced to a dot product |
| Yours alone | Your own note on what the flight model ignores, and your own answer when I put a question to you rather than to your partner |

> [!success]- If the line and the plane refuse to meet
> Dot the line's direction vector with the plane's normal first: if
> that dot product is zero, your approach runs parallel to the
> runway — reread the packet, because that is a transcription error,
> not a mathematics one.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 16, the working period where the answer gets
checked against the diagram
  That day's agenda hands them the CATCH — an impossible heading is
  caught by the picture and not by the algebra. What it does not hand
  them is the REPAIR, and the repair is the thing worth watching. A pair
  that goes back to the triangle and rebuilds it tip to tail, with the
  wind the right way round, is doing C2.3; a pair that flips signs one at
  a time until a number looks plausible is doing arithmetic roulette. The
  finished flight plan carries the correct heading either way and will
  never tell you which.
  Going well: the triangle redrawn before anything is recalculated, and
  the sign fixing itself once the arrow points the right way.
  Stuck: three attempts at the same computation with a different sign
  each time, and the diagram untouched throughout.
  Record: two columns down the class list — rebuilt, hunted. Only the
  pairs whose answer went wrong will give you data, and that is fine;
  that is where the evidence is.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 12, at the conference already on that agenda
  This conference is formative, not the mark — the individuating question
  that counts for this task comes on Day 17, when there is a solved plan
  to ask about. What it is for is finding out, early and cheaply, which
  of the two of them is holding the picture. One question each.
  Ask: "Your partner drew the wind arrow. Show me why it points that way
  and not the opposite way — and tell me what your answer would look like
  if you had it backwards."
  Then: "Your packet gives the approach direction and a point on it. When
  you write that as a line, where is the aircraft when the parameter is
  zero, and what is the parameter counting?"
  A strong first answer separates a wind FROM the south from a wind
  TOWARDS it, and knows the mistake would mirror the heading about the
  track rather than shift it a little — C2.3, whose own sample problem is
  this aircraft, argued from the picture instead of the arithmetic. In
  the second, "where is the aircraft at zero" is C4.2 — the point the
  representation was built from — and "what is the parameter counting"
  is C4.7's geometric interpretation. A correctly written parametric
  equation proves neither is there.
  Record: two names and two answers, in the margin of your day plan, as
  you stand up. Ask early: on Day 12 neither answer can be read off the
  page, because the page is still a diagram.

The product is the flight plan itself — the heading, the approach line,
the touchdown point substituted back and interpreted — on display at the
symposium on Unit 4, Day 21.
