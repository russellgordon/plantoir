Method on [[Media Texts and Their Arguments]].

**1.** A documentary opens with two minutes of archival footage under a
slow piano score, then a voice-over: "For decades, nobody asked." What
has the opening done before any evidence arrives?

> [!success]- Answer 1
> Established grievance and sympathy, and framed the film's subject as
> neglect rather than as dispute. "Nobody asked" is a claim, delivered as
> narration, which is where documentary claims are hardest to check.

**2.** Two articles report the same council decision. One quotes four
residents and one councillor; the other quotes three councillors and one
resident. Neither misquotes anybody. Name the difference and what each
argues.

> [!success]- Answer 2
> Source selection. The first frames the decision as something done to
> people; the second as a decision made by an accountable body. Selection
> is the argument — see [[Reading Non-Fiction]].

**3.** A podcast presents a reconstruction — actors reading a transcript
— without saying so until the credits. Is that dishonest?

> [!success]- Answer 3
> Defensible answers both ways, and the useful move is to name the
> standard: a listener cannot see quotation marks, so audio conventions
> have to do that work. If a reconstruction is indistinguishable from a
> recording, the form has borrowed authority it did not earn.

**4.** What would a documentary made by somebody with the opposite
conclusion, from the same material, look like?

> [!success]- Answer 4
> Different interviews foregrounded, a different sequence of events, and
> — most tellingly — a different definition of when the story starts.
> Start dates are arguments.

**5.** Take a text you like and write the "what is left out" paragraph
the task asks for.

> [!success]- Answer 5
> No fixed answer. The test: does your answer name people who would have
> spoken, and questions that would have been awkward? "It left out the
> other side" is not an answer; it is a placeholder.

**Industry.** Take any one of this unit's four texts and answer in two
sentences each: who paid for it, and what rule — a regulation, a
disclosure requirement, a libel risk, or the absence of any of them —
shaped what it could say?

> [!success]- Answer: what a full response does
> It names a payer specifically enough to be wrong about, not "a company",
> and it names a constraint that would have changed the text if it were
> different. "Nothing constrains a podcast" is a complete answer when it
> is followed by what that permits.
