Nothing dramatic happens in the sentences of [[Borders]], even when
something dramatic is happening in the scene. King's narrator describes a
tense standoff at a border crossing in the same plain, level voice he
uses to describe breakfast. That flatness is not the absence of style —
it *is* the style, and it is doing real work on how you read the story's
tension. This page names the tools King is using.

## Diction

**Diction** is word choice — and more specifically, the *kind* of words a
writer reaches for. Two words can point at almost the same thing and
still carry very different weight:

> [!example] Denotation versus connotation
> "Firm" and "stubborn" can describe the same behaviour. Denotation — the
> dictionary meaning — is nearly identical. Connotation — the feeling a
> word carries beyond its definition — is not: "firm" leans approving,
> "stubborn" leans critical. A writer's diction tells you, often before
> you consciously notice it, how you are meant to feel about what is
> being described.

Diction also has register — formal or informal, plain or ornate,
specialized or everyday — and a writer's choice of register is itself
meaningful. King's narrator in [[Borders]] uses almost entirely plain,
everyday diction, which is part of why the story's tension feels so
understated on the surface and so serious underneath.

## Tone

**Tone** is the writer's or narrator's attitude toward the subject, and
it is built largely out of diction plus the details a writer chooses to
include or leave out. Tone is not the same as the events of the plot: a
story can describe something upsetting in a calm tone, and that mismatch
is very often the point, exactly as it is in [[Borders]].

## Style

**Style** is the pattern all of this makes across a whole piece — the
writer's characteristic diction, sentence length, structure, and tone,
taken together. A writer's style is recognisable precisely because these
choices repeat and reinforce each other.
