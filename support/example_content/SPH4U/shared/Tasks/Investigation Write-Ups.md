> [!abstract] At a glance
> **Format:** five write-ups spread from Unit 1 to Unit 4, each written in
> class in a period set aside for it and handed in before the bell.
> **Individually**, from data your group collected. **Assessed on:**
> Thinking and Investigation, Communication, and Knowledge wherever the
> physics has to be explained rather than calculated.

Ten investigations run in this course. Five of them are written up
properly, and those five are marked. They are the part of your seventy per
cent that arrives steadily rather than in four large pieces — which is
what lets a report card say something about your most recent and most
consistent work, rather than about four afternoons.

## The five, and when they are written

| Write-up | Investigation | Written in class on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One | [[The Flying Pig]] | Unit 1, Day 11 |
| Two | [[Collisions in One Dimension]] | Unit 2, Day 9 |
| Three | [[Mapping an Electric Field]] | Unit 3, Day 6 |
| Four | [[Deflecting a Charged Particle]] | Unit 3, Day 9 |
| Five | [[Double Slit with a Laser]] | Unit 4, Day 4 |

The first one is a **full report** in the shape set out on
[[Writing a Lab Report]] — purpose through conclusion, because you should
write one complete one before you are allowed to skip the parts that are
obvious. The other four are the **analysis and conclusion only**: your
observations, what you made of them, and what you can claim.

Every investigation page carries its own question, method and safety
notes and says what its analysis has to answer. Nothing on this page
replaces that; this page is only the standard all five are marked
against.

> [!note] The one that is not marked
> [[Projectile Range]] is written up on Unit 1, Day 4 as the practice
> run, using these same six rows, and it carries no mark. That is on
> purpose: the first time you meet a standard you should be allowed to
> miss it.

## Written in class, and why

Each of these is written here, in a period set aside for it, with your
data still in front of you and me in the room. That is deliberate. A
write-up done at home three nights later is a write-up about what you can
remember; the thing worth marking — what you made of the numbers while
they were still strange — has gone by then. It also means the help is
there while you need it, which is the whole point of doing the marked
work in the room. Ask.

Four of the five are written in the period immediately after the bench
work, while the apparatus is still a memory rather than a note.
[[Deflecting a Charged Particle]] is the one exception: it runs on Unit 3,
Day 7 and is written on Day 9, with a consolidation day in between.

## Individually, from shared data

The bench work is done in pairs or threes and the raw data belongs to the
group. **The write-up is yours alone**, from the analysis onward. Two
people may hand in the same table of readings; two people handing in the
same analysis have handed in one piece of work between them.

## Success criteria

The same six rows every time, so that by January you can run them from
memory. Read your own work against them before you hand it in — see
[[Judging Your Own Work]]. The rows do not change, which is exactly what
makes improvement across five write-ups visible.

| Quality | What it looks like in your write-up |
| --- | --- |
| Observations kept clean | Tables with a unit on every column, the raw readings kept beside anything calculated from them, and the note that the string slipped on trial 3. No interpretation, and nothing quietly dropped |
| A graph that does some work | Axes labelled with units, a line or curve whose slope or intercept means something physical, and a sentence saying what |
| Symbols before numbers | The algebra carried to an expression, substituted once, and the answer given to a number of significant figures you could defend |
| A diagram drawn to convention | Free-body diagram, resolved components, field lines, ray path — labelled, and with the positive direction stated and then kept |
| Uncertainty that came from somewhere | The dominant source named, with a number, and what that number does to the conclusion. "Human error" names nothing |
| A conclusion the data allows | One paragraph answering the purpose, set against what the physics predicted, and honest enough to say "these data cannot separate the two" when that is the answer |

The fifth row is where Grade 12 separates from Grade 11. "There may have
been experimental error" is a sentence, not a finding. Naming the step
that could have misled you, and saying how far it could have moved the
number, is a finding — and it is the row most people lose most often.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

Five write-ups over four units is the steadiest product evidence in the
course, and the two bench days either side of them are the cheapest
place to gather the other two kinds. Watch on a day the data is being
made; talk on a day it is being written up, with the numbers on the desk
between you.

OBSERVE — Unit 3, Day 5, the conductive-paper bench
  Watch for: whether the probe ever leaves the space between the
  electrodes. Out at the edges of the sheet the potential barely changes
  and the readings wander, and that is precisely where a pair either
  takes the awkward readings or continues the pattern by eye. The
  finished map is a set of smooth curves and looks much the same either
  way, unless a pair happened to record numbers out there — so this
  corroborates the first criteria row rather than being hidden by it,
  and corroboration is worth the two minutes.
  Going well: someone says the reading is 1.8 to 2.1 all the way across
  here, and marks a band rather than a confident line.
  Stuck: four tidy equipotentials in the middle, nothing beyond them,
  and the outer curves drawn last.
  Record: a tick per bench on a sketch of the sheet, probed or not
  probed. One walk around the room.

TALK — Unit 2, Day 9, while they write up the collisions
  Sit down where the momentum table is already filled in.
  Ask: "All four of your momentum totals came out low, and none came out
  high. Does a pattern like that let you rule anything out?"
  Then: "What number would have made you say kinetic energy WAS
  conserved in the sticky collision — and how close is close enough?"
  A strong answer separates an offset that always leans one way from
  scatter that leans both, and can name something that would produce the
  first: a track that is not level, a photogate catching the flag
  off-centre, a mass taken from the label rather than the balance. On
  the second it sets a threshold against their own uncertainty instead
  of answering "it just wasn't". That is A1.8 heard — evidence weighed
  against the prediction, with the sources of error named — and a
  write-up can carry a whole paragraph about error without any of that
  thinking having happened.
  Record: three columns — leaned one way, scattered both ways, did not
  distinguish. Carry the third column to Unit 3, Day 6 and start there.

The product evidence is the five write-ups, handed in at the end of the
period each one is written in.
