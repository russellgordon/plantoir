This course teaches physical geography, and it teaches a set of
transferable skills that are worth naming out loud — partly because you
will need to describe them to somebody outside a classroom, and partly
because naming a skill is how you notice you are getting better at it.

## What you are actually practising

| Skill | Where it happens here | Where else it is used |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reading graphic text | Climographs, hydrographs, contour maps | Any technical or trades manual |
| Spatial reasoning | Overlays, profiles, watershed delineation | Surveying, logistics, planning, medicine |
| Measurement and recording | Field notebook, transects | Laboratory work, inspection, construction |
| Judging evidence | [[Judging a Source]], revised disaster figures | Every consequential decision you make |
| Communicating to an audience | Reports, seminars, the culminating brief | Every job that exists |
| Working with others outdoors | Field days, paired transects | Field, trades and emergency work |

Several of these appear in the Ontario Skills Passport as essential
skills — document use, numeracy, oral communication, decision making,
problem solving — which means an employer already has language for them.
Use that language.

## Careers a geography background supports

Conservation officer. Land surveyor. Emergency preparedness coordinator.
Urban or land-use planner. Civil engineer. Insurance adjuster. Hydrologist
and hydrogeologist. Seismologist. Meteorologist. Volcanologist.
Oceanographer. GIS analyst. Forest technician. Agrologist.

Two things are worth noticing about that list. It contains far more
field, technical and trades routes than the word "geography" suggests,
and almost every entry pairs geography with something else — engineering,
law, computing, agriculture, public health. The pairing is the career.

## Your entry for the portfolio

Write half a page, in class in the last week of the course, that does
three things: names two skills from the table you can evidence with
specific work you did; describes one occasion outside this classroom
where you used something from this course; and names one career from the
list, or beyond it, that you now know enough about to say why it
interests you or does not.

It is the closing entry of [[The Field and Map Portfolio]], and the
period it is written in is on the schedule, so bring the work you intend
to point at.

> [!tip] Evidence, not adjectives
> "I improved my mapping" is worth nothing to a reader. "My first field
> sketch had no scale and no north arrow; my hazard map has both, plus
> a source line for every layer" is the same claim with proof attached.

The maps live in [[A Map of Your Own]]; the observations live in
[[Your Field Notebook]].
