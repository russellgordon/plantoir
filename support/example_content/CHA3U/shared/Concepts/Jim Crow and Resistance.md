The Fifteenth Amendment, ratified in 1870, says the right to vote "shall not
be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of
race, color, or previous condition of servitude." Within a generation most
Black men in the South could not vote, and nothing in the amendment had been
repealed. How a constitutional guarantee was made inoperative without being
changed is the central problem of this period.

## How the rules were built

The National Archives describes the mechanism plainly: "Literacy tests for
the vote, 'grandfather clauses' excluding from the franchise all whose
ancestors had not voted in the 1860s, and other devices to disenfranchise
African Americans were written into the laws of former Confederate states."
None of these rules mentions race. Each was administered by officials who
did, and poll taxes and closed party primaries completed the work.

In 1896 the Supreme Court upheld segregated railway accommodation in *Plessy
v. Ferguson*, and "separate but equal" became the constitutional cover for
segregation in schools, transport, hospitals and courts. The facilities were
separate. They were not equal, and the budgets prove it. Economics enforced
the same order: sharecropping left families working land they did not own and
settling accounts with a landlord who kept the books, and convict leasing
turned arrests under vagrancy laws into forced labour sold to private
companies — the punishment clause of the Thirteenth Amendment doing the work
described in [[Slavery]].

> Racial terror lynching was public, frequently announced in advance, and
> almost never prosecuted. The Equal Justice Initiative states that its
> researchers "documented 4,075 racial terror lynchings of African Americans
> in Alabama, Arkansas, Florida, Georgia, Kentucky, Louisiana, Mississippi,

> and 1950 — at least 800 more lynchings of Black people in these states than

## Resistance, in public and at cost

Ida B. Wells investigated lynching as a journalist, established that the
accusations offered as justification were usually false, published the
evidence, and was driven out of Memphis for it. Booker T. Washington built
Tuskegee and argued for economic self-reliance first; W. E. B. Du Bois argued
that political rights could not wait and helped found the NAACP in 1909. Their
disagreement was strategic, both had followings, and treating either as the
correct answer is a poor reading of a real debate.

Millions of people left the South for northern and western cities — a
migration that produced the neighbourhoods in which blues, jazz and the
Harlem Renaissance developed. Separate baseball leagues existed because the
established leagues excluded Black players, and became institutions in their
own right.

## Citizenship, granted and suspended

Rights moved in both directions here. Congress extended citizenship to Native
Americans born in the United States in 1924, decades after the Fourteenth
Amendment, and several states still kept them from voting. In 1942, under
Executive Order 9066, approximately 122,000 men, women and children of
Japanese ancestry were forcibly removed from the west coast and confined; the
National Archives records that "nearly 70,000 of the evacuees were American
citizens." Citizenship did not protect them.
