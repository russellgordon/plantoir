Not all movement is migration in the same sense, and lumping it together is
the commonest error on this topic. Between 1791 and 1877 people arrived in
the United States by choice, were carried into it in chains, were driven
across it at gunpoint, and escaped out of it northward. Those are four
different phenomena, and the same country produced all four at once.

## Four kinds of movement

Immigration from Europe rose sharply from the 1840s. Irish families left
during a famine in which state policy determined who ate, and arrived with
nothing, in cities that had no housing for them. German-speaking migrants
came in comparable numbers, many with some capital or a trade, and settled
more widely across the Midwest. The difference in how the two groups were
received had less to do with their conduct than with religion and with
poverty: Irish Catholics met organized hostility that German Protestants
largely did not.

Forced migration ran alongside it. The domestic slave trade moved enslaved
people from the Upper South to the cotton frontier. Removal drove Native
nations west of the Mississippi. Both were compulsory, both broke families,
and both were carried out under colour of law.

And people escaped. The Underground Railroad carried people north, and after
1850 increasingly all the way into Canada, because federal law had made the
northern states unsafe.

## Nativism, and the rules it produced

Hostility to newcomers organized itself into politics. A movement built on
opposition to Catholic immigration became a significant electoral force in
the 1850s, campaigning on longer waiting periods for naturalization and on
the claim that Catholics could not be loyal citizens.[^nativism]

After the war the rules were rewritten twice in quick succession, in opposite
directions. The Naturalization Act of 1870 extended naturalization to people
of African descent — while leaving in place the exclusion of others, so that
Asian immigrants remained ineligible to become citizens. The Page Act of 1875
restricted entry in terms that were enforced overwhelmingly against Chinese
women, and it is where federal immigration restriction as an administrative
practice begins.

In the same years southern legislatures wrote Black Codes controlling the
labour, movement and contracts of newly freed people, and the Ku Klux Klan
formed as an organization using violence to reverse Reconstruction. Rights on
paper and rights in practice separated almost immediately, and the gap is the
subject.

Follow the exclusion forward in [[Immigration and Exclusion]], and use
[[Historical Significance]] to decide which of these rules mattered longest.

[^nativism]: Watch the vocabulary in nativist sources. Immigrants are
described as a *flood*, a *tide*, an *invasion* — language that turns people
into weather and removes the need to consider them individually. The same
metaphors are still in circulation, which is a reason to notice where they
came from and how they were used. Practise it in
[[Newspapers and Print Culture]].
