This unit puts several dance forms into your body rather than onto a
worksheet. You will spend real studio time on the basic movement vocabulary
of each — not enough to call yourself trained in any of them, but enough to
feel what each form values, which is something you cannot get from watching.

Every form on this list is a living practice with a history and with people
who are expert in it right now. None of them is a costume you put on for a
period and take off.

## Four we sample

| Form | Where it comes from | What it asks of you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ballet | Italian courts, then France | Turnout, lift, exact line |
| Jazz | African American social dance | Isolation, syncopation |
| Hip hop | The Bronx, New York, 1970s | Groove, grounded footwork |
| Bharatanatyam | Tamil Nadu, southern India | Geometry, rhythmic feet |

Two cautions about that table. First, it is a summary, and summaries flatten
things — "ballet" covers several distinct training systems that disagree
with each other. Second, watch out for labels that name a continent or a
country instead of a form. There is no such thing as "African dance" any
more than there is "European dance": there are hundreds of traditions
belonging to particular peoples, and using the specific name is the least
you can do.

## What they share

Practise them side by side and the family resemblances start showing.

- A **pulse** that the movement agrees with, argues with, or plays against.
- A relationship to **gravity** — every form has an opinion about whether
  weight goes down into the floor or lifts away from it.
- **Repetition**, so that a step can be recognised and passed on.
- Transmission **body to body**, from someone who already knows it.
- A community that agrees what doing it well looks like.

Those shared things are why a phrase learned in one form can be described
using the same vocabulary as a phrase in another. What differs is what the
form counts as beautiful.

Some dances — ceremonial, sacred, or belonging to a specific community —
are ones we study and watch rather than perform, and regalia is not costume.
[[Does a Dance Belong to a Culture|Does a Dance Belong to a Culture?]] is the seminar where we argue this out
properly. [[Where a Form Comes From]] traces one form's development in
depth, and [[Three Forms We Practise]] covers the three we return to all
year. [[Four Forms on Film]] has a lesson in each of the four to watch, with
questions to watch it by.
