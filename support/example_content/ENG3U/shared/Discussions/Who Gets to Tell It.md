## The question

Thomas King argues that the stories North America tells about Indigenous
people have mostly been told by other people, for other purposes. What
does that ask of a reader — and of a writer?

## Bring

Two passages from [[The Inconvenient Indian]] and one from
[[Monkey Beach]] where the question of who is telling matters.

## Positions to consider

| Position | The argument | The objection |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Only insiders can tell it | Centuries of misrepresentation with material consequences | Communities are not single voices, and the rule can trap writers inside one identity |
| Anyone may write anything | Imagination is the art | The right to write is not the right to be published, taught, or believed |
| It depends on relationship and permission | The difference between an account given and one taken | Permission from whom, and who enforces it? |
| It depends on what the telling displaces | A story that crowds out a community's own telling does harm a respectful one does not | Hard to know in advance, and easy to use as cover |

## The second question, which is harder

Who decides what gets published, taught, prized, and put on a course
list? This class is a case in point: the texts on
[[Reading/index|the reading page]] were chosen by somebody. Ask what that
choice assumes.

## Afterwards

One paragraph, for your journal and possibly for
[[The Comparative Response]]: what King's argument asks of you
specifically, as a reader of the novel we are reading.
