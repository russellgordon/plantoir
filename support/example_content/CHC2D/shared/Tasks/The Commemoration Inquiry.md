**The culminating task. Eight periods at the end of Unit 4, ending with
a recommendation delivered to someone who could act on it.** Pairs or
threes.

Every community decides what to remember. A monument, a street name, a
plaque, a school name, a day of recognition, an apology — or an absence
where one of those might have been. This task asks you to investigate
one, and to say what should happen to it.

## The eight periods

| Period | What it is for |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Choosing: what commemorations exist here, and which is arguable |
| 2 | Scoping: the question, and what evidence would answer it |
| 3 | Evidence: archives, records, local reporting, and who to ask; each of you opens an evidence log |
| 4 | Evidence: continued, including a site visit or an interview |
| 5 | Analysis: whose history is told, whose is absent, and why |
| 6 | The recommendation drafted, with its costs |
| 7 | Rehearsal, and a hostile question from another group |
| 8 | Delivery to your audience, with questions |

## What you deliver

- **An inquiry report** (about 1,200 words): what the commemoration is,
  how it came to be, what it says and omits, and your recommendation.
- **A presentation** of twelve minutes to a real audience — a
  councillor, a board trustee, a museum or historical society, a
  community organization, or the school's administration.
- **A one-page leave-behind** they keep.

You produce all three together and you are marked separately. Each of you
writes and signs a named section of the report, and each of you keeps an
**evidence log** — one page, started in period 3 and added to as you go,
recording what you found, where, and what it turned out to show. Those two
things are your mark. What happens at the delivery belongs to the group and
to the people who came; it is not something I sit at the back and score.

## The recommendation

It may be: keep it as it is; keep it and add context; move it; rename or
re-dedicate it; remove it; or create something that does not exist. Any
of those can earn full marks. What is marked is whether the evidence
supports it and whether you have taken the strongest opposing view
seriously.

## How it is marked

Every row below is applied to your own named section of the report and to
your evidence log, not to the group's work as a whole. Nobody here is
marked for what somebody else wrote.

| Criterion | What earns the marks |
| --- | --- |
| Investigation | Real sources, including local ones, properly cited |
| Significance | You argue why this matters, using the concept rather than the word |
| Perspectives | Those who would disagree are represented fairly and answered |
| Judgement | A recommendation someone could actually act on, with its cost named |
| Conduct | The delivery answers what the audience actually asked, and the leave-behind is something they can use |

> [!warning] Living communities are involved in this
> A commemoration argument is usually about somebody's ancestor,
> somebody's neighbourhood, or somebody's grief. Do the research before
> you form the view, and write as though the people concerned will read
> it — because you are going to hand it to them.

[[Commemoration and Public Memory]] is the concept, and the seminars
[[Does an Apology Matter|Does an Apology Matter?]] and [[What Should a Country Remember|What Should a Country Remember?]] are
where the arguments get tested first.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 18, the analysis period
  By now every group has an answer it has started to like, and every file
  holds at least one document that argues the other way, because you only
  approved subjects that were arguable.
  Watch for: what happens to that document. Not whether they find the
  absences — item 2 tells them to do that. What the awkward evidence costs
  them.
  Going well: it is read aloud to the group, and the recommendation moves
  or acquires a condition.
  Stuck: it is set aside as "not really about our question", or the
  question narrows without the document ever being named as the reason.
  Narrowing BECAUSE the evidence will not carry the wider question is what
  Day 17's conference told them to do — what you are listening for is
  whether the reason is said out loud.
  Record: one line per group on your day plan — moved, narrowed, has not
  met it yet. The third group is the one to reach on Day 19.
  The Perspectives row asks them to represent the other side fairly, and a
  good group will. What the row cannot show is whether the other side ever
  had a chance of winning, and that is A1.6: evaluating and synthesizing
  findings to reach a judgement. The report shows a conclusion. It does not
  show what the conclusion cost.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 17, the conferences on what the evidence supports
  The obvious two questions are already spent. Day 12's seminar sent them
  to find a local commemoration and ask who put it there and what argument
  they were making, and the Judgement row asks for the cost of the
  recommendation. Go past both.
  Ask: "There was a version of this that lost. What was it, and where is
  it written down?"
  Then: "Who has to be persuaded for this to happen, and what is the one
  sentence they will object to?"
  A strong first answer produces a rejected proposal, a minority on a
  council, a petition that failed — evidence that the thing in front of
  them was chosen over something else. Where the subject is a tragedy or a
  human rights violation, that is E3.4, which carries no date restriction
  and covers most of what a group will choose. Where the commemoration
  honours a person or an organization, the content code depends on WHEN
  they contributed: E3.1 only from 1982, D3.1 for 1945 to 1982, and for a
  cenotaph or a Victorian benefactor neither applies — record that one as
  A1.6 instead, which is about the judgement rather than the period. Decide
  which before you write it down; a code chosen by subject rather than by
  date is the easiest thing on this page to get wrong.
  A strong second answer names a person rather than "the public", and can
  say the objecting sentence out loud.
  Both questions are about the record and about the decision, never about
  the community the commemoration concerns. Where a group is investigating
  something that happened to people they know, ask what they want to do
  with what they find before you ask them anything else.
  Record: one line per group on the conference list you are holding. The
  rejected version and the name of whoever must be persuaded are enough.

SEMINARS — Unit 3, Day 12 and Unit 4, Day 12
  Two seminars do the argument of this task before the task exists, and
  both are worth recording even though you are chairing and cannot watch.
  "Who Gets to Tell the Story?" asks whose accounts became the record —
  listen for a student naming a specific person or organization whose
  account survived and one whose did not, which is D3.1. "What Should a
  Country Remember?" is E3.4 almost word for word. Do not ask the seminar
  questions again in the Day 17 conference; ask what a student's own
  subject does to the position they took here.
  Record: a phrase per student, on the class list, both days. Keep them —
  by Day 17 you will have forgotten who argued what, and the students will
  not have.

Gather neither kind of evidence on Day 20 or Day 21. The rehearsal and the
delivery are yours to run, and a teacher taking notes at the back of
somebody's first professional presentation changes what they are watching.

The product evidence is the report, the leave-behind, and the delivery on
Unit 4, Day 21.
