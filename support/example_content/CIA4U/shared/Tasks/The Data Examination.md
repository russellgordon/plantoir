**The final evaluation. Three hours, in the examination period.**
Individual.

An unseen dossier of economic evidence, and the whole course brought to
bear on it.

## What it looks like

You are given a dossier of about eight documents on one current economic
issue: two or three statistical tables, a chart, an excerpt from a
government release, a submission from an interested party, a news report,
and a passage from an economist. You have not seen them before. They will
not agree with each other.

You answer in three parts.

## Part one — the sources, about 45 minutes

Assess the dossier. For each document: what is measured, by whom, over what
period, and what would make it unreliable here. The submission from an
interested party is not disqualified by being interested — say what in it
you can use and what you cannot, and why.

## Part two — the analysis, about 75 minutes

Interpret the evidence. Read the tables properly — level or rate,
seasonally adjusted or not, real or nominal, and compared with what.
Choose the tools the dossier actually calls for; a paper that applies every
model in the course is a paper that could not decide. Then use the four
concepts of economic thinking — economic significance, cause and effect,
stability and variability, economic perspective — as the structure of the
argument rather than as vocabulary sprinkled through it.

## Part three — the judgement, about 45 minutes

Two shorter pieces of writing. First, take one economic theorist we
studied and say how they would read this dossier, and what in it they
would find difficult. Second, say how social movements or social justice
organisations have addressed an inequality of this kind, and how
effectively — criteria first, then the assessment.

The remaining time is for reading and planning, and you should use it. The
strongest papers spend twenty-five minutes on the dossier before writing a
sentence.

## What is being examined

| Criterion | What it means here |
| --- | --- |
| Source criticism | You can say what a document is good for, not just whether you trust it |
| Data reading | You get the units, the base and the comparison right, quickly |
| Selection | You choose the right tool and justify the choice |
| Conceptual structure | The four concepts organise the argument |
| Judgement | The conclusion follows from the evidence, including the evidence against |
| Communication | Structured, plain, and readable under pressure |

## Preparing for it

The review periods before the examination are built for this: we work old
dossiers against the clock and compare readings. Bring your notebook —
[[Your Economics Notebook]] is the single most useful thing you can have
practised, because the habit it builds is exactly the habit part one
tests. [[Judging an Economic Claim]] and
[[Index Numbers and Real Versus Nominal]] are the two pages to reread.

> [!tip] Practise the first twenty-five minutes
> Everybody can write for three hours. The difference between papers is
> made in the first twenty-five minutes, deciding what the dossier is
> actually about and which two documents carry the argument. Do that
> against a clock, several times, before the day.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

The examination itself is invigilated, so the observation and the
conversation both have to happen in the review periods. That is not a
compromise: the reading and planning that decide this paper are the part
the script never shows, and the review days are the only time you can
watch them.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 20, the third agenda item, where the first
twenty-five minutes of the examination are practised against a clock
  Watch for: who is annotating and who is already writing. A finished
  script shows the argument that came out; nothing in it shows whether
  the student read eight documents or started composing after two.
  Going well: the dossier is marked up, two documents are circled as the
  ones carrying the argument, and not a sentence has been written.
  Stuck: a first paragraph is finished and documents six to eight are
  still face down.
  Record: two sweeps of the room ten minutes apart, a tick and a cross
  per name. Tell the ones with two crosses before they leave — this is
  the last period where telling them changes anything.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 19, while the class argues the models in pairs and
you circulate
  Ask: "Two documents in the dossier we worked yesterday disagreed about
  the same number. What is it about the way one of them was produced
  that would make you weight it below the other?"
  Then: "There was a campaign group in that dossier. If it had got
  everything it asked for, which line of which table would have moved?"
  The first is A1.3 exactly as it is written — assessing credibility "by
  considering how the data or information was constructed". A weak answer
  says one source is biased; a strong one names the construction: who was
  sampled, over what period, on whose definition, and paid for by whom.
  A script cannot show you the difference, because both students write
  the same sentence about weighting.
  The second is E3.3: a student who can connect a movement's demands to a
  measurable line has criteria for effectiveness; one who cannot will
  write the part three that says a campaign "raised awareness".
  Record: on the review list, a tick where a real table was named.

The product evidence is the examination script itself.
