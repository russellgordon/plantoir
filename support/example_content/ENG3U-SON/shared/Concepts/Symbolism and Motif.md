The black box in [[The Lottery]] is falling apart — splintered, faded,
never replaced even though everyone agrees a new one could easily be
built. Jackson spends real space on that detail, and by the time the
story ends you understand why: the box is not just a box. This page names
what you were already doing when you noticed that.

## Symbol

A **symbol** is a concrete object, image, or action that stands for
something beyond its literal self. Not everything in a story is
symbolic — most objects are just objects — but a symbol earns the label
when a text gives it unusual weight: repeated attention, a detail that
would otherwise be pointless, a moment where a character reacts to it
more strongly than the object itself seems to justify. The black box
qualifies on all three counts.[^1]

[^1]: What the box is a symbol *of* is not fixed in advance — tradition,
    complicity, the weight of the past, the way a community protects a
    ritual by refusing to examine it. A strong reading argues for one of
    these using evidence from the text; a weak one just asserts "the box
    represents tradition" and stops there.

## Motif

A **motif** is a symbol, image, or idea that recurs — it shows up more
than once, in different forms, and the pattern of its repetition is where
the meaning accumulates. A single symbol can carry weight on its own; a
motif builds that weight across a whole text, so that its final
appearance means more than its first one did, because you are carrying
every earlier appearance with you.

## Where this returns

This idea comes back explicitly in Unit 2: ice and hockey work as a
sustained motif across [[Indian Horse]], and by the time you reach that
novel you will already have the vocabulary to trace how a single image
can carry a whole book's meaning. Watch, in both texts, for the moment a
symbol's meaning shifts or complicates itself — that shift is usually the
point.
