Alice Munro was born in 1931 in Wingham, Ontario, the daughter of a fox
farmer — the same trade the story you're about to read is built around. She
is the only Canadian writer to have won the Nobel Prize in Literature
(2013), and the Swedish Academy named her "a master of the contemporary
short story" when it gave her the award. "Boys and Girls" first appeared in
1964 and was collected four years later in *Dance of the Happy Shades*, her
first book and the winner of a Governor General's Award.

## Why we're reading it

This is the first text of the course, and it was chosen for that job on
purpose. Nothing about it is difficult to follow on the surface — a girl
helps her father on a fox farm, feeds the animals, does chores with her
younger brother — but everything under the surface is doing work. It's a
short, exact demonstration of what fiction can do that a plot summary
can't: show a change happening to someone before she has the words for it
herself.

## What to watch for on a first read

The narrator is never named. Notice what she's allowed to do at the start
of the story and what she's expected to do by the end, and notice that
nobody sits her down and explains the shift to her — it arrives through
small moments, one after another. Pay attention, too, to how her brother
Laird is treated differently for doing exactly the same things she does.

The story is told in the first person, looking back — an adult narrator
remembering a specific year of her childhood. The concept you'll meet the
class after this one, [[Point of View and Narrative Distance]], gives you
the vocabulary for exactly that kind of remembering.

This is also the first day you'll use [[Your Reading Life]] — a place to
catch your own reaction to a text while it's still fresh, before class
discussion smooths it into something tidier.

> [!info]- The title, read twice
> "Boys and Girls" sounds like a simple label at the start of the story.
> By the end, ask yourself whether it still reads as a description or
> whether it has started to read as a verdict.[^1]

[^1]: The story's own last line plays on exactly this — worth returning to
    once you've finished, rather than reading ahead for it now.
