> [!abstract] At a glance
> **Format:** a two-page brief written for a family, not for me.
> **Pairs**, with part 4 written by each of you separately.
> **Due:** end of Unit 3. **Assessed on:** Application, Communication.

You are given a pedigree and a condition. Work out the inheritance
pattern, calculate the risks, and explain them in language a worried
person can follow.

## What to produce

1. **The pattern**, with your reasoning: autosomal or sex-linked, dominant
   or recessive, and what in the pedigree rules out the alternatives.
2. **The genotypes** you can determine, and an honest note about the ones
   you cannot.
3. **The risk** for the next generation, as a probability, with the
   Punnett square that produced it.
4. **The explanation**, in plain words. No jargon that is not defined the
   first time it appears.
5. **What testing could and could not tell them** — see
   [[Genetic Technologies]].

## The standard for part 4

A reader with no biology since Grade 10 should finish it knowing what the
number means and what it does not. "One in four" needs the sentence that
stops it being read as "one of our four children".

**Part 4 is written by each of you separately**, and handed in with both
versions attached. Parts 1 to 3 are the pair's reasoning and one copy is
enough; the explanation is where the marks in Communication live, and an
explanation is not something two people can be judged on jointly. Part 5
is the pair's again.

## Success criteria

Published here on launch day, and used again with
[[Judging Your Own Work]] on the working period before you hand it in.

| Quality | What it looks like in your brief |
| --- | --- |
| The pattern is ruled in by ruling out | For each alternative you rejected, you name the individual in the pedigree who rules it out |
| Genotypes, with the gaps admitted | The ones you can determine are stated; the ones you cannot are said to be undetermined, not guessed |
| The risk has its working | A Punnett square appears, and the probability comes from it |
| Each birth is independent | Nothing in the brief implies the family has "used up" a risk |
| Plain words, defined once | No term appears undefined; a reader with Grade 10 biology finishes it understanding the number |
| What the test cannot say | The limits are stated as plainly as the result |
| The work is credited | The researchers or clinicians behind the test are named, with what each contributed, and sourced |

> [!warning] Real families
> Pedigrees in this task are constructed, not real. If any of this touches
> your own family, say so privately and we will handle the task
> differently — that is a normal request, not a favour.

## Whose work this rests on

Name the researchers or clinicians whose work made the test or treatment
you describe possible, and say what each contributed. Where the work is
Canadian, say so — the gene behind cystic fibrosis was identified by a
team led from Toronto, and students routinely assume this kind of
research happens somewhere else. One short paragraph, sourced.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 3, Day 14, the working period on the pedigree
  The criteria ask the brief to name, for each pattern they rejected, the
  individual who rules it out. Both routes to that page produce it: the
  student who ruled out first, and the student who recognised the pattern
  and then found an individual to justify it afterwards. What the page
  cannot record is the ORDER, and the order is the reasoning.
  Watch for: a finger landing on an individual BEFORE a pattern is named.
  Going well: pencil marks on the pedigree, and a sentence that starts
  "it cannot be".
  Stuck: the pattern announced in the first thirty seconds, then a hunt.
  Record: two columns — ruled out, recognised. Add a star for anyone who
  changed their mind partway; that is the strongest thing you will see
  all period, and it never appears in a finished brief.

TALK — Unit 3, Day 15, the working period on whose work the test rests on
  Ask: "What would this family have been told about the same risk
  twenty-five years ago, and what specifically changed?"
  Then: "If the test came back positive, what decision does that actually
  open up for them — and what does it not?"
  A strong answer weighs a technique by what it changes for somebody,
  rather than by how recent it is. That is D1.2 heard in conversation.
  A2.2 is met IN WRITING by the sourced paragraph on Day 15; this
  conversation corroborates it and does not replace it. In a task done in
  pairs, this is also where you find out which partner did the reasoning.
  Record: one line each, and whose contribution they named first.

The product evidence is the brief, handed in at the start of Unit 4 —
including the two separately written versions of part 4.
