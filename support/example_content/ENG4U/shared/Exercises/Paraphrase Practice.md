Method on [[Reading Shakespeare]]. Paraphrase first, then unfold.

**1.** *"O that this too too solid flesh would melt, / Thaw, and resolve
itself into a dew."*

> [!success]- Answer 1
> I wish this all too solid body would melt, thaw, and dissolve into
> dew.
> Three verbs for one process. Ask what the accumulation does that one
> verb would not — see [[Shakespeare's Language]] on hendiadys.

**2.** *"There is nothing either good or bad, but thinking makes it so."*

> [!success]- Answer 2
> Nothing is inherently good or bad; our thinking about it makes it so.
> Note who he says it to — Rosencrantz and Guildenstern, whom he already
> suspects. A paraphrase cannot carry that; the analysis has to.

**3.** *"The undiscovered country from whose bourn / No traveller
returns, puzzles the will."*

> [!success]- Answer 3
> The unexplored country from whose border no traveller comes back
> confuses the will.
> "Puzzles" is the word to keep: not terrifies, not prevents — puzzles.
> The speech is about being stuck, not about being afraid.

**4.** *"I'll have grounds / More relative than this. The play's the
thing / Wherein I'll catch the conscience of the King."*

> [!success]- Answer 4
> I will have evidence more directly relevant than this. The play is the
> means by which I will trap the King's conscience.
> "Grounds", "relative", "catch" — legal and evidentiary vocabulary, at
> the exact moment he decides to delay again.

**5.** *"The rest is silence."*

> [!success]- Answer 5
> There is nothing left to say.
> Four words, and one of the most argued-about lines in English. Ask what
> Horatio does immediately afterwards, and whether the play agrees.

**6.** Take fifteen lines from the act your group is seminaring and
paraphrase them in the right-hand column. Mark the two you could not do.

> [!success]- Answer 6
> The marks are the point. Bring them; fifteen lines with two marks is a
> better period than forty lines skimmed.
