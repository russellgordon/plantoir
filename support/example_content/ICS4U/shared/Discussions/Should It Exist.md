You could build things last year. This year you can build things
*with other people*, on top of work somebody else already did, fast
enough that the idea and the working version arrive in the same week.
That is a genuinely different power, and it makes the old question
sharper rather than softer: not "can I", not "is it legal" — should
somebody make this.

Four proposals. All are buildable by a team in this room, this
semester, and three of them would be genuinely useful to somebody.

> [!question]- The helpful integration
> Your community partner keeps their bookings in one system and their
> mailing list in another, and retypes everything between them. You
> propose a program that syncs the two automatically. Enormously
> helpful. It also means personal information now travels a path
> nobody has reviewed, sits in a third place nobody listed, and keeps
> flowing after your team graduates. Who agreed to that path? Who
> turns it off?

> [!question]- The efficiency dashboard
> A program that shows a club's leaders how many hours each member
> has contributed, ranked, updated live. Everything it uses is data
> the club already collects. Does gathering it into one ranked,
> permanent, public view create something new? Ask the person who
> comes last, and the person who has a job on Saturdays.

> [!question]- The good-enough model
> Given a few years of past applications, a program that predicts
> which new ones will be approved. It agrees with the real decision
> about 85 percent of the time, so the team proposes using it to
> "pre-sort" the pile. Nothing is decided by the program. Except that
> the pile is now in an order, and everybody reads the top first.

> [!question]- The scraper that pays for itself
> A program that collects public listings from several organisations
> and republishes them in one searchable place. Useful, obviously.
> Nobody asked those organisations. The listings arrive stripped of
> context, one of them is out of date, and the phone that starts
> ringing is not yours.

Questions worth arguing about:

1. Which of the four would you refuse to build, and what is the
   principle behind the refusal? State it in one sentence that would
   also cover a case you have not thought of yet.
2. "If I don't build it, somebody else will." Is that an argument or
   an excuse? Does it change anything about your responsibility for
   the version that exists?
3. Every one of these has a version that is fine. Find it. What
   changed — consent, scope, who holds the data, who can say stop?
4. The third proposal insists that "nothing is decided by the
   program". Is that true? What is the difference between deciding
   something and arranging the order in which a human decides it?
5. You are one of four people on a team and you are the only one who
   thinks the project should not be built. What do you actually do?
   Work out the protocol before you need it — [[Working in a Team]]
   has one, and it is worth arguing with.
6. Is there a difference between building something and *deploying*
   it? Between deploying it and defending it after it causes harm?

> [!important] The test that survives contact with a deadline
> Could you explain your program, in full, to every person it
> touches — including the ones who are not its users — and would they
> still agree? If the answer depends on them not finding out, you
> already know.

For your own work this is not hypothetical. [[The Software Project]]
is built for a real organisation with real people in its records, and
the scope you agree in the first week is the scope you will defend at
[[The Handover]]. The groundwork is [[When Code Hurts]]; the
professional frame, including what it means to refuse work, is
[[Ethics, Security, and the Profession]].
