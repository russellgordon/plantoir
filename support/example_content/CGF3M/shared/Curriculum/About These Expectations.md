The Ministry's course page carries no expectations of its own. Ontario
has rebuilt only some of its curriculum onto that platform, and the
Grades 11 and 12 Canadian and world studies courses are not among them —
so the page exists to hand you the 2015 PDF, which remains in force for
CGF3M.

Each page here holds one expectation, word for word. The codes are the
Ministry's own, and they are also tags, so every page tagged `#C2` can be
gathered in one place.

## Strand A is not a unit

Strand A's stem reads **"Throughout this course, students will:"** while B
through E say *by the end of this course*. That is not a typographical
accident. The geographic inquiry process, mapping and spatial technologies,
and the concepts of geographic thinking are meant to be woven through every
unit rather than taught as a block at the start of the course, and this
course is built that way — you will meet strand A in the first week and in
the last.

## What "FOCUS ON" means

Every overall expectation in strands B to E ends with a bracketed label
— `(FOCUS ON: Spatial Significance; Interrelationships)` and so on. Those
name the concepts of geographic thinking that the Ministry expects the
expectation to lean on: **spatial significance**, **patterns and
trends**, **interrelationships**, and **geographic perspective**. The
labels appear here because they are part of the printed expectation.
Their definitions are not — those live in the document's Geography
introduction, printed pages 124–125, with the geographic inquiry process
on 126–127.

## What this folder leaves out

The document opens each course with an at-a-glance **Overview** spread —
big ideas and framing questions, strand by strand — and prints **Sample
questions** under many specific expectations. Neither is reproduced here;
this folder is the expectations themselves. Both are worth an hour in the
PDF when you are planning a unit.

## Why the codes matter more than they look

A code is a shared address. When a student asks "why are we studying
this?", the answer is a link. When a department writes a course outline,
or a teacher takes over a class partway through, the codes are what make one
person's planning legible to another.
