When you write code, who owns it? Who is allowed to use it? 

Software licensing defines the legal rights users have to copy, modify, and distribute your work. In the professional world, understanding licenses is critical to avoid lawsuits and to participate ethically in the global developer community.

### The Landscape of Licenses

- **Proprietary / Closed Source:** The code is secret. You buy a license to *use* the software, but you cannot see or modify how it works (e.g., Microsoft Office, macOS, commercial video games).
- **MIT License:** Extremely permissive. Anyone can do almost anything with the code, including using it in closed-source paid products, as long as they include the original copyright notice.
- **GPL (General Public License):** Copyleft. You can use and modify the code, but if you distribute your modified version, it **must** also be open source under the GPL. It enforces software freedom.
- **Creative Commons (CC):** Often used for assets, data, and documentation rather than code.

### The BC Context: Open Data

Governments are increasingly adopting "Open Data" policies. The BC Government operates an Open Data Portal containing thousands of datasets—from BC Wildfire locations to historical maps and housing data—licensed under the Open Government License (similar to CC BY). 

By making this data public and freely usable, the government allows civic tech developers, journalists, and students to build applications (like real-time transit apps or air quality monitors) that serve the community.

### Discussion Prompts

1. **The Corporate Benefit:** Major tech companies often use free, MIT-licensed open-source software to build products that generate billions in profit, without giving back to the original developers. Is this fair? Why do developers choose the MIT license if they know this will happen?
2. **Security through Transparency:** Proponents of open source argue that "given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow"—meaning open code is more secure because anyone can audit it. Opponents argue that publishing code makes it easier for hackers to find vulnerabilities. Which side do you agree with?
3. **The Government Mandate:** Should all software funded by taxpayer dollars (like the code running BC Ferries' booking system or the BC Services Card) be open source by default? What might be the risks?

### How we run this

Open source culture runs on written, asynchronous, cumulative comment
threads — a pull request review, a GitHub issue. This discussion borrows
that shape instead of talking it out loud, as a **chalk talk**.

- Before class, three sheets of chart paper go up around the room, each
  headed with one of the Discussion Prompts above.
- For fifteen minutes, everyone circulates and writes — no talking at
  all. Answer a prompt directly, respond to what someone else wrote (draw
  a line to it, the way a reply threads under a comment), or leave a
  written question in the margin.
- You can visit a sheet more than once. Some of the best comments show up
  on a second pass, once you have read what others added.
- After fifteen minutes, stop and read all three sheets together,
  standing, then take five more silent minutes for a final round of
  additions.
- Close with two or three minutes of actual talking: which written
  exchange on the wall changed your mind, and why.
