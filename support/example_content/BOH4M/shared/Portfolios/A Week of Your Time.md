Seven days of evidence about where your time actually goes, and one
decision about what to do differently.

## The tracking

For one week, record what you did in thirty-minute blocks. Not a plan —
a record, written the same day. School, work, travel, sleep,
practice, phone, everything.

Be honest about the blocks you would rather not write down; they are
where the finding is.

## The analysis

1. Total the categories. Compare with your estimate *before* you started
   — write that estimate down first, or the comparison is worthless.
2. Sort a normal Wednesday into the urgent/important grid from
   [[Managing Your Time]]. How much of the day was in the top-right box —
   important, not urgent?
3. Find your two largest blocks of unplanned time. What filled them?
4. Identify one thing you would delegate, decline, or batch if this were
   a job.

## The decision

Choose **one** change, make it for the following week, and record what
happened — including if it failed. One change that survives contact with
a real week teaches more than five changes announced on a Sunday.

> [!tip] This is the same discipline you will use as a manager
> Every organization you study this term measures something before
> changing it. Do the same here: measure, decide, act, and check — which
> is [[The Control Process]] applied to a week of your own life.

The decision section is written in class, and it is marked against the
four rows on [[The Management Portfolio]] — the same four rows as every
other entry.
