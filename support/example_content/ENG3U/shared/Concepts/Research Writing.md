Research writing is argument with other people's work in it. The
difference from an ordinary essay is not the number of sources; it is
that you now have to manage voices other than your own.

## Using a secondary source well

| Move | Looks like |
| --- | --- |
| **Support** | "As one critic argues, the novel's orality is a formal choice rather than a stylistic one." |
| **Extend** | "That account of orality explains the interviews but not the dream sequences, where…" |
| **Complicate** | "That reading holds for the first half; after the funeral the novel does the opposite." |
| **Disagree** | "This underestimates how much the novel withholds — see chapter nine." |

The first is the weakest and the most common. The other three are what
research writing is *for*.

"One critic" is a placeholder here and a fault in your own writing: name
them and give the page. A reader judges a claim partly by whose it is,
and has to be able to go and check it — see [[Citing Sources]].

## The two failures

**Source stacking.** A paragraph made of three critics agreeing with each
other and one sentence from you. Your argument has to drive; sources are
evidence, not authority.

**Ventriloquism.** Using a critic's frame without acknowledging it. If
your reading came from a source, say so — that is not weakness, it is
scholarship.

## Managing the material

1. Keep a source log from the first day: author, title, where, and one
   sentence in your own words.
2. Record the page number when you copy the quotation, not later.
3. Draft with the sources closed. Then check.

That third habit is the single best defence against accidental
plagiarism, and it produces better prose, because you write the idea
rather than paraphrasing the sentence.
