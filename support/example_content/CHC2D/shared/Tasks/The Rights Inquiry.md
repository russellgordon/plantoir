**Due: the end of Unit 3.** Pairs. An inquiry with a public-facing
product.

Rights in Canada were not granted in one moment. They were claimed,
resisted, litigated, and legislated, over decades, by people who were
often told to wait.

## The inquiry

Choose one movement or campaign between 1945 and 1982 and investigate
how change actually happened:

- Indigenous organising against the 1969 White Paper, and what followed
- Black Canadian organising against segregation and employment
  discrimination
- The women's movement and the Royal Commission on the Status of Women
- Labour organising and the right to strike
- Early gay and lesbian organising, and the state's response
- Disability rights organising

## What you produce

**An inquiry report** (about 1,000 words) answering: what was demanded,
who demanded it, who resisted and how, what changed, and what did not.

**A public-facing piece** — a display panel, a short recorded
explanation, a briefing sheet — that someone outside this class could
learn from in five minutes.

The inquiry is a pair's work and the mark is not. Each of you writes a
named section of the report — your name at the head of it — and each of
you states in one line what you made in the public piece. Those are what I
mark, and neither of you receives a mark for what the other wrote.

## How it is marked

Every row below is applied to your named section of the report and to the
part of the public piece you made, not to the pair's work as a whole.

| Criterion | What earns the marks |
| --- | --- |
| Agency | The people who organised are the subject, not the government that eventually responded |
| Evidence | Primary where possible: pamphlets, testimony, proceedings, reporting |
| Causation | You explain how change happened, not just that it did |
| Honesty | What did not change, and what was lost, are in the report |
| Communication | The part of the public piece you made works for a stranger, and your one-line account of what you made is accurate |

> [!warning] Watch the passive voice
> "Rights were extended" hides who did the work and who resisted. Name
> both. It is the most common way a rights history quietly becomes a
> story about a benevolent state.

[[Rights, Movements, and Resistance]] and
[[Indigenous Policy and Indigenous Response]] are the ground.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 3, Day 13, the work period on the public-facing piece
  A display panel holds perhaps two hundred words, so the whole period goes
  on cutting, and the cut is where the history gets decided.
  Watch for: what goes. A pair that cuts the department and keeps the
  organisers is making a claim. A pair that cuts the organisers and keeps
  the legislation has written a story about a benevolent state without
  noticing, which is what this task's warning about the passive voice is
  trying to prevent.
  Going well: an argument about what to leave out, with a reason said out
  loud.
  Stuck: cutting by length alone, from the bottom up.
  Record: on your day plan, the first thing each pair cut. One phrase each.
  The finished panel shows you the RESULT of the cut; only the period shows
  whether it was argued about or done by word count. For the pairs working
  on Black Canadian organising, D2.4 has this trap written into it — the
  societal changes that resulted from THEIR efforts — and a panel that
  keeps the statute and loses the people has answered a different question.

TALK — Unit 3, Day 11, the conferences during the research period
  Ask: "Who inside this movement disagreed with the others, and about
  what?"
  Then: "What did they call themselves, and did it change?"
  A strong first answer breaks a movement into people: the ones who wanted
  the courts and the ones who wanted the street, the ones who would deal
  with the department and the ones who would not. That is D2.1's "analyse
  them from multiple perspectives" applied inside the movement rather than
  between the movement and the state, and it is a good deal harder than the
  version the report asks for. The second question is a thirty-second test
  of whether they have read the movement's own paper — names change, and a
  student working from secondary accounts will not know it.
  Record: one line per pair on your conference list; the disagreement they
  name is the record.

The product evidence is the report and the public piece, on Unit 3, Day 16.
