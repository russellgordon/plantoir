**One seminar per unit, in class, four times.** Individual. The same
criteria every time, printed here from the first week so that nothing about
the judgement is a surprise on the day.

The four questions are [[What Was the Revolution For|What Was the Revolution For?]],
[[Could the Civil War Have Been Avoided|Could the Civil War Have Been Avoided?]],
[[Is the United States an Empire|Is the United States an Empire?]] and
[[Who Counts as American|Who Counts as American?]]. Each page says what to
bring. This page says what is marked.

## What a seminar is for

Some things you understand never reach paper. You can hold a position
against a good objection, notice that two documents are talking about
different years, or concede a point precisely rather than wholesale — and
none of that survives into an essay written a week later. A seminar is the
only place in this course where that reasoning is evidence, so it is
gathered there.

It is also the reason the room is not marked on how much anyone talks. Four
sentences that turn on a document you brought are worth more than twenty
minutes of agreement.

If speaking in a full room is genuinely hard for you, say so before the
seminar rather than after it, and we will arrange another way for the
reasoning to exist where I can hear it — a written contribution read into
the discussion, a smaller group, a conversation the same day. The one thing
that cannot happen is the evidence not existing at all.

## How it is marked

| Criterion | What earns the marks |
| --- | --- |
| Evidence | Your contributions rest on the source you brought, named and dated |
| Position | You state one, early enough to be argued with, and you say what it rests on |
| Answering | You engage the strongest thing said against you, not the easiest |
| Fairness | You state someone else's position in a form they would accept before you answer it |
| Precision | You concede what has been shown and say exactly what has not |

Five rows, four times, and the fourth seminar is the one that counts most —
your mark reflects where you got to, not the average of September and
January.

> [!tip] Preparation is not the marked thing, and it is what makes the mark
> possible
> Bringing the document, the criterion, the voice — that is homework, and
> homework is never what I mark. What I mark is what you do with it in the
> room. Students who arrive without the source almost always spend the hour
> agreeing with whoever spoke last, which the criteria above cannot reward.

## Two things that are not in this mark

Nobody's mark depends on a classmate's judgement of them, and nobody is
marked on how the room treated their idea. A position that turns out to be
wrong, argued from evidence and given up when the evidence went against it,
is a strong contribution and is marked as one — see [[How Marks Work]].

And what is said in a seminar about slavery, dispossession, internment or
segregation is bound by [[Our Working Agreement]] before it is bound by
these criteria. The care is not a marking scheme. It is the condition of
the room.

Prepare with [[Building an Argument]] — the section on stating criteria
before judging is the one that most changes how a seminar goes — and record
the source you bring in [[Your Inquiry Notebook]] as you would for any
task.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

This task IS conversation evidence, so the usual proportions invert: there
is almost no product, and what there is (the source each student brings) is
the thinner half. Plan to record during the seminar or you will not record
at all.

OBSERVE — Unit 2, Day 14, the second seminar, "Could the Civil War Have
Been Avoided?"
  Use the second one rather than the first: by then the routine is known
  and what you are watching is not simply nerves.
  Watch for: what happens to a document after it is put on the table.
  Does anyone else pick it up — quote it back, date it, argue with it — or
  does the room step over it and resume the previous line? Whether a
  student's evidence gets taken up is invisible in any record either
  student keeps, and it tells you which contributions the room could
  actually use.
  Going well: a second speaker names the first speaker's source before
  disagreeing with them.
  Stuck: three consecutive turns with no source in any of them; or a
  student who reads a prepared paragraph and then goes quiet for the rest
  of the hour — unless that is the arrangement you agreed with them, in
  which case the paragraph and the conversation you promised instead ARE
  the evidence, and this line does not apply to them.
  Record: a seating plan photocopied four times. A tick for a contribution
  resting on a source, a small arrow when someone picks up another
  student's evidence, a circle when a position moves. Nothing else, or you
  will stop.

TALK — Unit 3, Day 10, the preparation slot already on that agenda
  The page hands them a table of criteria with a counter-case already
  written against each, so do not ask for a counter-case — ask them to
  judge the ones they have been given.
  Ask, while they are choosing: "Which of the counter-cases in that table
  is the weakest, and what would you have to show to knock it down?"
  Then: "Which of the three positions do you think the room will find
  easiest, and is easy the same as right here?"
  A strong answer treats the criterion as something that can fail a test
  rather than as a label to apply — findings evaluated and synthesised into
  a judgement the student can defend, which is A1.6. An essay shows you the
  judgement; only this shows you whether the standard was chosen or
  inherited.
  Record: one line per student on the class list, in the ten minutes after
  the bell.

The product evidence is thin by design: the source each student brings,
recorded in their notebook. Do not let it stand in for the seminar.
