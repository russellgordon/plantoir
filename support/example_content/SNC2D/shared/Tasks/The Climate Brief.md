A brief is a short document written for somebody who has to make a
decision and does not have time to read everything. It is the most
useful thing a scientist writes and the hardest to write well, because
every sentence has to earn its space.

## The situation

Somewhere near you, a real organisation is going to make a decision
that a changing climate is relevant to. A municipal council setting a
budget. A school board deciding what to do about buildings that
overheat. A conservation authority planning for a floodplain. A farm or
a small business deciding what to invest in. A community organisation
choosing where to put its effort.

Pick one that **actually exists** in your area, find out what it is
actually deciding, and write it two pages.

## Who it is for

A real body, named, with real constraints. This matters more here than
on any other task, because a brief that could have been addressed to
anybody is addressed to nobody.

Find out, before you write a word: what does this body have the power
to do? A school board cannot change a provincial rebate. A council can
change a zoning by-law and cannot change a fuel standard. Advice aimed
outside somebody's authority is not advice.

You do **not** have to send it. If you want to, talk to me first and we
will do it properly.

## What to hand in

Two pages, plus sources. Six parts, and they are all required.

1. **The hypothesis you tested.** Take one widely repeated
   cause-and-effect claim about climate — that global temperatures have
   risen since the industrial revolution, that rising carbon dioxide
   concentration is driving rising temperature, that severe weather of
   some particular kind is becoming more frequent — and examine it
   against **time-trend data you obtained yourself**. State plainly
   whether the data you found supports it, and how strongly.
2. **One figure you made.** Not a screenshot. Download the series,
   plot it, label the axes with units, state the source and the period
   underneath, and say what each point is — a single reading, an annual
   mean, a running average. Then apply the tests in
   [[Reading a Graph Honestly]] to your own graph before anyone else
   does.
3. **Who is affected.** Effects on human activity and on natural
   systems, positive and negative both, and **specific to a place**.
   Somebody in your audience should recognise their own situation in
   it. A general paragraph about polar bears is not this section.
4. **An initiative, assessed.** Choose an existing programme operating
   at any scale — individual, municipal, provincial, national, or
   international — and assess how effective it has actually been.
   Effective at what, measured how, and according to whom? Then
   **propose a further course of action** connected to it. This is the
   part your audience will read first.
5. **Two portrayals, compared.** Find how the same issue is presented
   in a scientific source and in a non-scientific one — a news article,
   an advertisement, a political statement, a social media post. Say
   what each one emphasises, what each leaves out, and **what values or
   interests would lead somebody to frame it that way**. Bias is not
   the same as dishonesty, and the interesting cases are the ones where
   nothing said is false.
6. **What your brief does not settle.** One honest paragraph.

> [!warning] Where the data must come from, and what happens if it does not
> Go to the source and take the numbers out of it yourself:
>
> - **Environment and Climate Change Canada** — long temperature and
>   precipitation records for Canadian stations, and national climate
>   assessment reporting.
> - **NOAA** — global surface, ocean, and atmospheric datasets.
> - **IPCC assessment reports** — summaries of the published
>   literature, rather than any single study, with their own
>   uncertainties stated in the text.
>
> Read what the authors say about the uncertainty in their own data.
> That sentence is always there and it is always dropped by the time a
> number reaches a headline.
>
> **Every figure in your brief must be traceable to a dataset or report
> you can open in front of me.** If you cannot point at it, delete it.
> "I could not find a reliable figure for this" is an acceptable
> sentence in a brief and costs you nothing. A number from an article
> that does not say where it came from is worth less than no number,
> because it looks like evidence.

## How it will be judged

| What I am looking for | Level 3 sounds like | Level 4 sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The hypothesis | Stated clearly and checked against real data | Says how strongly the data supports it, and what would count against it |
| Your figure | Correct, labelled, sourced | Axis and range choices defended, and its own weaknesses named first |
| Who is affected | Real effects, correctly described | Specific to this place and these people, with both columns of cost visible |
| The initiative | Described accurately, and a further action proposed | Assessed against a stated measure of success, and the proposal costed |
| Two portrayals | Differences identified | Explains **why** each source frames it that way, without treating framing as lying |
| Fit to audience | Aimed at the right body | Every recommendation is inside what that body can actually do |
| Honesty | States a limitation | States precisely what the brief does not settle, and does not overclaim anywhere else |

## What sinks an otherwise good brief

- **A number nobody can trace.** Fastest possible way to lose a
  reader's trust and the marks attached to it.
- **A recommendation with no cost.** The costs of a changing climate
  and the costs of responding to it land on different people, and a
  brief that only counts one column has taken a side without saying so.
  See [[Whose Climate, Whose Cost]].
- **A graph borrowed rather than built.** Making it yourself is how you
  find out what is in the data.
- **Treating "biased" as "wrong".** A source with an interest can still
  be accurate. The task is to say what the framing emphasises, not to
  dismiss it.
- **Advice the audience has no power to act on.**

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 3, Day 14, the working period on the figure
  Watch for: the ORDER in which the claim and the window get settled. Every
  brief will caption its figure with a source and a period, so a badly
  cropped record is catchable afterwards; what the finished page cannot show
  you is whether the claim was narrowed to fit what the data said, or the
  start year dragged until the line agreed with a claim already written.
  Only the period at the screen tells you that.
  Going well: the whole record plotted first, at its full length, and then
  the hypothesis sentence rewritten smaller to match what is actually there.
  Stuck: the same sentence on screen at the end of the period as at the
  beginning, with the axis handles having moved four times.
  Record: three columns on your seating plan — narrowed the claim, trimmed
  the data, or neither yet. One circuit at the twenty-minute mark and
  another near the end; the change between the two is the finding. That is
  D2.4 watched rather than marked: investigating a popular hypothesis
  against time-trend data means letting the data answer.

TALK — Unit 3, Day 13, the conference already on that agenda
  You are asking each of them about their audience anyway. Do not ask
  whether the decision is one somebody could actually take — that is the
  agenda item — and do not ask what values would lead a source to frame it
  that way, which the task page prints.
  Ask: "Take the non-scientific source you found and change one thing about
  who wrote it — the employer, the country, or the year. What would be
  different in the piece?"
  Then: "Your recommendation asks your audience to spend something they had
  other plans for. What do they cut instead of doing it — and what have you
  put in the brief that makes that harder to choose?"
  A strong first answer changes something specific and traces it to a
  specific sentence or omission, rather than saying the piece would be
  "more biased"; that is D2.9 in conversation, where a written comparison
  usually stops at listing differences. A strong second answer shows the
  student knows what their audience's money is currently doing, which is
  what separates a proposal from a wish.
  Record: two ticks per student in the conference column of your day plan.

The product evidence is the two-page brief with its sources, submitted at
the start of Unit 3, Day 16.
