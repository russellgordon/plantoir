> [!abstract] At a glance
> Groups of 4–5 · rehearsed in class across the whole of Unit 4 · performed
> on Unit 4, Day 20 for an invited audience · three to five minutes on stage
> · this is your final evaluation, the thirty per cent described in
> [[How Marks Work]]

## What you are making

Your group will stage one polished piece for a real, invited audience.
You may revive the strongest work your group made this course — a
[[Tableau Story Sequence]], a scene grown from your
[[Improvisation Showcase]], or your [[Scene Study from a Story]] — and
make it better than it was, or you may build a new short scene from a
story your group agrees on. Reviving is not repeating: the piece must grow.

Two things make this performance different from everything before it:

1. **Production elements.** The piece uses at least two simple production
   choices deliberately — costume, prop, sound, or light — chosen because
   they sharpen the piece, not decorate it. Start with
   [[Simple Costumes and Props]], and place every body on stage with
   intention using [[Blocking and Stage Areas]].
2. **Production roles.** Every group member performs *and* holds one
   production role — director's eye, stage manager, costume and props
   lead, or sound and front-of-house. Your [[Production Roles Interview]]
   told you what these jobs involve; now you do one of them for real.

## How to work

1. Choose fast: revival or new piece. A revival buys you rehearsal time;
   a new piece buys you fresh energy. Either can earn top marks.
2. Assign production roles before you rehearse — the stage manager runs
   the clock from day one, and every cue gets written down.
3. Rehearse the piece, then rehearse the production: a technical run for
   cues and entrances, then a dress run with every costume and prop in
   play, working as [[How to Rehearse]] describes.
4. Trade audiences with another group and exchange notes —
   [[Giving and Receiving Notes]] keeps the feedback kind and useful.
5. Performing for guests raises the stakes — [[Learning Lines]] early and
   trusting your warm-up routine are what nerves cannot take from you.

Rehearsal time is class time, and it is where most of what I judge actually
happens — see [[How Marks Work]].

> [!note] A company of five, and five separate marks
> There is no common group mark on this task, and on the piece that decides
> thirty per cent of your credit that matters more than anywhere else. Your
> mark comes from two things that are yours alone: your work on stage, and
> the production role you hold — the cues you called, the props you set, the
> house you managed, the decisions you made and wrote down. A company can be
> uneven and you can still meet every criterion below.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like from the audience |
| --- | --- |
| A finished piece | Clear beginning, middle, and end — no drift, no filler |
| Growth | A revival is visibly better; a new piece is visibly rehearsed |
| Production choices | Costume, prop, sound, or light sharpens meaning, and the group can say how |
| Blocking | Stage areas are used deliberately and sightlines stay clean |
| Production roles | Each member's role visibly worked — cues called, props set, house managed |
| Ensemble | The piece is built for everybody in it — every performer has something the piece needs |

> [!success]- If your group is stuck (click to expand)
> Stuck groups usually try to make the piece bigger. Make it smaller: cut
> thirty seconds, cut a prop, cut a location. Then ask each production
> role to name the one thing their job must deliver on performance day —
> that list is your rehearsal plan for everything that remains.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

This is the final evaluation, so read the timing before the prompts. On
Day 20 there is an invited audience in the room and you are running it:
house, order, transitions, guests. You cannot confer, you cannot circulate,
and the thirty seconds of looking you get per student is the performance
mark, not evidence-gathering beside it. Days 17 to 19 are dress runs and
are no better — you will be watching the whole evening, not one company.
Everything below therefore happens in the first two weeks of the unit,
while there is still time for what you find to change somebody's work.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 10, the first run with technical elements
  Watch for who has a pen. A technical run makes more decisions in an hour
  than anybody remembers by Friday, and the criteria table promises that
  each member's role is visibly worked — on the night you will see cues
  called, but you will never see who wrote them down. The run will be slow
  and stop often; that is the day working, not failing. What varies between
  companies is whether the stopping leaves a record.
  Going well: the stage manager writing during the holds, and performers
  going to the book to check rather than asking the same question again.
  Stuck: a company whose only record of tonight is five memories, and a
  stage manager standing in the scene rather than beside it.
  Record: one tick per company — book, loose notes, nothing — and the names
  of the performers you saw consult it. Two minutes at the end of the
  period.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 3, the five-minute company conference already on that
agenda
  The agenda tells them to arrive with one sentence about what the piece is
  about, so take that first, then spend the rest of the five minutes here.
  Ask: "Three weeks from now, which two minutes of this piece will still
  not be working?"
  Then: "Which single moment in your piece needs somebody offstage to get
  it right — and what happens if they are half a second late?"
  A company that answers the second with a specific moment and a specific
  consequence — the blackout that lands on the wrong line, the door that
  opens before the knock, the sound that starts under the last word instead
  of after it — has understood that the production roles ARE the piece,
  which is B3.3 and C1.3 a fortnight before either becomes visible. A
  company that says "nothing really" has told you what to teach on Day 4,
  when the roles get assigned. The first question is the opener: it gets
  the quiet members talking, because the two minutes that are not working
  are usually theirs.
  Record: two lines per company on your day plan — the two minutes they
  fear, and the dependency they named. Both are worth rereading on Day 12,
  when they work their notes.

The product evidence is the performance on Day 20 and the strike that
follows it — the only part of this task that arrives without you.
