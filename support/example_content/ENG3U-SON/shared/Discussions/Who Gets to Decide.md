In [[Borders]], a woman is stopped at the Canada–US border because she will
only give one answer to "what is your citizenship?" — Blackfoot — and it
isn't one of the two the form allows. The standoff that follows is quiet:
nobody raises their voice, nothing violent happens, and it still takes days
to resolve. This seminar is about where the power in that standoff actually
sits, and whether it moves.

## The questions

1. The mother could answer "Canadian" or "American" and settle the whole
   thing in five seconds. Why doesn't she — and is her son right to be
   embarrassed by her about it, at least at first?
2. The border guards hold all the institutional power in this story — they
   can refuse to let the car through, and they do. By the end, has that
   power actually moved anywhere, since the mother's answer never changes?
3. The story is narrated by the son, watching his mother do something he
   doesn't fully understand while he's living through it. Does the story
   trust him to understand it later? Do you?
4. Whose decision is really being fought over here — what the mother calls
   herself, or what the guards are allowed to write down on a form?

> [!success]- What a strong answer to question 2 sounds like
> "The guards never budge on the rule, so if you're only looking at the
> rule, nothing changes. But the story doesn't end with the rule changing —
> it ends with the mother getting through anyway, on her own terms, because
> the situation becomes more trouble for the border than holding the line
> is worth. That's not the guards losing power. It's the mother showing
> that her answer was never actually up for negotiation, and eventually
> the border has to work around that instead of through it."
>
> Notice what this does that "the mother wins" doesn't: it says what
> winning actually looks like here, since nobody's mind on either side of
> the counter ever officially changes.
