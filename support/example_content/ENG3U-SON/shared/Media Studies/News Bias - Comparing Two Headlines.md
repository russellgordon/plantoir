The same event can be reported by two outlets and come out sounding like
two different stories. Today, in pairs, you will find out exactly how much
work a headline can do before a single sentence of the article is read.

## Finding your pair of headlines

With your partner, find two headlines — from two different news outlets —
covering the *same* story, published around the same time. A search
engine's news tab is the fastest way to find a pair; a story that is
currently in the news works better than an old one, because the coverage
is still fresh enough to compare in detail.

If you are stuck, ask your teacher for a pair — some classes keep a folder
of good comparison pairs from past terms.

## A worked example

Here is the kind of gap you are looking for, using an invented but typical
pair — a city council vote on a new bike lane:

> [!example] Two headlines, one vote
> **Outlet A:** "Council Approves Contested Bike Lane Despite Business
> Owners' Objections"
> **Outlet B:** "City Moves Forward on Long-Awaited Cycling Safety
> Upgrade"
>
> Same vote, same day. Outlet A's headline puts the conflict first —
> *contested*, *despite*, *objections* — and names one side. Outlet B's
> headline puts the outcome first and frames it as progress that was
> already expected. Neither headline is factually false. Each one decides,
> before you read a word of the article, who you should be thinking about
> and how you should feel about the decision.

## Applying what you know about rhetorical appeals

Back in Unit 3, you used
[[Rhetorical Appeals - Ethos, Pathos, Logos|Rhetorical Appeals: Ethos, Pathos, Logos]]
to read Claudius and Polonius. The same three appeals are at work in a
headline, and this is exactly that skill returning in a new context:

- **Ethos** — which outlet's headline borrows authority (an official's
  title, a government body's name) to sound more credible?
- **Pathos** — which word choices are working on your feelings before you
  have any facts — a word like *contested*, *fought*, *finally*, or
  *forced*?
- **Logos** — does either headline lean on a number, a vote count, or a
  timeline to sound more objective than the other?

With your partner, mark each of your two real headlines for ethos, pathos,
and logos, the same way you marked Claudius's speeches. Then write three
or four sentences: which headline is more persuasive, which appeal is
doing that work, and what a reader would believe about the story from the
headline alone, before reading a single paragraph.
