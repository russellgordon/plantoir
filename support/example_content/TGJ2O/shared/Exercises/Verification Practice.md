These questions follow [[Misinformation and Verification]] and the
instinct you built in [[News or Not]]. The habit chain for every claim:
find the source, find the date, find the original context — in order.

## Questions

1. The group chat says: "No school Friday — a pipe burst, my cousin
   works there." Walk the claim down the habit chain. What do you do
   before repeating it to anyone?
2. A screenshot shows the cafeteria menu with every price doubled —
   no date, no source, just the image. What is missing? How to check?
3. A hallway poster: "90% of students want a later start time." The
   poll turns out to be ten people from one friend group. What is
   wrong with the number — and is it *useless*?
4. **Explain why** "it has thousands of shares" is not evidence that
   a claim is true. What *is* it evidence of?
5. A photo of a flooded gym circulates, captioned "our school, last
   night." A reverse image search finds it on a news page from three
   years ago, in another city. What happened — and why does it work?
6. **Challenge.** A tip lands Thursday night: the spring trip is
   cancelled. Probably true — but nobody who knows is answering, and
   you publish Friday morning. Run it, hold it, or something else?

## Answers

> [!success]- Answer 1
> Source: not the cousin — the school office. Date: is this about
> *this* Friday? Context: burst pipes close hallways more often than
> schools. Until confirmed, repeating it just makes the chat louder.

> [!success]- Answer 2
> A screenshot has no date, no author, no history — a picture of a
> claim, not proof of one. Check the original: the posted menu, the
> cafeteria staff, the school site. Ten steps beat squinting at pixels.

> [!success]- Answer 3
> Ten friends cannot speak for a school — the sample is tiny, nothing
> like random, and friends tend to agree. But it is a *lead*: "some
> students want a later start" is true, and checkable with a fair survey.

> [!success]- Answer 4
> Shares measure how a claim makes people *feel* — and false claims
> are built to feel surprising or infuriating. Popularity is evidence
> of spread, never of truth; the chain has no step called "counting."

> [!success]- Answer 5
> Real photo, false context — the commonest trick, because nothing is
> faked: the image is genuine, so the claim *feels* verified. Only the
> caption lies — which is why the chain ends with original context.

> [!success]- Answer 6
> More than one defensible route, but "run it as-is" is not among
> them. Hold the claim and publish what is confirmed — even "the school
> did not respond by deadline" is honest reporting. First and wrong
> costs what you cannot buy back; second and right costs one day.
