Five stories in the first unit, read for structure and voice rather than
for plot.

| Story | Writer | What it teaches |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "The Boat" | Alistair MacLeod | Retrospective narration, and a debt that cannot be paid |
| "Boys and Girls" | Alice Munro | A narrator judging her younger self, and a childhood in which the roles close |
| "A Short History of Indians in Canada" | Thomas King | Satire compressed into three pages |
| "The Painted Door" | Sinclair Ross | Isolation, weather as pressure, an ending readers still argue about |
| "Borders" | Thomas King | A refusal that becomes a position |

> [!warning] Content
> "The Boat" and "The Painted Door" both end in a death. "A Short History
> of Indians in Canada" uses a violent image satirically, to make a point
> about how Indigenous people have been treated as spectacle.

## How we read them

Three passes, per [[Close Reading]]. Then one structural question each
time:

- Where does the story start, and what was left out?
- Who is telling this, when, and how much older are they than the events?
- What is repeated, and does its meaning change?
- What does the last sentence do to the first?

## Why these five

Because they disagree about what a story is for: a debt, a closing door,
a joke with a knife in it, a decision made in a storm, a word repeated at
a border. By the end of the unit you should be able to say what the short
story form makes possible — and what it forbids.

## What it feeds

[[The Close Reading Essay]], which asks for one argument about one story,
made in 700 words.
