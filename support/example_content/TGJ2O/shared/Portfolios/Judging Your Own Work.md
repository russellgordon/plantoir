Every beat page publishes a table of success criteria before you start.
Most reporters read it once, on launch day, and never again — then file
work that misses two rows they could have fixed in twenty minutes. This
page is the twenty minutes. It is the edit desk you run on yourself
before anyone else sees the piece.

We do it together the first time, and I go first: my own draft on the
screen, judged out loud against the same table, weakest row named in
front of you. Watching somebody else be honest about their own work is
the only part of this that cannot be explained.

## The routine

1. Open the beat page and put its criteria table beside your work.
2. Go row by row and write **yes**, **not yet**, or **partly** — plus
   one sentence of evidence: the paragraph, the frame, the caption,
   the thing a reader would actually see. "Yes" with no evidence is a
   wish.
3. Name your weakest row. Not the easiest to fix — the weakest.
4. Write one sentence saying what you will change, and when.
5. Change it, in the rest of this period or the next one. That is why
   this is never scheduled on a deadline day: a judgement you have no
   time to act on is only a worry.

Steps 2 and 3 are the whole exercise. Naming the weakest row honestly
is harder than any of the fixing, because it means writing down the
thing you were quietly hoping the desk would not notice.

> [!example]- One row, judged honestly
> From [[Your First Byline]]: *"Accuracy — names spelled right, quotes
> checked against the recording."*
>
> **Partly.** Both names are checked against the attendance list. The
> coach's quote is typed from my notes, not from the recording, and I
> remember him saying "we were flat" but my notes say "we were slow".
> Weakest row. **Change:** relisten to that minute of audio and fix
> the quote, this period, before the peer edit.
>
> That is worth more than "looks fine" written against seven rows, and
> it took less time to write than this sentence took to read.

## Where this fits

Your judgement of your own work is not part of your mark, and neither
is a classmate's — [[How Marks Work]] says why. That cuts both ways:
nothing you write here can cost you anything, which is exactly what
makes it worth writing honestly.

What it changes is the work. A self-check that produces one real fix is
the cheapest improvement available in this course, and by January you
run it without being asked — which was the point the whole time. The
same move, pointed backwards across the term instead of at one piece,
is [[Showing Growth]].

> [!tip] Read it aloud to the desk beside you
> Say your judgement out loud to one person. You will hear the rows
> where you were being generous before they say a word — the same
> reason a lede sounds wrong read aloud and looks fine on the screen.
