Every task page carries a criteria table, published on the day the task
launches. Most people read it once, at launch, and never again — and
then hand in a report that misses two rows they could have fixed in
twenty minutes. This page is the twenty minutes.

We do this together the first time, on a report from another year, so
that the move is not new to you when it is your own work being judged.

## The routine

1. Open the task page and put the criteria table beside your draft.
2. Go row by row. For each one write **yes**, **not yet**, or **partly**
   — and one sentence of evidence: the table, the line, the paragraph, the
   thing a reader would actually see. "Yes" with no evidence is a wish.
3. Find your weakest row. Not the easiest to fix — the weakest.
4. Write one sentence saying what you will change, and when.
5. Fix it — in the rest of this period, or in the next one. This is why
   the self-check is never scheduled on the day something is due: a
   judgement you have no time to act on is just a worry.

Steps 2 and 3 are the whole exercise. Naming your weakest row honestly
is harder than any of the fixing, because it means saying the thing you
have been hoping nobody would notice.

> [!example]- One row, judged honestly
> From [[The Yield Investigation]]: *"Every error mechanism has a
> direction attached."*
>
> **Partly.** I have three mechanisms listed and only one of them says
> which way it pushed the number. "Some product stayed on the filter
> paper" is in there twice in different words, and "the crucible may
> have still been warm" has no direction at all — which is careless,
> because warm glassware reads **low** and that one is easy. Weakest
> row. **Change:** give all three a direction and a size in grams, then
> total them against the gap I actually observed — this period, before
> I write the conclusion.
>
> That entry is worth more than "looks good to me" on all nine rows,
> and it took less time to write. It is also the kind of honesty a
> marker rewards rather than punishes: an acknowledged flaw, sized, is
> a finding.

## Three rows worth checking every single time

These are the ones that go missing under time pressure, whatever the
task is:

- **Does every number carry a unit and a defensible number of
  significant figures?** Not the same number of decimal places
  everywhere — the number the instrument entitles you to. See
  [[Significant Figures in Practice]].
- **Does every limitation say which way it pushed the result?** A
  limitation with no direction is a gesture, not an analysis.
- **Is every figure you did not measure yourself traceable to a source
  a reader can open?** If you could not find one, does the page say so?
  See [[What Counts as Evidence]].

For a lab report, the criteria on [[Writing a Lab Report]] are the ones
to use, and the row to check hardest is whether the analysis says what
the data **allows** you to claim rather than what you expected to find.

## Where this fits

Your judgement of your own work is not part of your mark, and neither is
a classmate's — see [[How Marks Work]]. That is deliberate, and it cuts
both ways: nothing you write here can cost you anything, which is
exactly what makes it worth writing honestly.

What it does change is the work. A self-check that leads to a fix is the
cheapest mark improvement available in this course. Keep the entries in
your [[Chemistry Journal]]; by the end of the semester they are also the
clearest record you have of what you got better at, which is what
[[Showing Growth]] is asking for.

One last thing worth doing: read your judgement out loud to the person
next to you. It catches the rows where you were being generous, and you
will usually hear it yourself before they say anything.
