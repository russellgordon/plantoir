## The question

Can a reading of a text be wrong? If so, what makes it wrong?

## Positions to consider

| Position | The argument | The objection |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The text decides | Evidence settles it | Texts under-determine meaning constantly; that is why critics disagree in print |
| The writer decides | Intention anchors meaning | Usually unavailable, and writers are not always right about their own work |
| The reader decides | Meaning happens in reading | Then criticism is impossible, and criticism is what we are doing |
| The community of readers decides | Interpretations are tested and some survive | Which community, and for how long were they wrong last time? |

## Three readings to test

Of *Macbeth*'s witches: they cause the tragedy; they describe a man
already deciding; they are irrelevant and the marriage is the engine. All
three can be built from the play — see [[Ambiguity and Irony]].

## The complication this course adds

When we read [[Monkey Beach]], "any reading is as valid as any other"
collides with something real. A reading that converts a specific family's
knowledge into a general symbol, or that treats Haisla material as
scenery for a universal story, is not simply a different reading — it is
doing the thing the novel is about. Bring that into the discussion rather
than around it.

## Afterwards

One paragraph: the strongest reading you heard that was not yours, and
what would convince you of it.
