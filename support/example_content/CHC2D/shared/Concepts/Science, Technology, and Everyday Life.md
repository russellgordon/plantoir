Technology is easy to list and hard to assess. Anyone can name the car, the
radio and the telephone. The historian's question is different: what did a
device actually change about how people lived, who got it first, and who was
still waiting a generation later?

## The war as a laboratory

The First World War industrialized killing. Machine guns, heavy artillery,
poison gas, aircraft and submarines all matured under wartime pressure, and
the medical response matured with them — blood transfusion, triage,
reconstructive surgery, and the beginnings of a language for what was then
called shell shock. Soldiers survived wounds that would have killed them a
decade earlier, and then lived for decades with injuries the country had no
system to support.

The best-known Canadian medical achievement of the period came out of the
University of Toronto in the early 1920s, where Frederick Banting and Charles
Best, working in J. J. R. Macleod's laboratory with the biochemist James
Collip, isolated and purified insulin. Before insulin, a diagnosis of what we
now call type 1 diabetes in a child was a death sentence within months. After
it, the disease became something a person could live with.[^1]

[^1]: Notice how credit was distributed. The Nobel Prize went to Banting and
Macleod; Banting shared his half with Best, and Macleod shared his with
Collip. Who gets named in the story of a discovery is itself a historical
question, and this one was argued about bitterly at the time.

## What arrived in the house

Electrification, where it reached, brought electric light, then irons,
washing machines and refrigerators. The promise was that housework would
shrink. In practice standards rose to fill the time — clothes were expected
to be cleaner, meals more elaborate — so the labour changed shape more than
size. That is a useful corrective to the assumption that a new machine simply
means less work.

Radio did something else. For the first time, people in different provinces
heard the same voice at the same moment. Much of what they heard came from
American stations, which begins a Canadian anxiety about broadcasting that
leads directly to a national public broadcaster in the next decade.

## Who was filmed, and who was filming

*Nanook of the North*, made in the Canadian Arctic in 1922, was received
worldwide as a documentary record of Inuit life. It was staged: scenes were
directed, a family was assembled for the camera, and tools already in
ordinary use were replaced with older ones to look authentic. It shaped how
Inuit were seen abroad for decades, by audiences who never asked whether the
Inuit on screen agreed with the portrait. Meanwhile the Canadian Arctic
Expedition of 1913–18 depended on Inuit knowledge of ice, weather, travel and
clothing to survive at all. Both facts belong in the same paragraph.
