Photograph a teammate so that a stranger looking at the frame learns
something true about them — not just what they look like, but what
they care about. This is the portrait work behind every profile the
newsroom will run, and it uses everything in [[Making Photographs]]
plus one skill no camera setting provides: directing a real person
with respect.

> [!warning] Care and consent notes
> **Consent is the first exposure** — ask before the camera comes up,
> and your subject can decline any pose, veto any frame, or withdraw
> entirely, no reasons required; [[Releases and Consent]] is the full
> agreement, and today you practise it for real. **Strap on before
> the camera leaves the bag**, wrist or neck, every time. **Light
> stands get all three legs fully spread**, cables along walls, never
> across walking paths. **Back up before the card clears** — and
> delete any frame your subject vetoes, everywhere it lives.

## What you need

- [ ] The class camera, charged, strap attached
- [ ] A teammate who has agreed to sit — and knows they can veto
- [ ] A window, a plain wall, and five minutes of conversation first
- [ ] Your notebook, for the one true thing you learn

## The work

1. **Talk before you shoot.** Five minutes of real questions — what
   they make, what they are proud of. The answer you write down
   becomes the photograph's job.
2. **Choose light on purpose.** Window light from the side sculpts a
   face; light from behind you flattens it — walk the room and watch.
3. **Choose the background second.** A plain wall says "look at the
   person"; a sketchbook or gym bag says who the person is.
   Everything in frame is a claim — cut what lies.
4. **Direct with verbs, not adjectives.** "Look toward the window"
   works; "be natural" never has — one small action at a time.
5. **Work the composition.** Eyes on an upper third, room to look
   into — [[Composition and Framing]] applies to people most of all.
6. **Review together.** Show your subject the frames and let them
   flag any they refuse. Then pick the one a stranger learns the
   most from — and say out loud what it teaches.

## What can go wrong

- **Technically fine, says nothing.** You skipped the conversation —
  the photograph can only answer a question you actually asked.
- **Your subject stiffens when the camera rises.** Keep talking while
  you shoot, and ask about the thing they love; the real face comes
  back mid-sentence.
- **A busy background eats the person.** Two steps sideways is the
  usual fix — check the frame's edges before you press, not after.

## Level up

Make a second portrait of the same person that tells a different
true thing — then bring both to [[Photo Critique Practice]] and see
whether the room can read each claim without being told.
