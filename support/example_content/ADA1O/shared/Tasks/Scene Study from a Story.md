## What you are making

Your group will adapt a fairy tale, myth, or legend into a scripted scene
of three to four minutes. Not the whole story — a scene: choose the stretch
of the tale where the most is at stake and stage that, trusting your
audience to carry the rest.

The adaptation must be shaped by **exactly one** convention, chosen from:

- [[Flashback and Flash-Forward]] — break the timeline to reveal a cause or
  a consequence
- [[Corridor of Voices]] — surround a character with the voices pulling at
  them
- [[Choral Speaking]] — give words to many voices at once

Alongside the performance, **each of you** writes a short note — half a page
— justifying the choice: why this convention, for this story, at this
moment. The group makes the decision together; the argument for it is
yours, in your own words, and you write it in class the day after we
perform. "It seemed cool" is a start, not a justification;
[[Writing About Drama]] shows what a reason looks like.

> [!note] You rehearse in a group, and you are marked as yourself
> There is no common group mark. Your mark comes from your own work in the
> scene — the character you play and the choices your body makes — and from
> your own half-page note. Two people in the same scene can and often do
> come out differently.

## How to work

1. Read two or three candidate stories before choosing one. The best story
   for this task has a character facing a real decision — see
   [[Role and Character]].
2. Find your scene: where does the decision happen? What came before can
   be implied or flashed to; what comes after can be left to the audience.
3. Try the scene with two different conventions before committing to one.
   The comparison is what makes your note honest — this is the
   experimenting stage of [[The Creative Process]].
4. Script it, but keep the script thin. Decisions about levels and
   [[Focus and Emphasis|focus]] will carry more than extra dialogue.
5. Rehearse in class, and log what changes and why in your
   [[Drama Journal]] — your note practically writes itself from those
   entries.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Adaptation | The scene stands on its own; the source is honoured, not recited |
| Convention | The convention does real work — cut it and the scene loses something |
| Character | The motives of primary and secondary characters are readable |
| Elements | [[The Elements of Drama\|Elements]] like tension and contrast are used on purpose |
| Written note | Your justification names what the convention adds, in drama terms |

> [!success]- Choosing between the three conventions (click to expand)
> Ask what your scene is missing. If the audience needs to know *why* —
> use [[Flashback and Flash-Forward]]. If your character is torn between
> pressures — use [[Corridor of Voices]]. If the story's power is in its
> telling, its rhythm, its bigness — use [[Choral Speaking]]. A convention
> should answer a problem, not decorate a scene.

OBSERVE — Unit 3, Day 8, the blocking working period
  Watch for who is out front. A blocking pass only means anything if
  somebody is sitting where the audience will sit, and every group in this
  room is short a body when they try it. The performance shows you where
  people stood; it cannot show you whether that was looked at from the
  house or only felt from inside the scene.
  A five-hander cannot spare anybody for a whole run, and the agenda asks
  for two runs, so what you are watching for is what happens BETWEEN them.
  Going well: somebody steps into the house between runs, and the person
  who stepped out is the one talking when they come back.
  Stuck: nobody sits down all period — or the same student is parked in the
  house every time because they have the fewest lines.
  Record: one word per group on your day plan — rotating, fixed, or nobody.

TALK — Unit 3, Day 7, while they work on whether the scene needs a time
shift
  That day's agenda already asks them to defend choosing NOT to flash back,
  so take two minutes per group and go past it. The half-page note each
  student hands in after the performance will give you their reasoning in
  writing; this is where you find out whether the reasoning came first or
  was assembled afterwards.
  Do not ask whether the scene would lose something without the convention
  — that sentence is printed in the criteria table and they will hand it
  straight back to you.
  Ask: "You tried another convention first. What did that version do to the
  scene that you did not want?"
  Then: "Which character does the convention help most, and which one does
  it cost?"
  A strong answer to the first names an effect rather than a feeling — it
  made the ending arrive too early, it turned the mother into the villain,
  it told the audience something they should have worked out. The second
  gets at the secondary characters, which is where B1.2 actually lives:
  clarifying the feelings and motives of primary AND secondary characters
  in a story. Ask each student, not the group's spokesperson.
  Record: one line per group, and a tick beside the name of anyone who
  answered without looking at somebody else first.

The product evidence is the performance on Day 15 and the individual notes
written in class on Day 16.
