## The question

Grade 12 English in this province still usually means a Shakespeare play
and a canonical novel. Who decided that, and what is it for?

## Look at the evidence in front of you

The list on [[Reading/index|the reading page]]: a play from 1601, a
Canadian novel from 1985, five short texts, ten poems, and a choice of
independent study. Somebody chose all of it. Ask what the choices assume
about what a Grade 12 student should have read by January.

## Positions to consider

| Position | The argument | The objection |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shared reference | A canon gives a culture common ground and a shorthand | Common to whom? And imposed by whom, historically? |
| Difficulty | These texts reward the effort; many easier ones do not | Plenty of excluded texts are difficult, and were excluded anyway |
| Access | Students who arrive at university without Shakespeare are disadvantaged | Reproducing an advantage is not the same as justifying it |
| Replace it | A syllabus is a set of choices and can be made differently | Every replacement is also a canon, chosen by somebody |

## The honest version of my own position

I chose *Hamlet* because it is the best play I know for teaching how a
text withholds certainty, and *The Handmaid's Tale* because it argues
with the idea of testimony in a way that makes the play newly strange.
Both choices are defensible and neither is neutral, and you are entitled
to argue with them.
