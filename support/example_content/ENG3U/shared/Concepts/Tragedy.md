Tragedy is a form with a shape and a set of expectations, and *Macbeth*
both uses and strains them.

## The classical apparatus

| Term | What it means | The question it raises |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Tragic hero** | A figure of stature whose fall matters publicly | Is Macbeth still that, by Act 5? |
| **Hamartia** | The error or flaw that produces the fall | Ambition, or something more like impatience? |
| **Hubris** | Overreaching pride | Where exactly does it appear — and is it his or his wife's? |
| **Peripeteia** | The reversal | Which scene, and what makes you say so? |
| **Anagnorisis** | Recognition — the moment of seeing clearly | "Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow" — recognition or collapse? |
| **Catharsis** | What the audience is left with | Pity, terror, relief, or something colder |

Use the terms where they clarify. An essay that applies all six
mechanically is doing vocabulary, not analysis.

## The Shakespearean shape

Rise, turn, unravelling. The turn is usually mid-play and is usually a
choice rather than an accident — which is what distinguishes tragedy from
misfortune.

## What *Macbeth* does differently

- **We are inside the murderer.** The soliloquies make an audience share
  the reasoning of a man doing something monstrous.
- **The supernatural is unresolved.** The play never confirms whether the
  witches cause or merely predict.
- **The marriage is the engine**, and it disintegrates in inverse
  proportion to Macbeth's power.
- **It is short and it accelerates.** The form itself performs the
  narrowing of options.

> [!question] Worth arguing about
> Is the tragedy Macbeth's, Scotland's, or Lady Macbeth's? Each answer
> makes a different play, and all three can be defended from the text.
