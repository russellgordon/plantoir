Drop a strip of zinc into copper(II) sulfate solution and something
obvious happens: the strip goes dark, the blue fades, and the beaker
gets slightly warm. Electrons moved from the zinc to the copper ions,
about a millimetre, and the energy came out as heat you cannot use for
anything.

Today you separate the two halves into different beakers and make the
electrons take the long way round — through a wire, past a voltmeter.
The same reaction, the same electrons, and now you can put a number on
what the zinc was willing to pay.

## What you are trying to find out

If one metal will displace another from solution, then the two metals
are not equally willing to hand over electrons, and there ought to be a
way to measure the difference rather than just observe it.

That measurement is a **voltage**. It is the size of the push behind the
electrons, and it belongs to the *pair* of half-cells rather than to
either one alone — which is going to turn out to be the most important
sentence on the page.

Three things you are after:

1. **Can you rank the metals** by measuring cells, and does that ranking
   agree with the displacement reactions you can see in a test tube?
2. **Do the numbers add up?** If zinc against copper gives one voltage
   and magnesium against zinc gives another, does magnesium against
   copper give their sum? If it does, then each metal can be given a
   number of its own and the whole table collapses into a list.
3. **Which electrode is which, and how would you know without being
   told?** Oxidation happens at one of them and reduction at the other.
   Work out which from the direction the meter deflects, not from a
   mnemonic.

## What you have to work with

- **Metal strips**: magnesium, zinc, iron, and copper. Fine sandpaper or
  emery paper.
- **Solutions of the corresponding salts**, all at the same
  concentration, as supplied. Read the concentration off the container
  and record it — you will need it in the analysis and it will not be
  what the data booklet assumes.
- **A salt bridge**: a U-tube of potassium nitrate solution, or strips
  of filter paper soaked in it.
- **A digital voltmeter** and leads, small beakers, and a wash bottle.

Your design decisions, written and justified before the lab:

- **Which pairs you will build, and how many.** Four metals give six
  possible pairs. You do not need all six to build a ranking — say how
  many you actually need, which ones, and how you would use the ones you
  skipped as a **check** rather than as data.
- **Which metal you will use as your reference**, and why a reference is
  necessary at all. You cannot measure one half-cell. Everything you
  measure is a difference, so every number you report is relative to
  something, and you should choose that something on purpose.
- **How you will decide which electrode is the anode**, from your own
  measurement, before consulting anything.
- **How you will prepare the electrode surfaces**, identically, every
  time.
- **How many readings per cell**, over what time, and what agreement you
  will accept. A cell's voltage drifts. Decide when you will read it.
- **What you will do with the salt bridge between cells.** Reusing one
  bridge across six cells carries ions from each into the next. Say what
  you will do and why.

> [!note] Cell notation, and reading it as a picture rather than a code
> There is a shorthand for a cell and it is worth learning today,
> because it stops you having to draw a diagram every time.
>
> $$\ce{Zn(s)}\ |\ \ce{Zn^2+(aq)}\ ||\ \ce{Cu^2+(aq)}\ |\ \ce{Cu(s)}$$
>
> Read it left to right as a journey the electrons take. A single bar is
> a boundary between two phases — solid metal meeting its solution. The
> double bar in the middle is the salt bridge. **The anode is always
> written on the left and the cathode on the right**, so the notation
> encodes your answer to question 3 above, and writing it down commits
> you to a claim.
>
> The two rules that go with it never change and never swap, in this
> course or any other: **oxidation happens at the anode, reduction
> happens at the cathode.** Learn them as a pair. In the cell you build
> today the anode is the negative terminal — but that is a fact about
> galvanic cells specifically, and it is the part that flips when you
> get to electrolysis. The anode-is-oxidation rule does not flip. Ever.

> [!danger] Metal salt solutions, sanding dust, and hydrogen from electrolysis
> - **Every solution on this bench is a heavy-metal salt and is toxic.**
>   Copper(II) and iron salts are harmful if swallowed and harmful to
>   aquatic life. Nothing is tasted, nothing goes down the sink, and
>   everything goes into the labelled heavy-metal waste container.
>   **Wash your hands before you leave the room**, including on the days
>   you are sure you touched nothing.
> - **Eye protection from the first move to the last of the cleanup.**
>   Splashes are the normal failure mode with a lot of small beakers on
>   one bench.
> - **Sanding metal produces fine dust and sharp edges.** Sand over
>   paper, not over your solutions, and do not blow the dust off — brush
>   it into the bin. Magnesium ribbon in particular has edges that cut.
> - **Magnesium is flammable and burns fiercely.** Not an issue today
>   because nothing is hot, and worth knowing anyway: no flames near
>   this bench, and never try to light a strip to see what happens.
> - **Never connect anything here to a mains supply**, and never short
>   the leads of a cell together for longer than a moment. The
>   voltmeter's job is to measure without drawing current, and a wire
>   across a cell does the opposite.
> - **The electrolysis extension makes gas, and one of the gases is
>   hydrogen.** If your class runs it: use the low-voltage DC supply at
>   the setting I give you and nothing else, **no flames anywhere in the
>   room**, and **the cell is never sealed or covered** — a stoppered
>   electrolysis cell is a pressure vessel with a flammable gas in it,
>   built by accident. Run it for a short time, in a ventilated space,
>   and disconnect before you take it apart.
> - **The electrolyte for that extension is sodium sulfate solution with
>   universal indicator, and it is not negotiable.** Electrolysing a
>   chloride solution produces **chlorine**, which is toxic, and that is
>   exactly the class of accident the rule about never combining
>   unapproved chemicals exists to prevent. If a procedure you have seen
>   elsewhere uses salt water, it is not the procedure we are running.
> - **Nothing returns to a stock bottle**, and **report every incident
>   immediately**, however small.

## The prediction you write first

Before you build anything:

1. **Your ranking of the four metals**, most willing to give up
   electrons first. Commit to an order.
2. **The sign and rough magnitude of each cell you plan to build**, in
   volts, to one decimal place. You are guessing and it costs nothing.
3. **For your first cell: which strip is the anode**, and what you
   expect to see happen at each electrode over the period.
4. **Your additivity prediction.** Pick three metals, A, B and C. Write
   down what you expect the A-against-C voltage to be, in terms of the
   other two, **before you measure any of them**. This is the strongest
   prediction on the page because it is quantitative and it can fail.

Record voltages to the resolution the meter actually gives, and record
**every** reading including the ones that drifted. Note the time between
building the cell and taking the reading, because it matters.

Then the matrix, which is the result:

| | Mg | Zn | Fe | Cu |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Mg** | — | | | |
| **Zn** | | — | | |
| **Fe** | | | — | |
| **Cu** | | | | — |

Fill in the cells you measured; leave the rest blank. Then, from your
ranking, **predict** the blanks and check two of them. A prediction you
then test is worth more than four more measurements.

## What to bring to the consolidation

- Your prediction sheet, dated before the data, with the wrong ones
  still readable.
- Your table and your matrix.
- **Your ranking of the four metals**, with the evidence for each
  adjacent pair.
- **Your additivity check, as numbers.** State the two measured
  voltages, their sum, the third measured voltage, and the difference
  between the sum and the measurement. Then say whether that difference
  is bigger than the spread in your own repeat readings.
- **Your identification of the anode in one cell, and how you knew.**
  Not from a table — from what your meter and your eyes did.
- **The class matrix.** Everybody's zinc-against-copper on one number
  line. Look at whether the scatter is centred or piled to one side, and
  be ready to name a technique step that would produce the pattern you
  see.

## What you should not claim

- **You did not measure standard cell potentials, and the difference has
  a direction.** A standard potential is defined at specified
  concentrations and at 25 °C. Your solutions are almost certainly not
  at those concentrations and your room is not at 25 °C, so your numbers
  are not the booklet's numbers and should not be reported as though
  they were. Say what your conditions actually were.
- **Almost every fault at this bench lowers the reading.** An electrode
  with an oxide layer, a salt bridge that has dried at one end, a bridge
  that barely dips into the solution, a loose crocodile clip, a
  voltmeter that draws a little current — all of them add resistance or
  add an unwanted reaction, and all of them make the measured voltage
  come out **too low**. If your class sits below the booklet, look here
  before you look at the chemistry.
- **One thing changes the sign and not the size.** Leads on backwards
  gives you a negative number of the right magnitude. That is not a
  failed measurement; it is a labelling error, and the honest response
  is to say which strip you had on which terminal, not to drop the minus
  sign.
- **A voltage is not a rate and not a yield.** A cell with a large
  potential is telling you the reaction is strongly favoured. It is
  telling you nothing about how fast it will run, how long it will last,
  or how much charge it can deliver — which is why a battery's voltage
  and its capacity are printed separately on the label.
- **Cell potentials do not scale with the amount of stuff.** You will be
  tempted, when you get to balancing equations next class, to multiply a
  half-reaction's potential when you multiply the half-reaction. **Do
  not.** A potential is an *intensive* property, like temperature or
  density: two identical cells in parallel give the same voltage as one,
  and a bigger zinc strip does not push harder. Your own matrix is
  evidence for this if you used strips of different sizes — check
  whether it made any difference, and report that it did not.
- **A ranking is not a mechanism.** You have ordered four metals. You
  have not explained why they order that way, and the explanation is
  the next two classes rather than a sentence you can add to the
  conclusion.
- **Two readings on a drifting cell is not a measurement.** If the
  voltage fell steadily while you watched, report the drift as a range
  with a time attached, rather than picking the number you liked.
