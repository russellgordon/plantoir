These questions follow [[News Values]] and the repair work you did in
[[Headline Help Desk]]. A headline is a promise the story must keep.

## Questions

1. Write a headline for this story: the robotics club placed second
   at Saturday's qualifier and will compete at the provincial
   championship a year from now — a first in school history.
2. **Sharpen this.** "Student Council Meeting Held" — the story: the
   council voted to cancel the winter semi-formal after ticket sales
   covered less than a third of the venue cost.
3. **Sharpen this.** "An Interesting Week at the Book Fair" — the
   story: the fair raised $2,100, double last year's total, and the
   money buys graphic novels for the library.
4. **Explain why** "Ravens Take First Regional Title in Nine Years"
   works harder than "Ravens Win" — then name a situation where the
   two-word version is actually the better call.
5. **The accuracy trap.** The story: council will pilot a phone-free
   period in two Grade 9 classes for one month, then review it. The
   proposed headline: "School Bans Phones." What exactly does the
   headline claim that the story cannot back? Write one it can.
6. **Challenge.** Write two headlines for the food-drive story (1,214
   items, goal 800, Bridge Street pantry): one for the printed front
   page, one for a social post — and say why they differ.

## Answers

> [!success]- Answer 1
> Something like "Robotics Club Qualifies for Provincials — a School
> First." The rarity is the news, so *first* earns headline space.

> [!success]- Answer 2
> "Council Cancels Winter Semi-Formal" — the decision is the news,
> and "over ticket sales" earns its spot if the count allows. A
> meeting happening is never a headline.

> [!success]- Answer 3
> "Book Fair Doubles Its Haul: $2,100 for Library Graphic Novels."
> "Interesting" is a word that promises nothing — the number and the
> *double* are what make a reader stop.

> [!success]- Answer 4
> The long version carries the actual news — the nine-year first.
> "Ravens Win" earns its keep when the space is tiny or when everyone
> already knows the stakes, like a scoreboard graphic on game night.

> [!success]- Answer 5
> Three false claims: *school* (two classes), *bans* (a pilot with a
> review), and the missing *one month*. Supported: "Two Grade 9 Classes
> to Try a Month Without Phones." Less dramatic — and that is the
> point: drama the story cannot back is a correction waiting to happen.

> [!success]- Answer 6
> More than one right pair. Print might run "Food Drive Smashes Goal
> with 1,214 Items"; social might run "We asked for 800. You brought
> 1,214." Print headlines label a story on a page; social headlines
> *are* the story, so they can speak directly to the reader.
