## What you are making

The symposium is the finale: everyone presents the function behind
[[The Signature Function]] to invited guests — other teachers,
administrators, family — who circulate the room the way visitors
work a science fair. Your station holds the graph, the data, the
fitted equation, and you. Every group answers the same two
questions, from every guest who asks: *why is this the right
model?* and *where does it stop being one?* You finish with a
five-minute defence you can give repeatedly, a one-page handout for
your station, and answers to whatever the room throws at you.

If you built [[The Signature Function]] with a partner you stand at one
station together, and you are still marked separately: I come to every
station once and hear the part of the defence that is yours, then ask
you one question of my own.

## Milestones

- [ ] Station materials made: graph, data, equation, one-page handout
- [ ] The five-minute defence rehearsed until it survives
      interruptions
- [ ] The two symposium questions answered in writing first, then out
      loud, per [[Showing Your Thinking]]
- [ ] [[Final Reflection]] begun while the day is still fresh

## How it is assessed

Per [[How Marks Work]], communication is the evidence today: precise
vocabulary, representations chosen for the audience in front of you,
and honesty about the model's limits. This is the smaller of the two
pieces that make up the final thirty per cent of your mark; the
[[Final Examination]] is the larger.

Guests leave written feedback and it is worth reading, but no
visitor's opinion is part of anybody's mark — the evaluation is mine and
it comes from the defence I hear and the question I ask. Afterwards your
[[Math Journal]] closes with [[Final Reflection]], which I read and do
not score.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like in your work |
| --- | --- |
| A clear story | A stranger follows the model in five minutes |
| Precision | Vocabulary and notation used correctly, aloud |
| Grace under fire | Questions answered, not deflected |
| Honest limits | The model's edges volunteered, not extracted |
| Built for the room | The station is readable by a stranger in a minute, and nothing on it needs this course to decode |

> [!success]- If nerves are the obstacle
> The defence is a conversation, not a recital. You have answered
> these two questions all semester; the guests are simply new people
> asking them. Rehearse once with someone patient, then trust the
> preparation.

Triangulation — the evidence that never arrives on its own.

Symposium day itself is unusable for this: you are hosting a room of guests
and cannot hear a station for more than the defence you came for. Both
prompts below therefore sit on the two preparation periods, which is where
the choosing actually happens.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 17, the station-building working period

  Watch what gets CUT. A station is a choice about what to leave out, and the
  handout shows only what survived — so the decision is invisible by the
  eighteenth. The tell is what a student does with their best-looking piece
  of work when it turns out not to answer either of the two questions the
  station exists to answer.

  Going well: a graph a student is plainly proud of comes off the board,
  because it is decoration rather than argument. Or a whole strand's worth of
  algebra reduced to one line with the working folded behind it.

  Stuck: the station keeps growing, and the answer to "what is this for?" is
  "it took ages".

  Record: on your class list, one word for the thing each student cut. A
  blank beside a name means nothing was cut, which is itself the finding.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 12, at the dress rehearsal already on that agenda

  Ask: "A guest looks at your graph and says, 'so it is just a line of best
  fit, then.' What do you say back?"

  Then: "Point at the one thing on your station a person who never took this
  course will misread, and tell me the sentence you will use when they do."

  On the first, the strong answer refuses the framing without being rude
  about it: the family was chosen because of something the phenomenon DOES —
  it repeats, it saturates, it cannot go negative, it grows by a percentage —
  and only then fitted. A student who answers "no, it's a sinusoid" has said
  nothing; a student who names the structural feature that ruled the rivals
  out is doing D3.1 out loud, and it is the answer that will be needed six
  times on the eighteenth.

  On the second, listen for a specific artefact rather than a worry: an axis
  that starts at forty rather than zero, a smooth curve drawn through points
  that were measured monthly, a tail extrapolated past the last real
  reading. That is the communicating half of D3.3 — results interpreted for
  the person in front of you — and a student who cannot find anything
  misreadable on their own station has not yet looked at it as a stranger.

  Record: two words per student on your class list, at the desk, while they
  are still talking.

The product evidence is the station, the handout, and the defence given on
Day 18.
