> [!abstract] At a glance
> Individual · three hours, in the examination period · written, with a
> calculator and one formula sheet supplied · all four strands, weighted
> as the course spent its time

## What it is for

Advanced Functions is the prerequisite course. What you can do
unassisted, under time, is exactly what MCV4U and first-year
mathematics will assume on day one — so this paper is less a
gatekeeping exercise than an honest measurement of what you are taking
with you.

## What is on it

| Part | Roughly | What it asks you to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A. Exponential and logarithmic | 25% | Convert between forms; evaluate logarithms without technology; apply the laws; transform and graph; solve exponential and logarithmic equations, including problems |
| B. Trigonometric | 25% | Radian measure and the unit circle; graph and transform sinusoids in radians; the tangent function; prove identities; solve trigonometric equations |
| C. Polynomial and rational | 25% | Factor theorem and zeros with multiplicity; sketch from an equation; asymptotes and end behaviour; solve inequalities with a sign chart |
| D. Characteristics and rates of change | 25% | Even and odd; combining and composing functions; average and instantaneous rates; sketch a graph from a described rate; solve graphically where algebra will not |

## What to expect, precisely

- **Evaluate logarithms without a calculator.** $\log_8 2$ and
  $\log_{10} 0.001$ are testing the definition, not your device —
  [[Logarithms as Undoing]] is the page, and the method is "what power
  of the base is this?".
- **Exact values without technology.** The special angles and their
  multiples up to $2\pi$, for the primary ratios and the reciprocal
  ones — built off [[The Unit Circle]], not recalled from a list.
- **Radians throughout the trigonometry.** Degrees appear nowhere, and
  the period of $y = a\sin(k(x-d)) + c$ is $\tfrac{2\pi}{|k|}$.
- **One tangent-function question**, where there is no amplitude and
  the period is $\pi$. It is testing whether you noticed.
- **One ratio you evaluate with your calculator**, from a radian
  measure given as a decimal rather than as a multiple of $\pi$ — a
  reciprocal ratio among them, so the calculator's missing keys matter.
- **One sinusoid whose equation you write from its graph**, reading the
  four parameters off the picture rather than the picture off the
  equation.
- **One logarithmic graph to transform**, with the equation of the
  asymptote, the domain and the range stated afterwards — the parameters
  behave exactly as [[The Same Four Parameters]] says, and the asymptote
  is the one thing only a horizontal move can shift.
- **One applied problem on a logarithmic scale** — a magnitude, a
  decibel reading, a pH, or a doubling time — solved algebraically and
  answered in the units of the situation, per [[Logarithmic Scales]].
- **Identity proofs** are marked on layout: one side only, each line
  following from the one above.
- **One question is deliberately not solvable by algebra**, and asks for
  a graphical or numerical solution with the method stated. Presenting a
  numerical root as though it were exact loses the mark that question
  exists to award.
- **One question describes a situation in words** and asks for a sketch
  showing the rate of change, then a verification at three points.
  [[Rates of Change in the World]] has the technique.
- **One composition with its domain**, where the domain of $f(g(x))$ is
  not the domain of either piece by itself.
- **One rational function sketched from its equation**, with every
  asymptote justified from the algebra rather than read off a screen.
- **One polynomial inequality**, solved with a sign chart and answered
  as an interval.

## How each part is judged

The same four kinds of thinking as everything else in this course —
[[How Marks Work]] names them — but here is what that looks like on a
paper written under time.

| What earns the mark | What it looks like on the page |
| --- | --- |
| The method is visible | Every line follows from the one above it, and a reader can see the decision as well as the arithmetic |
| The answer is exact where exactness was asked for | Radicals and $\pi$ left alone; a decimal only where a decimal was requested |
| The restriction is written down | Domains, excluded values, and the values an identity is silent at, on the page rather than in your head |
| The units and the context come back | An interval turned into hours, a rate turned into the phenomenon's own words |
| A stalled question still shows a plan | The method named in a sentence, which earns marks that a blank space cannot |

## How to prepare

1. **Redo problems, do not reread them.** Five from each set, cold, with
   only the formula sheet: [[Logarithm Practice]],
   [[Radian Measure Practice]],
   [[Identities and Equations Practice]],
   [[Polynomial Graphing Practice]], [[Rational Functions Practice]],
   [[Rates of Change Practice]].
2. **Rebuild the connections.** The four parameters are one idea across
   four families of function; a logarithm is an exponential seen from
   the side; a transformation is a composition. Draw that map from
   memory — the gaps are your study list.
3. **Sketch from memory.** Each parent function, then one transformation
   of each. If you cannot draw it, you cannot analyse it under time.
4. **Say the proofs out loud.** If you cannot state the first move
   before writing, you are hoping rather than working.
5. **Bring questions to the review classes.** This page is what is on
   the examination.

> [!tip] In the three hours
> Budget by the weightings — they are even, and so should your time be.
> If a question stalls, write the method you intended in words and move
> on: a described method earns marks, and a blank space earns none.

## How this is assessed

Against the same expectations as everything else. Per [[How Marks Work]],
this examination is part of the final 30% of the course mark alongside
[[The Functions Symposium]], so that neither one afternoon nor one
project decides your grade alone.

Triangulation — and the honest thing to say about this one.

This evaluation is a product and nothing else: three hours, alone, on paper.
No observation and no conversation feed its mark, and pretending otherwise
would be a nicer note than a true one. What the four review classes give you
is different and still worth the minutes — the last chance to find a
misconception while there are periods left to spend on it, and the last
chance to tell a student where they stand while they can still do something
about it. Both change what you TEACH in the classes that follow them
rather than what you write in a mark book.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 20, the review clinic at the boards

  Watch the first thirty seconds on a cold mixed problem, before any writing.
  Everything on this paper begins with a classification — which family is
  this, and what is actually being asked — and a student who starts
  manipulating before classifying will lose an hour on the eighteenth to
  questions they can do.

  Going well: the family named out loud, then the form of the answer
  predicted ("this is going to be an interval"), and only then a pen.

  Stuck: the pen moves first, and the first move is whatever was practised
  most recently rather than whatever the question asked for. Watch
  particularly for a sign chart started on an equation, or denominators
  cleared in an inequality — Unit 2 spent a period on why that costs the
  answer.

  Record: on your class list, one letter per student for the strand they
  reached for first when the problem was not from that strand. Three of the
  same letter across the room is your Day 22 lesson, already written.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 23, at the individual conferences already on that agenda

  Ask: "Of the four parts, which would you rather sit first, and what does
  that tell you?"

  Then: "Name one thing you could learn in the next two days that would
  change your paper the most. Not the thing you like least — the thing with
  the most marks attached."

  The first is a warm question with a cold answer inside it: students almost
  always name the part they find easiest, and the useful follow-up is why
  they want the easy one first. The second is the one worth waiting for. A
  strong answer is specific and weighted — "sketching rational functions
  from the equation, because it is in Part C and it comes back in Part D
  when they ask me to describe end behaviour" — rather than "everything" or
  "trig". A student who cannot name one has not yet looked at the paper's
  shape, and the fix is two minutes with the table at the top of this page.

  Record: one line per student, and a tally on the board of the topics named.
  The top three are what the open clinic runs on.

The product evidence is the paper itself.
