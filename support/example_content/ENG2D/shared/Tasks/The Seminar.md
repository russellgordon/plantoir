> [!abstract] At a glance
> Groups of four · one 25-minute seminar led by your group, plus what
> you contribute in everybody else's · a question, two passages, and a
> written follow-up · assessed on Communication, Thinking, and Knowledge

## The task

Lead a discussion of [[Moon of the Crusted Snow]] that other people want
to be in.

## What your group brings

1. **One question** that cannot be answered yes or no, and that the
   novel does not settle. Approved by me on day 2 of this task, before
   anybody leads.
2. **Two passages**, with page numbers, that the room will read aloud.
3. **A plan for the silence.** What you will do when nobody answers:
   a smaller question, a vote, a "read this line again and tell me what
   the verb is doing".
4. **A closing move**: what you will ask the room to leave with.

## What being led looks like

You are not presenting. A seminar leader speaks less than a fifth of the
time. The skills are on [[Seminar Skills]]: opening with evidence,
building, complicating, inviting, steelmanning, and conceding.

## The follow-up

Individually, after your seminar: 400 words on what your question
produced. Not a summary of what people said — an argument about what the
discussion revealed about the novel, including anything the room found
that you had not expected.

Then a last paragraph, marked with the rest. Name one thing you did
beforehand that turned out to matter; one thing you did in the room to
keep the discussion moving — a smaller question, a vote, a line read
again, a concession; and one thing you did afterwards, in the days
between the seminar and this, that changed what you now think. Say which
of the three was worth the most, and name the one move you have avoided
all term and will use next time.

## How it is marked

| Category | What I am looking for |
| --- | --- |
| Knowledge | The passages you brought and the question you helped build show that you have read the whole novel |
| Thinking | Your follow-up argues something about the novel, using the discussion as its evidence, and says where the argument is still open |
| Communication | You listen, respond to what was actually said, and make room for other people |
| Communication, in the last paragraph | One thing named from before your seminar, one from during it and one from after; the most useful of the three picked out; and the next move specific enough to watch for |

## What you do in the five seminars you do not lead

Marked, individually, on the same terms as your own: a contribution that
brings evidence, one that takes up what somebody has actually just said,
one that concedes a point. Five contributions that would have fitted any
question are worth less than one that could only have been made in that
room.

This is not a count. A mark for how often you speak would be a mark for
confidence, and confidence is not on the achievement chart.

> [!important] There is no group mark on this task
> Your group builds one discussion together; everything that carries a
> mark is yours on its own — what you do while your group leads, your
> follow-up, and what you do in the five seminars you sit in.

> [!tip] The best seminar question this room has had
> "Aileen says the word 'apocalypse' does not fit. Is the novel agreeing
> with her, or with the reader who brought the word?" It is answerable in
> four different ways, all with passages, and no group has ever finished
> it in twenty-five minutes.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

One thing to hold on to on all three seminar days: what is being judged
is how a student reads this novel — the passages they can find, the
inferences they can hold up — and never their opinion of the community
the novel is about.

OBSERVE — Unit 3, Days 8, 9 and 11, the seminars, and specifically the
first real silence in each one
  Every seminar has one, usually inside the first eight minutes. The
  group has already handed you a plan for it, so the plan is product;
  what no page can tell you is whether it survived contact. Somebody else
  is running these periods, and you are already sitting at the side of the
  room with a class list open for contributions — so the watching costs
  nothing extra.
  Watch for: who moves first, and how long they wait. Four seconds of
  held silence is a decision; filling it at half a second is a different
  one, and both leaders will describe it afterwards as "I kept it going".
  Going well: the smaller question comes out, or a passage is read again,
  or the leader says nothing and somebody else starts.
  Stuck: the leader answers their own question, then asks a second one
  before anybody has replied to the first.
  Record: one extra column on the seminar list you are already keeping,
  three states — waited, used the plan, filled it. One mark per group per
  day.
  Evidence for A2.2, and be precise about which part: this is ONE
  interpersonal speaking strategy, adapted to the situation. The
  "variety" the expectation asks for is built across the five seminars
  each student also sits in, which is what the contribution column is
  already for. What this column adds is the only four seconds in the unit
  when the situation cannot be prepared for.

TALK — Unit 3, Day 13, the drafting period for the follow-up, with
seminar notes open
  This is the same round of the room that leaves a note on each opening
  claim: leave the note, then take the minute. You will not reach
  everybody in one period — finish the row on Day 14, while the room
  revises from those notes.
  Steer clear of everything the follow-up's own brief asks for. They are
  drafting answers to it in front of you.
  Ask: "Something was said in your seminar that you did not follow at the
  time. What was it, and what do you now think it meant?"
  Then: "Name something the novel never says outright that your seminar
  agreed on anyway. What made the room sure?"
  A strong first answer can reproduce somebody else's sentence roughly,
  say why it did not land, and say what changed since. That is the AFTER
  half of A1.3 — listening comprehension strategies used before, during
  and after listening — and it is the half that leaves no trace anywhere:
  nothing is handed in from listening, and a student who was never
  confused was not listening either.
  A strong second answer states a proposition the book never puts in a
  sentence — that the novel has made up its mind about Scott long before
  anybody in it has, that a decision was taken before the meeting that
  appears to take it — and then traces it to two or three places that
  imply it. That is B1.4: making an inference about
  a text and explaining it. A student who can only offer something the
  novel says outright has been following the discussion rather than
  reading, and their follow-up will summarise at four hundred words.
  Record: one line per student on the drafting list, in seating order,
  and a circle beside anyone whose "inference" turns out to be something
  the novel states outright.

The products arrive on their own: the seminar the group leads on Day 8,
9 or 11, and the follow-up handed in on Unit 3, Day 14.
