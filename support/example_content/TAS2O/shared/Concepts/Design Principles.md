There was a moment in [[Build the Front Page]] when you moved one
headline, and the whole page suddenly snapped into place. That snap has
a vocabulary. Four principles explain nearly every layout decision a
designer makes, and every one of them is really an instruction to the
reader's eye.

## The page is a path

Nobody reads a page all at once. The eye lands somewhere, travels, and
leaves — and design decides the route. A well-designed front page walks
a reader from the biggest story to the smallest without them noticing
they were guided; a badly designed one strands them, and they leave
early. Layout is not decoration on top of the journalism. It is the
last act of the journalism: deciding, spatially, what matters most —
the same judgement [[News Values]] describes in words.

## Four principles

| Principle | What it tells the reader |
| --- | --- |
| Contrast | "Start here" — big against small creates order |
| Repetition | "This is one publication" — same styles, every page |
| Alignment | "This is deliberate" — everything shares an edge |
| Proximity | "These belong together" — captions touch their photos |

Each has a failure mode worth knowing. Timid contrast — everything
almost the same size — creates mud, not calm. A caption an inch from
its photo becomes a mystery; an element aligned with nothing looks like
an accident. The four work as a system: proximity groups things,
alignment connects the groups, repetition relates the pages, and
contrast says what to read first. When a layout feels wrong, test it
against each in turn — that diagnosis is the whole game in
[[Layout Practice]], and the standard your spread will be held to in
[[The Front Page]].

One ingredient is so large it earns its own page: the type itself,
covered in [[Typography]].
