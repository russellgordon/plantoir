A seminar is a discussion where the participants have all read the thing
and the teacher is not the source of answers. It is assessed in
[[The Macbeth Seminar]], and it is the closest thing in school to how ideas
actually get tested.

## Before

- **Read with marks**, per [[Close Reading]].
- **Write two questions that cannot be answered yes or no.** The best
  seminar questions begin "Why does…", "What is the effect of…", or
  "Which matters more…".
- **Choose two passages** you will bring to any seminar — three, with
  act, scene, and line, if you are the one leading it. Nobody waits while
  you find it.

## During

| Move | Sounds like |
| --- | --- |
| Open with evidence | "At 1.7.1 he says \"If it were done…\" — which suggests…" |
| Build | "That connects to what Amir said about…" |
| Complicate | "That works for Act 1, but what about the banquet?" |
| Invite | "Sara, you had a different reading of that scene." |
| Steelman | "The strongest version of that argument is…, and even then…" |
| Concede | "You are right, and it changes my claim to…" |

The last one is worth more than any of the others. A seminar where nobody
concedes anything is four people practising a speech.

## What lowers the quality of a seminar

- **Talking to be counted.** Repeating a point in new words is not
  contribution.
- **The plot summary.** Everybody read it.
- **The unanswerable pivot** — "well, that is just your opinion" — which
  ends the argument rather than advancing it.
- **Silence used as a weapon.** If you have nothing, ask a real question.

## If you find speaking difficult

Prepare a specific first sentence and use it in the first three minutes.
Speaking early is far easier than speaking after twenty minutes of
listening, and it is the trick every experienced seminar participant
uses.
