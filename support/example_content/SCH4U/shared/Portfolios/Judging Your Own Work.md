Every task page in this course carries its criteria before the work
starts, and most people read them once, on launch day, and then hand in
something that misses two rows they could have fixed in twenty minutes.

This page is those twenty minutes. It is a routine, not a mood, and it
takes about that long once you have done it twice.

We run it together the first time, on a piece of work that is not
yours — a property sheet from another year — so that the move is
already familiar when the work being judged is your own.

## The routine

1. Put the criteria table beside your draft. The actual table, open, not
   your memory of it.
2. Go down it row by row and write **yes**, **not yet**, or **partly**
   against each one — with one sentence of evidence: the section, the
   number, the sentence a reader would point at. "Yes" with nothing
   beside it is a hope.
3. Find your **weakest** row. Not the quickest to fix. The weakest.
4. Write one sentence saying what you will change and when.
5. Change it, in what is left of this period.

Steps 2 and 3 are the whole exercise. Naming your weakest row honestly
is harder than any of the fixing, because it means writing down the
thing you were hoping nobody would look at closely.

> [!example]- One row, judged honestly
> From [[The Property Prediction]], the *Accounting for gaps* row: level
> 3 is *"differences are noted"*, level 4 is *"each difference is
> diagnosed, and the diagnosis is specific"*.
>
> **Partly.** Four of my five differences have a diagnosis attached. The
> melting point one says "the published value was different", which
> diagnoses nothing — and it is the largest gap on the page, about
> 300 °C. I think my classification was right and I expected the wrong
> end of the range for a covalent network solid, but I have not written
> that anywhere. Weakest row.
>
> **Change:** write the melting-point gap up as the second of the four
> kinds of miss — right type, wrong end of its range — and say what in
> the structure should have told me to expect the high end. Ten minutes,
> now.
>
> That paragraph is worth more than "looks fine" written against all
> nine rows, and it took less time to produce.

## Why this is a chemistry skill and not a study tip

The whole course asks you to judge what your own evidence is entitled to
support. A titration curve, an enthalpy that came out low, a rate ratio
with a spread of ten per cent — each one arrives with a question about
how far to trust it, and answering that question is the work rather than
the paperwork around it.

Judging your own draft against a published standard is the same move,
pointed at a different object. If you can say where your calorimeter
stopped being trustworthy, you can say where your discussion section
does, and the second one is easier.

## Where this fits in your mark

Your judgement of your own work is not part of your mark, and neither is
a classmate's — [[How Marks Work]] says so plainly, and it cuts both
ways. Nothing you write here can cost you anything, which is exactly
what makes it worth writing honestly. The mark comes from what you
finally hand in, and this routine exists to make that better before it
leaves your hands.

The self-check is never scheduled after something has gone in, for one
reason: a judgement you have no time to act on is just a worry with a
table attached. On the four report days, where the work is handed in at
the bell, it runs twenty minutes before that bell — the last moment at
which it can still change something.

> [!tip] Read your weakest-row sentence out loud
> To the person beside you, or to the wall. Generosity is audible in a
> way it is not visible, and you will usually hear it yourself before
> anybody says anything.
