These questions follow [[Composition and Framing]] and the shooting you
did for [[Portraits with Purpose]]. Critique each described photo with
the vocabulary from the concept page — name the problem, then fix it.

## Questions

1. The photo: a student dead centre in the frame, a metre of empty
   ceiling above her head, her feet cropped off at the ankles. Name
   two problems using composition vocabulary.
2. The photo: a portrait of the drama club president — sharp, well
   lit, and with an exit sign growing out of his head against a
   cluttered noticeboard. What went wrong, and when to catch it?
3. The photo: the championship point, shot from the top of the
   bleachers — the players are specks. What is the fix, and why is
   it not the zoom?
4. **Explain why** a photographer covering the Grade 1 reading-buddy
   visit should spend most of the visit kneeling.
5. The scene: a long hallway of lockers, a custodian waxing the
   floor at the far end. What compositional gift is the hallway
   offering, and how would you frame to accept it?
6. **Challenge.** One frame, two crops: wide shows the whole robotics
   team mid-celebration with the arena behind them; tight shows two
   teammates mid-hug, faces filling the frame. Argue for each; choose.

## Answers

> [!success]- Answer 1
> Too much headroom — the empty ceiling pushes her down the frame —
> and an awkward crop at the ankles (crop between joints, or not at
> all). Placing her off-centre on a thirds line would help too.

> [!success]- Answer 2
> Background failure — the frame has two subjects: him, and the noise
> behind him. Catch it *before* the shutter: check the edges and
> behind the head, then move him or yourself. Two steps was the fix.

> [!success]- Answer 3
> Fill the frame with what matters — move your feet, get courtside.
> Zooming from the bleachers flattens the scene and magnifies every
> shake; moving changes the *angle*, which zoom never can.

> [!success]- Answer 4
> Eye level is where the story is. Shooting down at small children
> makes them look small; kneeling puts the reader inside their world
> instead of above it. Camera height is a choice — make it on purpose.

> [!success]- Answer 5
> Leading lines. The rows of lockers converge toward the custodian —
> frame so the lines run from the corners toward him and let the
> hallway walk the reader's eye to the subject.

> [!success]- Answer 6
> Both are defensible. The wide shot carries *context* — the arena
> says provincials, the whole team says team. The tight crop carries
> *emotion*, but it could be any two people anywhere. Many editors
> would take the faces and let the words carry the where. Whichever
> you pick, be able to say what you gave up.
