A time series is one measurement repeated. Almost every mistake people
make with economic data is made in the first ten seconds of looking at
one, so slow those ten seconds down.

Start with four questions, in this order, before you say anything about
the line.

**What is on the vertical axis?** A level or a rate of change? Employment
in thousands of people and employment growth in per cent are different
series that can point in opposite directions at the same moment.

**Compared with what?** Month over month, or the same month a year
earlier? The Consumer Price Index rose 2.8% year over year in June 2026
(Statistics Canada, table 18-10-0004-01, released 20 July 2026), while
excluding gasoline it was 2.2%. Same month, same country, two numbers,
both true.

**Is it seasonally adjusted?** Retail employment always rises in November
and falls in January. Seasonal adjustment removes the part of the change
that happens every year so you can see the part that does not. Comparing
an adjusted series to an unadjusted one is the most common unforced error
in this course.

**Real or nominal?** See [[Index Numbers and Real Versus Nominal]] — this
one deserves its own page.

## What a turning point actually looks like

Real GDP fell 0.2% in the fourth quarter of 2025 and was flat, at 0.0%, in
the first quarter of 2026 (table 36-10-0104-01, released 29 May 2026).
Monthly GDP by industry then rose 0.3% in May 2026, with a flash estimate
of 0.2% for June (table 36-10-0434-01, released 31 July 2026). Three
readings, three different pictures of the same economy, and none of them
is the whole story — which is why [[Business Cycles]] insists that a
recession is dated after the fact rather than declared during it.

> [!success]- Why the last point on a chart is the least trustworthy
> Because it is usually preliminary. Flash estimates get revised, and the
> revision is often larger than the change being reported. In the example
> above, June 2026 is a flash estimate and May is a first estimate; both
> may move before the quarterly figure appears on 28 August 2026. Argue
> from the shape of several points, not the height of the last one.

Two more habits worth building. Look at the axis origin — a chart that
starts at 6.0% rather than zero makes a 0.1-point move in the
unemployment rate look like a cliff. And read the length of the series:
the July 2026 unemployment rate of 6.4% was the third consecutive monthly
decline and the lowest since July 2024 (table 14-10-0287-01, released
7 August 2026), which is a claim about a run of months, not about July.
