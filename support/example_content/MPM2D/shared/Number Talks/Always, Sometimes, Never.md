A claim goes up — "a quadrilateral's diagonals bisect each other" —
and everyone must file it under *always*, *sometimes*, or *never*.
"Sometimes" is not a shrug: it obliges you to produce a case where the
claim holds, one where it fails, and the exact boundary between them.

## How we play

1. Classify silently first. Gut verdicts welcome; they get audited.
2. "Always" and "never" demand an argument covering every case.
3. "Sometimes" demands an example, a counter-example, and the boundary.

> [!example]- The diagonals claim, argued (click to expand)
> - "A square's diagonals cut each other in half — so not *never*."
> - "A kite fails it — one diagonal is bisected, not both. Sometimes."
> - "Grid check: vertices $(0,0)$, $(4,0)$, $(6,3)$, $(2,3)$ — both
>   diagonals share midpoint $(3, 1.5)$. Parallelograms are the when."
> - "Then the sharpened claim — *the diagonals of a parallelogram
>   always bisect each other* — is one we could actually prove."

## One variation

Trig claims: "$\sin A < 1$ for an acute angle $A$" — always, and the
reason lives inside the triangle. "Similar triangles are congruent" —
sometimes, and saying *when* is the line [[Similar Triangles]] draws.

> [!tip] "Sometimes" is where the mathematics is
> The boundary of a claim is its content, and chasing it with
> coordinates is [[Properties on the Grid]] in miniature. The habit of
> demanding arguments is [[What Makes a Proof Convincing]] — the
> standard [[The Quadrilateral Case File]] will hold you to.
