## What it is for

Everything else you hand in this semester is work you had weeks for,
help with, and the chance to rewrite. This is the one piece of
evidence that is unambiguously yours, produced in the same three
hours as everyone else's. That is its whole purpose — not to catch
anybody out, and not to be the course's real opinion of you. It is
the larger half of the thirty per cent described in
[[How Marks Work]]; [[The Data Symposium]] is the other half, and the
two together are why neither an afternoon nor a project decides this
alone.

Because it reaches across the whole course, the first unit is on it. The
counting arguments from the first fortnight turn up again inside a
binomial probability in Part B, and the sampling frame you argued
about in Unit 3 turns up again in Part D. That is not a trick; it is
the shape the course has had all along.

## What is on it

| Part | Roughly | What it asks you to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A. Counting and probability | 25% | Count arrangements and selections — including from a row of Pascal's triangle — build a sample space, decide whether two events are mutually exclusive, and compute probabilities that are conditional, complementary, joint, or "A or B" |
| B. Distributions | 25% | Recognize which distribution a situation is, build it, compute an expected value, use the normal model and a $z$-score |
| C. Data and its collection | 30% | Separate a population from the sample that stood for it, name a sampling method and its weakness, summarize one- and two-variable data, read a regression and its residuals, interpret a margin of error and the confidence level printed beside it |
| D. Reading a claim | 20% | One published claim, dissected: who was measured, what was compared, what the graph does, and what the data can carry |

Part C is the largest because the middle half of this course is data,
and Part D is last because it is where all of it is for.

## What to expect, precisely

- **Deciding, not just calculating.** Several questions give you a
  situation and ask which technique fits before any arithmetic is
  possible — permutation or combination, binomial or hypergeometric,
  mean or median; a combination or a row of Pascal's triangle. The
  deciding is the marked part, and a correct calculation of the wrong
  quantity earns very little.
- **Tables and diagrams are welcome.** A tree, a two-way table of a
  thousand people, a rough histogram in the margin — these are
  methods, not scratch work, and a marker who can see your route can
  give you credit when the last line goes wrong.
- **One data set, several questions.** Part C hands you a small real
  data set, printed with its column descriptions, and asks four or
  five things of it in sequence. Read the descriptions first; the
  same discipline [[Reading a Data Dictionary]] asks for.
- **One claim from the world.** Part D prints a short article extract
  and its graph. You will not have seen it. You will have practised
  exactly this on [[The Statistical Claim Report]] and every Unit 4
  number talk.
- **No investigation questions.** Strand E — your own question, your
  own data, your own defence — is evaluated where it happens, at
  [[The Data Symposium]] and across the milestones of
  [[The Culminating Investigation]]. Nothing on this paper asks you to
  invent a study in an afternoon.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like on the paper |
| --- | --- |
| The right question answered | The technique matches the situation, chosen before the arithmetic |
| A visible route | Table, tree, sketch, or working — enough that a reader can follow you |
| Notation that means what you intend | $P(A \mid B)$ written when you mean it, not when you mean $P(B \mid A)$ |
| Numbers interpreted, not just produced | Each answer followed by what it says about the situation |
| Claims sized to the evidence | Conclusions that stop where the data stops, including "cannot tell from this" |

## How to prepare

1. **Work problems cold, do not reread notes.** Take three problems
   from each unit and do them without looking at anything. Reading a
   solution you already understand builds confidence and nothing else.
2. **Redo the ones you got wrong the first time.** The checkpoints you
   marked yourself, and the revision lists that came out of them, are a
   map of exactly what to practise. That is what they were for.
3. **Finish the practice sets you abandoned.** The pages in
   [[Exercises/index|Exercises]] were written with this paper in mind,
   and the ones you skipped are not a coincidence.
4. **Your journal is a study guide.** The entries where you named
   something foggy are the most efficient revision in the course,
   because you wrote them at the moment of not-understanding.
5. **Bring questions to the review classes**, rather than a hope of
   being told what is on the paper. This page is what is on the paper.

> [!tip] On the reading question, read the numbers last
> In Part D the instinct is to start with the statistic. Start with
> the caption and the axis instead, then who was measured and how
> many. Half the marks in that part are for noticing something before
> you have done any arithmetic at all — the habit
> [[The Graph Gallery]] and [[Correlation and Causation]] spent Unit 4
> building.

## How this is assessed

Against the same expectations as everything else, and with the same
standards the tasks used: a number without an interpretation is half
an answer, and a confident claim the data cannot carry costs more than
an honest "the sample is too small to tell". Nothing new is being
asked of you here. It is simply the only occasion all semester where
nobody can help you, which is exactly what makes it worth having.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

Three hours of silent paper is the purest product evidence this course
produces, and it is also the one occasion where the other two kinds of
evidence are unavailable by design: a room in which every student is
being treated identically is a room you cannot walk around asking
questions in. So this prompt does not try. It points at the four
review classes instead, Days 19 to 22, where the same expectations are
still visible in a student's hands and where noticing something can
still change how somebody spends the weekend.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 19, review part one at the boards
  Watch for what happens BEFORE the pencil moves on a counting
  problem. Some students settle whether order matters and only then
  reach for notation; others write a factorial and check afterwards
  whether it was the right one. Both routes can reach the same
  answer on a familiar problem, and the finished script cannot tell
  you which one you are looking at — which is why the boards are the
  only place this is visible. It is A2.1's "distinguish between
  situations that involve the use of permutations and those that
  involve the use of combinations", watched rather than marked.
  Going well: the words "does order matter here" said out loud, or a
  small case listed by hand before any formula appears.
  Stuck: notation first, then a hunt for a reason to keep it.
  Record: three columns on the class list — decides, checks after,
  neither. The third column tends to be the students who arrive at Day
  22 with no questions to ask, so keep it and go to them first.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 22, at the individual conferences already on that
agenda
  Put an unfamiliar two-way table of a thousand people on the desk
  between you — a screening result, a returned survey, anything with
  four cells, with one row and one column shaded. Ask: "Out of the
  shaded ROW, what fraction is in the shaded cell? Now out of the
  shaded COLUMN. Those are two different questions about the same
  people — say what each one is asking, and tell me which one a
  newspaper would print." Then, if there is time: "Which of the two
  should be the one printed on a leaflet in a waiting room, and who is
  harmed if it is the other?"
  A strong answer works from the counts in front of them rather than
  from a percentage quoted in the stem, and treats the two conditional
  probabilities as genuinely different quantities rather than as one
  fact stated two ways. That is A1.6 — determine whether one event is
  conditional on another, and solve related problems — heard rather
  than read. A student whose written work locks up against a clock can
  be fluent here, and this is the last chance in the course to find
  that out about them.
  Record: one line each on the conference list, plus which of the two
  conditional probabilities they reached for first.

The paper is the product evidence, and nobody has to go and get it.
