A newspaper is four different sources bound together. The news column
reports; the editorial argues; the advertisement sells; the cartoon
persuades in a second and a half. They were printed on the same page and
they need reading four different ways.

Add wartime and a fifth thing appears: material produced or approved by the
state to change what civilians did. Recruiting posters, Victory Bond
appeals, films, and censored despatches are not failed journalism. They are
successful propaganda, and they are excellent evidence — of what the
government believed would work on its own population.

## Reading the paper, not the clipping

**Start with the masthead and the date.** Who owned this paper, in what
town, and what did it depend on for revenue?

**Notice placement.** A story on page one and the same story on page nine
are two different editorial judgements. Casualty lists moved around during
the First World War, and where they sat is itself a finding.

**Read what is beside it.** Advertisements tell you who the readers were
assumed to be, and often contradict the tone of the news column above them.

**Ask what is not there.** Wartime censorship, the ordinary limits of a
local paper's reach, and simple commercial caution all removed things. An
absence you can explain is worth more than a quotation you cannot.

> [!tip] Propaganda is a source about its audience
> A poster tells you almost nothing about the war and a great deal about
> who was being asked, what they were assumed to fear, and what the
> government thought they owed. The Archives of Ontario holds roughly 500
> First World War posters and publishes a free Grade 10 lesson built on
> them, *Good propaganda? Evaluating wartime posters, 1914–1918*, at
> [archives.gov.on.ca/for-educators/lesson-plans](https://www.archives.gov.on.ca/for-educators/lesson-plans/)
> (accessed August 2026). The posters themselves are on Wikimedia Commons
> under an open licence, so you can reproduce one in your own work.

## Check the idea

Nothing you answer here is sent to anyone. It is for you.

> [!quiz] A First World War recruiting poster asks a young man why his friends are at the front and he is not. What is the poster the best evidence of?
> - [ ] How the fighting at the front was going
> - [ ] What most Canadians thought about the war
> - [ ] How many men enlisted after seeing it
> - [x] What the people who made it believed would move young men to enlist
>
> > [!explain]- Why
> > A poster is made to change what people do, so it is evidence about
> > its makers and the audience they imagined: here, that shame in front
> > of friends would work on a young man. It cannot tell you what most
> > people thought, or whether it worked. Enlistment records and letters
> > are the sources for those questions.

## Where you can actually read Canadian papers

Free and full-text searchable: Peel's Prairie Provinces, the University of
Alberta's collection of Prairie newspapers, browsable at
[library.ualberta.ca/peel/newspapers](https://library.ualberta.ca/peel/newspapers)
with the full text served from the Internet Archive (accessed August 2026).
It is the most reliable open option for the Depression years.

Be warned about the two papers you probably want. The *Toronto Star* and
*Globe and Mail* historical archives are licensed to public libraries and
need a library card; they are not open to a class. Check what your own
board and public library already license before you plan around them.

Then test whatever you find with [[Historical Perspective]] and put it to
work in [[Was Conscription Necessary|Was Conscription Necessary?]].
