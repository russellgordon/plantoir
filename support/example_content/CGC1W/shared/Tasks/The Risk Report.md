**Launched at the end of Unit 1, due early in Unit 2.** Individual.
About 700 words plus a map or diagram.

A community in Canada, a physical process that threatens it, and an
honest account of what can be done.

## Choose a real pairing

Flooding on a river town. Wildfire at the edge of a forest. Coastal
erosion or storm surge. Ice storms. Drought on farmland. Permafrost
thaw under northern infrastructure. Choose a community you can find
evidence about, including at least one source written by someone who
lives there.

## What it must contain

1. **The hazard**, described physically — what the process actually does.
2. **The exposure** — who and what is in its way, with a map.
3. **The risk**, which is not the same as the hazard: how likely, how
   severe, how often, and who carries the most of it.
4. **What has changed**, including any effect of a changing climate on
   frequency or severity — and say how confident the evidence is.
5. **Responses**, at least two, assessed rather than listed: what each
   would cost, who pays, and what it does not solve.

## How it is marked

| Criterion | What earns the marks |
| --- | --- |
| Evidence | Sources that are current, named, and diverse — see [[Judging a Source]] |
| Reasoning | Hazard, exposure, and risk kept distinct |
| Fairness | You notice who is most exposed and least able to move |
| Honesty | Uncertainty stated rather than smoothed over |

> [!warning] Do not make the community a victim in your writing
> People who live with a hazard usually know it best and have already
> done something about it. Find out what, and say so — a report that
> arrives with solutions and no idea what has been tried is the kind
> nobody acts on.

[[Hazard, Exposure, Risk]] gives you the vocabulary;
[[A Changing Climate]] is where the fourth section comes from.

TALK — Unit 1, Day 19, in the working period after the seminar
  Ask: "If this happened tomorrow, what would you want to know within
  the first hour — and can you find it in your own report?"
  A strong answer names something operational: which road closes, how
  far up the street the water reaches, who is supposed to call whom.
  Then it says honestly whether the report answers it. Follow it with
  "and which of the responses you assessed would have changed that
  hour?" — the pair is B2.3 whole: the risk analysed and the ways of
  responding assessed, tested by use rather than by rubric. A report can
  score well on every criterion and be no use to anybody in it.
  Then: "Whose account is missing from your sources, and where would
  you have to go to get it?"
  A strong answer names a group and a route to them — a band council, a
  conservation authority, a local paper's archive. That is A1.2's
  requirement that sources reflect multiple and diverse perspectives,
  heard from the other side: the report shows the voices present, never
  the ones absent.
  Record: two words per student — the operational fact, and the missing
  voice.
