Nobody in this course is asked to guess what a good report looks like.
The criteria are published on the day each task launches, in the words a
marker will use. What happens instead is that people read them twice —
once at launch, once when the mark comes back — and never in the fifteen
minutes when reading them would have changed something.

That fifteen minutes is what this page is.

## Three questions, in this order

**One: which rows can I put a finger on?** Go along the criteria table
and, for each row, point at the thing in your draft that satisfies it —
the paragraph, the axis, the labelled component, the sentence. Write
**yes**, **partly** or **not yet** beside each one. A "yes" you cannot
point at is not a judgement, it is a mood.

**Two: which row is weakest?** Not the fastest to repair. The weakest.
This is the question the whole exercise exists for, and it is the one
people skip, because answering it means writing down the thing you were
hoping would go unnoticed.

**Three: what changes, and in which period?** One sentence, naming the
change and the period you will make it in. If there is no period left,
you have run the check too late — it belongs where there is still
working time after it, which is why the schedule puts it inside a
working period rather than on a hand-in day.

Questions one and three take a few minutes each. Question two is the
work.

> [!example]- What an honest "partly" reads like
> The row, from [[Investigation Reports]]: *"What could have moved your
> number — how much the living material varied, which step could have
> misled you and in which direction, and one change that would reduce
> it."*
>
> **Partly.** There is a sentence about experimental error. It does not
> say that two of my six potato cylinders were soft before they went
> into the sucrose, which is almost certainly why the 0.4 mol/L point
> sits above the line, and it does not say that softness would make the
> mass gain look smaller than it was. Weakest row.
>
> **Change, this period:** name the two cylinders, mark that point on
> the graph, say which direction the error pushes it, and add the
> improvement — cut all six from one potato instead of three.
>
> Six lines. It is worth more than a tick against all six rows, and it
> took less time than deciding whether to write it.

## None of this is your mark

Your judgement of your own work does not move your percentage, and
neither does a classmate's. [[How Marks Work]] sets out why: the mark is
determined from evidence, by me, and that is policy rather than
politeness.

The useful consequence is that an honest **not yet** is free. It cannot
be held against you, it cannot be quoted back at you, and it is the only
kind of entry that leads anywhere. A generous self-check protects a draft
for about a day and then costs you on the one that counts.

## Keep them, and read them sideways

Copy each judgement into your [[Biology Journal]], dated, with the task
it belongs to. One of them tells you about one draft. Five of them
together tell you something no single mark can: whether the row you lose
is the same row every time.

If it is — if it is the analysis row on every write-up, or the mechanism
row on every report — then you have found the one thing worth working on
this semester, and you found it yourself, in fifteen-minute pieces.
