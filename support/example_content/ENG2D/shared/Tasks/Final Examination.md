> [!abstract] At a glance
> Individual · two and a half hours in the examination period · closed
> book, with the texts' titles and characters provided · all four strands
> · the whole of the final thirty per cent

## What is on it

| Part | Roughly | What it asks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A. Sight passage | 30% | A short text you have never seen: comprehension, inference, and one paragraph on how it works |
| B. Short answers on the studied texts | 25% | Precise recall and interpretation of *Romeo and Juliet*, the novel, and the short stories |
| C. Media question | 15% | An unseen media text, read with the five questions |
| D. Essay | 30% | One argument about a studied text, planned and written in the room |

## What it rewards

- **Evidence you actually remember.** Not quotations word-perfect —
  moments, and what happens in them. Two passages per text, known well,
  is worth more than a hundred half-remembered lines.
- **Answering the question asked.** Underline the verb: *explain*,
  *compare*, *evaluate*. Each asks for a different shape.
- **Planning the essay for five minutes** before writing. Every year, the
  strongest essays in this room are the ones with a visible plan in the
  margin.
- **Reading the sight passage twice** before answering anything.

## How to prepare

1. **Two passages per text**, chosen now, known cold. Know what happens,
   who is there, and what it shows.
2. **Redo the analytical paragraphs** you wrote in the autumn — not
   reread, redo, from the passage.
3. **Practise the sight passage under time**, twice: once in the review
   period and once at home.
4. **Rebuild your essay outline template** so that it is automatic.
5. **Bring your questions to the review classes.** There are four of
   them, and this page is what is on the paper.

> [!tip] In the room
> Budget by the weightings above and wear a watch. Do the essay while you
> are fresh if you know your argument; do the sight passage first if you
> do not. Answer every question — a partial answer earns partial marks
> and a blank earns none.

## How this is assessed

Against the same expectations as everything else, in the same four
categories. Per [[How Marks Work]] this paper is the whole of the final
thirty per cent, which is why it reaches across all four units at once
instead of testing the last one: a text you have never seen, the studied
texts from memory, an unseen media text, and an essay built in the room.

**What it rewards**, above, is the criteria list, and it is published now
rather than on the day — so that the four review classes can be spent on
whichever part of it you already know is weak.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

Nothing can be gathered from the examination itself, and it is worth
saying why rather than pretending otherwise. You may not be the person
invigilating; nobody speaks once the papers are out; and the marking
happens when the course is already finished, so a note made in that room
would have nowhere to go and nobody left to reach.

Both slots below sit in Unit 4's preparation classes instead. Neither is
evidence for the thirty per cent — they are evidence for the seventy,
gathered while the examination happens to be the reason everybody is
working.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 19, the ten minutes after the room picks the best
of the three plans
  This is not the planning; it is what happens to a student's own plan
  once a better one has been read out. It is the only moment in the term
  when everybody in the room is holding their own work beside a visibly
  better version of it, and the examination essay shows you the plan that
  got used and nothing whatever about where it came from.
  Watch for: pens on their own paper while the chosen plan is discussed.
  An arrow moved, a claim struck out, a passage written into a margin
  that had none — that student is taking something away. A student who
  watches, nods and writes nothing is not.
  Going well: somebody who argues that their own order would have worked
  too and can say why; somebody who takes one move rather than copying
  the whole plan.
  Stuck: three plans face down on the desk before the discussion starts.
  Record: stand at the back. The names you can see writing are one list;
  everybody else is the list that matters. Thirty seconds, no paper.
  Evidence for C1.2: generating and focusing ideas for a piece of writing
  using strategies — here, the strategy of taking one off somebody else,
  which is how most fifteen-year-olds acquire a new one and the only
  place the acquiring is ever visible.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 20, the open clinic, while the rest of the room reads
  That agenda has you coming round the room, and the students who bring
  nothing to a clinic are exactly the ones this is for.
  Nothing from this page's "how to prepare" list — they have read it, and
  its first item is about the passages they chose in class on Day 17 and
  annotated that night.
  Ask: "Books shut. In the novel, find me somebody who is in the room
  when something goes wrong and is not the person it goes wrong for. What
  do they do — and what does the book get out of showing us them instead
  of only the person it happens to?"
  Then: "Now the play, and make it a scene nobody in this class staged."
  The first half of each answer is recall; the second half is the one
  that matters. A strong answer places the moment roughly, names an
  action or a silence rather than a feeling, and then says what the
  bystander is doing to the book — which is B1.3 under exactly Part B's
  conditions: the most important ideas, and the supporting details they
  rest on, produced with nothing in front of them. A student who can only
  reach the scene their own group staged in Unit 2 has one text out of
  three and does not know it yet.
  Record: class list, two columns, a text name in each — the one they
  reached for and the one they could not. The second column tells you
  which text to give most of Day 21 or Day 22 to.

The only product is the paper, marked alone, after everything else.
