You sorted the light sources in this room by how the light is actually made,
and the room disagreed about at least two of them. Almost everybody puts the
tubes overhead and the phone screen in the same pile, because both are flat,
white and cool to the touch, and almost nobody puts the window in a pile at
all. This page settles it.

## Where light comes from

Every source on that bench makes light in one of a small number of ways, and
the way it is made decides what comes out — which colours, how much heat,
and whether it keeps going after you switch it off.

| How it is made | What is happening | In this room |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Incandescence** | Something is hot enough to glow. Raise the temperature and the light shifts from red towards white | The filament in the ray box, the sun through the window, a stove element |
| **Electric discharge** | A current is driven through a gas, and the gas emits at its own particular set of colours | A neon sign, a sodium street lamp, the tube in an older overhead fitting |
| **Fluorescence** | A material absorbs invisible ultraviolet and re-emits it as visible light — **only while the ultraviolet is arriving** | The coating inside a fluorescent tube, a highlighter pen under a blacklight |
| **Phosphorescence** | The same absorption, but the material lets the energy back out slowly, so it **keeps glowing in the dark** | Glow-in-the-dark stars, some exit signs, watch hands |
| **Light-emitting diode** | A current crosses a junction inside a semiconductor and light comes out directly, with almost no heating on the way | The phone screen, the newer ceiling panels, the indicator on the projector |
| **Bioluminescence** | A chemical reaction inside a living thing releases its energy as light rather than as heat | Fireflies, some fungi, a great deal of what lives below 200 m of ocean |

The distinction that decided the room's argument is the fourth row against
the fifth: **fluorescence stops the instant the ultraviolet does, and
phosphorescence does not.** If it still glows when you cover it, it is
storing the energy and letting it out slowly.

> [!question]- Why does a filament lamp get hot and an LED not? (click to expand)
> Take the two at the same brightness, which is the fair comparison.
> Because heat is not a side effect of incandescence — it *is* the
> mechanism. A filament glows because it is hot, and the great majority of
> what a hot filament radiates is infrared you cannot see. An LED does not
> get light by being hot; it emits in a narrow band close to the colour you
> wanted, so far less of the energy leaves as invisible radiation. That is
> the whole of the efficiency argument, and it is a statement about
> mechanism rather than about quality.

## The spectrum, in order

Visible light is a narrow strip in the middle of a much wider range, and
every part of that range is the same kind of thing travelling at the same
speed — only the wavelength differs. In order, from the longest waves to the
shortest:

**radio · microwave · infrared · VISIBLE · ultraviolet · X-ray · gamma**

Inside the visible strip, the same ordering continues: red is the long end,
violet the short end, and the rest of the spectrum runs between them in the
order you see in a rainbow.

Two consequences worth carrying into [[The Optics Design]]:

- **The invisible neighbours arrive whether you planned for them or not.**
  Anything you point at the sun is handling infrared and ultraviolet as well
  as light. A lens that concentrates the visible concentrates the infrared
  too, which is why a converging lens in sunlight is a fire risk and not a
  metaphor.
- **A camera sensor does not see what your eye sees.** Most of them respond
  into the near infrared, which is why a remote control looks dead to you and
  lit up on a phone camera.

## Why an instrument uses the lens it uses

The last part of the period was the eye, a phone camera, the projector and
somebody's glasses — four instruments, all doing the same physics, all
choosing different pieces of glass. The choice is never arbitrary.

- **A short focal length bends light hard over a short distance**, so it
  suits anything that has to be small: a phone camera, the eye itself, the
  objective of a microscope.
- **A long focal length bends gently and needs room**, which is what a
  projector has and a phone does not.
- **A converging lens can make a real image you can catch on a screen or a
  sensor**; a diverging lens cannot, which is why it appears in glasses that
  correct short sight rather than in a projector.
- **A curved mirror does the same jobs without the light ever passing
  through glass**, which is why a large telescope uses one: a mirror can be
  supported from behind, and a lens that size would sag under its own weight
  and split the colours on the way through.

The rule underneath all four: **what an instrument needs the image to do
decides the shape of the glass, not the other way round.** Hold onto that
when you choose your own device, because the sentence "I picked this lens
because it was in the drawer" is the one thing that cannot be defended.
