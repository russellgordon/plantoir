> [!abstract] At a glance
> **Format:** four pages with original diagrams. **Individually.**
> **Due:** handed in on Unit 4, Day 13. **Assessed on:** Knowledge,
> Thinking, Communication.

Take a disorder in which a feedback loop fails, and explain the failure at
every level: molecule, cell, system, person.

## Choose one

Type 1 or type 2 diabetes · hyperthyroidism or hypothyroidism · diabetes
insipidus · Addison's disease · multiple sclerosis · chronic kidney
disease.

## What to produce

1. **The intact loop**, diagrammed: stimulus, receptor, control centre,
   effector, response.
2. **The break**, located precisely — which component fails, and how.
3. **The consequences**, followed through at least two systems.
4. **Treatment**, named — the drug, hormone, or procedure — and which
   part of the loop it substitutes for.
5. **Research evidence and documentation**: Primary clinical or physiological
   sources cited in accepted academic format for the disorder's molecular
   mechanism and treatment efficacy.
6. **What it cannot fix**, and why.

7. **A working model you built.** Construct something physical or
   computational that shows the loop behaving — a thermostat rig, a
   water-bath analogue, a spreadsheet or short program that oscillates
   around a set point and overshoots when the delay is lengthened.
   Demonstrate it, and say which part of your model is the receptor,
   which the control centre, and which the effector. Name the real
   components properly throughout, using the words the biology uses:
   insulin, glucagon, ADH, the pituitary, the nephron, the synapse,
   dialysis where it applies, negative feedback, set point.

Point 6 is where the strongest reports separate themselves: insulin
replaces the effector's output but not its regulation, and saying exactly
that is a Grade 12 answer.

## How this is assessed

Published on launch day, Unit 4, Day 8. Run them against your draft with
[[Judging Your Own Work]] on Unit 4, Day 12.

| Quality | What it looks like in your report |
| --- | --- |
| The loop is drawn, not described | Stimulus, receptor, control centre, effector and response on a diagram you made, each one a named structure or molecule rather than a box labelled with its job |
| The break is located, not gestured at | One component named, and how it fails — a receptor that no longer binds is a different report from a gland that no longer secretes |
| The consequences travel | Followed through at least two body systems, in the order they actually happen, with the intermediate step shown rather than skipped |
| Treatment mapped onto the loop | Which component the treatment stands in for, and what it therefore cannot restore |
| Documented from research | Primary clinical or physiological sources cited in accepted academic format, distinguishing established mechanisms from clinical trials |
| The model behaves | Your rig, bath or program oscillates around a set point, its three components can be pointed at, and it does something different when you change one thing about it |
| The words are the right words | Insulin, glucagon, ADH, the pituitary, the nephron, the synapse, dialysis, negative feedback, set point, receptor, effector — the ones your disorder touches, used where they belong and not as decoration |
| A reader could follow it cold | Four pages that somebody who has not studied your disorder can read start to finish without going elsewhere for a definition |

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 10, the period where the loops are diagrammed
  Watch for: which end they start from. A loop drawn first, with the
  disorder's failure then placed on it, or a list of symptoms copied out
  and a loop reverse-engineered to explain them. Both routes produce a
  correct diagram by Day 13, and the finished report cannot tell you which
  one was taken — but only the first survives being asked about a disorder
  the student did not choose.
  Going well: the intact loop on paper before the disorder has been looked
  up at all, and the break argued about afterwards.
  Stuck: five symptoms in a list and no loop yet, half an hour in.
  Record: three columns on the day plan — loop first, symptoms first, not
  started. One walk around the room, and take the same three columns into
  the Day 14 retrieval clinic to see whether the pattern holds.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 11, at the demonstrations, once a model is running
  The agenda already has them demonstrating and naming the three
  components, so do not ask for that again; stand at the running model and
  ask what it is not.
  Ask: "Break it for me. Change one thing so that it does something no
  real body would ever do — and tell me what you changed."
  Then: "Now which part of what I am watching is a convenience you built
  because a bath cannot do what a body does?"
  A strong answer breaks the model at a component rather than by typing a
  silly number, and can then name something specific the model leaves out
  — that the delay is fixed and a body's is not, that one hormone is
  standing in for two, that there is no second loop — and say which of
  their report's claims that limits. That is E2.2 heard: a model can
  illustrate the essential components without its builder ever having
  worked out which components were inessential, and a working
  demonstration looks identical either way.
  Record: two columns — broke it at a component, or could not break it on
  purpose.

The product evidence is the report, handed in on Unit 4, Day 13.
