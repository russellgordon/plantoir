Prokaryotes have no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles. They are
also the most numerous, most metabolically varied, and most consequential
organisms on the planet.

## Structure

A cell wall, a plasma membrane, a single circular chromosome, ribosomes,
and often flagella and plasmids. That is nearly the whole list, and it
runs everything from nitrogen fixation to yoghurt.

## Reproduction and variation

Binary fission gives identical daughter cells — but three mechanisms
introduce variation anyway: transformation (taking up loose DNA),
transduction (viral transfer), and conjugation (direct transfer via a
pilus). Those mechanisms are why antibiotic resistance spreads between
species, not just within them.

## How the rest of life came out of this

Every organism in the other four kingdoms is built from cells that
contain something this page has not mentioned: organelles with their own
membranes. The best-supported account of where those came from is
**endosymbiosis** — one prokaryote engulfed another and, instead of
digesting it, kept it.

The evidence is unusually direct, and it is worth knowing rather than
taking on trust. Mitochondria and chloroplasts have **their own circular
DNA**, of the kind described above. They have **their own ribosomes**,
and those ribosomes are the prokaryotic size rather than the eukaryotic
one. They have **two membranes**, which is what being swallowed leaves
behind. And they **divide by fission** on their own schedule, not by
being manufactured when the cell divides. Each of those is a leftover
from the free-living organism the ancestor was.

That single event — an archaeal-type host acquiring a bacterium that
could respire oxygen — is what made a large, energy-hungry, complex cell
possible at all, and a later one involving a photosynthetic bacterium is
where every chloroplast in [[Photosynthesis]] came from. Multicellularity,
tissues, and everything in [[Animal Systems Overview]] are downstream of
it.

**The shape of the change is the part to carry forward.** The step from
prokaryote to eukaryote was not a gradual improvement of one lineage. It
was one lineage acquiring the machinery of another and keeping it, which
is a structural change with functional consequences that no amount of
slow refinement would have produced. The same pattern — a change in
structure that unlocks a new function, rather than a better version of
the old one — is what to look for in the move from single cells to
colonies, from water to land in [[Plant Structure]], and from gills to
lungs. Evolution is often described as accumulation; the interesting
episodes are usually acquisitions and rearrangements.

## Archaea are not "primitive bacteria"

They differ from bacteria in membrane chemistry and in their genetic
machinery, which is closer to ours. Many live in extreme conditions;
plenty do not. The 1977 discovery that they are a separate domain is one
of the most significant reorganisations in the history of biology.
