**Due: the last week of Unit 2.** Individual. A visual piece plus about
400 words.

One ordinary object you own, traced from raw material to disposal.

## Choose something small and specific

A phone, a pair of jeans, a hockey stick, a can of tomatoes, a bicycle
tire, a laptop charger. Something you can hold. The more ordinary it is,
the better this works.

## What you produce

**A journey map or diagram** — not a poster of pictures, a map that
shows movement: where the materials came from, where it was made, how it
reached you, and where it goes when you are done with it. Distances
matter, so put them on.

**The writing** answers four questions:

1. Which stage of this journey has the largest environmental effect, and
   how do you know?
2. What in the journey surprised you?
3. What would have to change for the journey to be shorter — and what
   would be lost if it were?
4. Whose work is in this object, and under what conditions?
5. Where is Canada in this journey — raw material, manufacture,
   transport, sale, or disposal? Name the stage or stages and what this
   country does there. If Canada is in none of them, say what that tells
   you about what we make and what we buy.

## How it is marked

| Criterion | What earns the marks |
| --- | --- |
| Research | Real supply-chain evidence, cited, not a guess drawn as a diagram |
| The visual | Readable, accurate, and it carries the argument |
| Communication | Clear for an audience who has not studied this — see [[Citing Geographic Sources]] |
| Canada's place | The stage where this country enters the journey is named, with what it does there — or its absence is explained rather than skipped |
| The shorter route | Your answer to question 3 names a real change to how the object moves, and what that change would cost somebody |
| Honesty | You say which parts of the journey you could not find out |

> [!tip] The gaps are part of the finding
> Most supply chains are hard to trace past the second step, and that
> difficulty is itself worth writing about: a company that cannot tell
> you where its cotton was grown has told you something.

[[The Life of a Product]] sets this up, and [[Moving People and Goods]]
is where the transport half of your map comes from.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 2, Day 19, the mapping clinic on showing movement
  Watch how the distances get onto the map. The task asks for them, so
  numbers will appear; what it cannot show you is whether they were
  measured on a base with a scale, or estimated off the shape of a
  world map somebody half remembers. Both print the same way and both
  look equally sure of themselves.
  Going well: a measuring tool open, and a note of which route was
  measured — sea, road, or straight line.
  Stuck: a line drawn corner to corner across a Mercator with a round
  number written beside it and no way back to where it came from.
  Record: seating plan, tick, plus the tool if they used one.

TALK — Unit 2, Day 20, while the peer review of journey maps is running
and you are free to move
  Ask: "This map has to be read by somebody who has never thought about
  where their clothes come from. What is the one thing on it you would
  take off to make it land — and what do you lose by taking it off?"
  A strong answer trades something real away and knows the cost: the
  disposal leg, the distances, the second supplier. That is A1.6,
  choosing a format and a style for a particular audience, which the
  finished sheet shows the result of and never the reasoning behind.
  Then: "Which country on this journey could stop trading with Canada
  tomorrow and change what this object costs — and which industry here
  would feel it first?"
  A strong answer names a country, a mechanism and a Canadian industry
  at the other end of it. That is C1.6's interconnections between
  Canadian industries and those of other countries, which question 5 on
  the page asks for in one line and this asks them to defend.
  Record: class list, two columns — what they would cut, and the country
  they named.

The product evidence is the journey handed in on Day 21.
