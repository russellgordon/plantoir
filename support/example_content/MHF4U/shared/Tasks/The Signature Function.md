## What you are making

Every unit this semester ended with someone else's phenomenon —
coasters, doses, tones. This time the phenomenon is yours: anything
you genuinely care about that changes. A sport statistic, a growing
channel, a cooling coffee, a tide, a market, a migration. You finish
with the **function that models it**, built from real numbers you
sourced and cited; a **defence of the family** you chose —
polynomial, rational, sinusoidal, exponential, logarithmic, or a
combination — against at least one rival family; its **rates of
change** — one average rate over an interval that matters and one
instantaneous rate at a moment that matters, both interpreted in the
phenomenon's own units; and an honest statement of **where the model
stops being true**.

If you work in a pair, the mark is still yours alone: the sections you
wrote and initialled, your own milestone journal entry, what you say at
the conference, and the part of the defence you give at the symposium.

## Milestones

- [ ] Phenomenon chosen; data sourced, cited, and plotted
- [ ] Family chosen and defended against a rival, per
      [[What Makes a Model Good]]
- [ ] Model fitted in [[Using Desmos]]; the misfit measured and
      stated in the phenomenon's units
- [ ] Both rates of change computed and interpreted, per
      [[Rates of Change]] — the average one over an interval you can
      say why you chose, the instantaneous one estimated by shrinking
      intervals until the answer stops moving in the digit that matters
- [ ] The model's domain of honesty stated — where it breaks, and why
- [ ] Defence rehearsed for [[The Functions Symposium]]

## How it is assessed

This is the culminating task, and per [[How Marks Work]] it is
assessed on reasoning, growth, and communication: the argument in
your write-up is where the evidence lives, not the polish of the
poster. The defence itself is judged separately, at
[[The Functions Symposium]]. Your [[Math Journal]] across the whole
semester is the growth record — the entry on why you chose this
phenomenon, written at launch, gets its answer in your
[[Final Reflection]]. Ten minutes of the last working period are set
aside for your milestone journal entry on the rival family you
rejected and what finally decided it; that entry belongs to this
task.

The same period opens with [[Judging Your Own Work]], run against the
table below, so that what you find has the rest of that period to be
mended in.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like in your work |
| --- | --- |
| A real stake | A phenomenon you can explain caring about |
| An argued family | The rival model taken seriously, then beaten |
| A measured misfit | Model-versus-data disagreement stated in units |
| Rates that mean | Both rates interpreted in the phenomenon's units |
| Honest edges | The model's breaking point named, not hidden |
| An entry that names the rival | The milestone entry says which family you rejected and what decided it |

> [!success]- If no phenomenon feels right
> Pick the one you would talk about anyway. The mathematics can be
> made interesting; your interest cannot be faked, and the symposium
> guests can tell the difference.

Triangulation — the evidence that never arrives on its own.

Mostly solo, so this is cheaper to gather here than on any other task in the
course. Day 15 carries the conference; the questions below are deliberately
not the one printed on that agenda.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 15, the first half of the working period

  Watch for the moment a data point is RESERVED. The agenda asks for a model
  tested against a point that was not used to build it, and the write-up
  will show a held-back point either way — what it cannot show is whether
  the point was set aside before the fitting started, or chosen afterwards
  from the ones that happened to agree. That is the whole difference between
  a test and a demonstration, and it is gone by hand-in.

  Going well: a row of the table circled and struck out, on paper, before
  the graphing window is open. Better still, a student who reserves the
  point at the far end of the data rather than one in the comfortable middle.

  Stuck: the curve is fitted, the table is being scanned, and the phrase is
  "which one should I use?"

  Record: a tick against a name the moment you see a point reserved. The
  names with no tick by half-time are the conferences to reach first, and
  the question to open with is not the one below — it is "show me your
  table".

TALK — Unit 4, Day 15, at the conference already on that agenda

  Ask: "Somebody at the next desk says everything is really exponential
  underneath if you watch it long enough. Argue with them about yours."

  Then: "Your instantaneous rate came out of shrinking an interval. How
  small did you go before you stopped, and how did you know that was small
  enough?"

  The first is a disagreement rather than a report, and disagreements pull
  out reasoning that a write-up flattens. A strong answer says what the data
  would have to DO for the exponential claim to be right — no ceiling, a
  constant ratio between equal steps, no turning back — and then says what
  their own numbers actually do instead. A weak one repeats the family's name
  and its shape. That is D3.1 heard: comparing the characteristics of
  candidate families rather than announcing the winner.

  Then listen, on the second, for a stopping rule rather than a number. The
  strong answer is that the successive average rates stopped changing in the
  digit the decision cares about — a stability argument they can show you on
  their own list of secant slopes. "I used 0.001 because that is small"
  is not it, and neither is "the calculator said so". That is D1.9 in a form
  no write-up carries, because the write-up prints only the final rate.

  Record: one line per student on your class list, written at the desk.

The product evidence is the model and write-up finished on Day 16, and the
milestone entry written in class that day. The defence belongs to
The Functions Symposium and is judged there.
