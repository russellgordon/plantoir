Classification is not vocabulary. Naming a reaction correctly means you
can say what happened to the substances, and that is what lets you
predict the products of one you have never seen.

**1.** Classify each reaction, and say in words what happened to the
substances — not just which name it gets.

> (a) $\ce{2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO}$
> (b) $\ce{2H2O2 -> 2H2O + O2}$
> (c) $\ce{Zn + 2HCl -> ZnCl2 + H2}$
> (d) $\ce{Na2CO3 + CaCl2 -> CaCO3 + 2NaCl}$

> [!success]- Answer 1
> **(a) Synthesis.** Two elements, each on its own, combine into a
> single compound. Two things in, one thing out.
>
> **(b) Decomposition.** One compound breaks apart into simpler
> substances. One thing in, two things out — the reverse move of (a).
>
> **(c) Single displacement.** An element that started alone — the zinc
> — has taken the place of the hydrogen in the compound, and the
> hydrogen is now the one on its own. One element swaps in, one swaps
> out.
>
> **(d) Double displacement.** Two compounds exchange partners. Sodium
> ends up with chloride and calcium ends up with carbonate, and at no
> point is any element on its own.
>
> The test that keeps you right: **look at what is alone**. Nothing
> alone on either side means double displacement. Something alone on
> both sides means single displacement. Alone only on the left means
> synthesis; alone only on the right means decomposition.

**2.** A zinc strip is placed in copper(II) sulfate solution. Predict
the products, write the balanced equation, and say what you would
expect to see.

> [!success]- Answer 2
> Zinc is an element on its own and copper(II) sulfate is a compound,
> so this is a **single displacement**. Zinc takes the place of the
> copper, and the copper is set free as an element. Sulfate travels
> through unchanged as a unit.
>
> $$\ce{Zn(s) + CuSO4(aq) -> ZnSO4(aq) + Cu(s)}$$
>
> Count: 1 Zn, 1 Cu, 1 sulfate group on each side — balanced as
> written.
>
> **What you would expect to see:** a reddish-brown solid building up
> on the zinc, and the blue of the solution fading, because
> $\ce{ZnSO4(aq)}$ is colourless. Note carefully that this is
> what you would *expect*, and that observing it does not by itself
> identify the solid as copper — as your group found in
> [[Reaction Types Lab]].

**3.** Write the balanced equation for hydrochloric acid reacting with
sodium hydroxide solution. Name the products, and state the general
pattern.

> [!success]- Answer 3
> $$\ce{HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) -> NaCl(aq) + H2O(l)}$$
>
> Products: **sodium chloride** and **water**. Counting: 1 Na, 1 Cl,
> 1 O, and 2 H on each side.
>
> The general pattern for neutralization is
>
> $$\text{acid} + \text{base} \rightarrow \text{salt} + \text{water}$$
>
> where "salt" means the ionic compound built from the metal of the
> base and the negative ion of the acid — not specifically table salt,
> although here it happens to be exactly that. Structurally this is a
> double displacement: the partners have swapped.

**4.** Balance:
$\ce{H2SO4(aq) + NaOH(aq) -> Na2SO4(aq) + H2O(l)}$,
and name the salt produced.

> [!success]- Answer 4
> The formula $\ce{Na2SO4}$ needs two sodium, and each
> $\ce{NaOH}$ brings only one, so two of them are required. That puts
> 2 extra hydrogen and 2 extra oxygen on the left, which together with
> the acid's 2 hydrogen makes 2 water molecules.
>
> $$\ce{H2SO4(aq) + 2NaOH(aq) -> Na2SO4(aq) + 2H2O(l)}$$
>
> Check: Na 2 and 2. Sulfate 1 and 1. H — two from the acid plus two
> from the hydroxides makes 4 on the left, and $2 \times 2 = 4$ on the
> right. O beyond the sulfate — 2 on the left, 2 on the right.
>
> The salt is **sodium sulfate**. An acid carrying two hydrogens needs
> twice as much of a base carrying one hydroxide, which is a fact worth
> having in your head before you meet it in a calculation.

**5.** Solution A has a pH of 3. Solution B has a pH of 5. How do their
acidities compare? Be quantitative.

> [!success]- Answer 5
> A is **more acidic**, and by more than most people guess. The pH
> scale is built so that each whole unit is a factor of ten in
> hydrogen ion concentration.
>
> Two units apart means $10 \times 10 = 100$.
>
> So solution A has roughly **one hundred times** the hydrogen ion
> concentration of solution B. "A bit more acidic" is a serious
> understatement, and this is why a pH strip that reads to the nearest
> whole unit is a coarse instrument even when it looks precise.

**6.** A student tests five liquids and records: lemon juice 2, black
coffee 5, distilled water 7, baking soda solution 9, soap solution 10.
Classify each, put them in order of hydrogen ion concentration, and
say how much more acidic the lemon juice is than the coffee.

> [!success]- Answer 6
> **Acidic** (below 7): lemon juice, black coffee. **Neutral**:
> distilled water. **Basic** (above 7): baking soda solution, soap
> solution.
>
> Hydrogen ion concentration runs **opposite** to pH, so highest first:
> lemon juice, coffee, distilled water, baking soda solution, soap
> solution.
>
> Lemon juice to coffee is 3 units of pH, so
> $10 \times 10 \times 10 = 1000$ — about a thousand times the
> hydrogen ion concentration.
>
> One caution about the data: classroom pH strips are typically read to
> around half a unit, and the colour match is a judgement made by eye.
> These readings support the ordering and the classification. They
> would not support a claim that two liquids reading 5 and 5 were
> identical.

**7.** A student writes: *"If you spill acid, pour a base on it. The
base neutralizes the acid and then it's safe."* Say what is right, what
is dangerously incomplete, and what you should actually do.

> [!success]- Answer 7
> **What is right:** an acid and a base really do neutralize each
> other, and the products are a salt and water, which are usually far
> less hazardous than what you started with. That chemistry is sound,
> and it is the basis of how spill kits work.
>
> **What is dangerously incomplete**, and there are three parts:
>
> 1. **Neutralization releases heat.** Pouring a base straight onto a
>    concentrated acid can heat the mixture enough to spatter it, which
>    turns a puddle on the bench into droplets in the air.
> 2. **Excess base is not safe.** Once the acid is used up, the extra
>    base is itself corrosive. "Neutralized" and "harmless" are not the
>    same word, and there is no way to tell by looking when you have
>    stopped and gone past.
> 3. **A spill is an incident, not a chore.** Deciding to handle it
>    yourself means nobody who knows the hazard has looked at it.
>
> **What you actually do:** move people away from it, tell me
> immediately, and let the spill kit and its procedure do the work.
> Small spills are handled with the neutralizing agent from the kit,
> applied by somebody who knows how much, or by dilution with plenty of
> water where that is the correct response for that chemical. You will
> never be in trouble for reporting a spill — see [[Safety in the Lab]].

**8.** A student writes: *"A pH of 7 means there are no hydrogen ions in
the solution — that's why it's neutral. And a concentrated acid and a
strong acid are two ways of saying the same thing."* Correct both
claims.

> [!success]- Answer 8
> **First claim.** A pH of 7 does not mean zero hydrogen ions. It means
> the hydrogen ion concentration sits at the particular value we call
> neutral, where it is balanced by an equal concentration of hydroxide
> ions. Pure water contains both, in small and equal amounts. If a
> pH of 7 meant none at all, the scale could not continue upward to 8,
> 9, and beyond — every step above 7 is a further *reduction* in
> hydrogen ion concentration, and you cannot reduce something that is
> already nothing.
>
> **Second claim.** These are two different ideas and mixing them up is
> a genuine safety problem.
>
> - **Concentrated** describes *how much acid there is in a given
>   amount of solution*. It is about dilution, and you change it by
>   adding water.
> - **Strong** describes *how completely the acid releases its hydrogen
>   ions in water*. It is a property of the substance itself, and no
>   amount of water changes it.
>
> So a dilute solution of a strong acid and a concentrated solution of
> a weak acid are both perfectly ordinary things, and knowing that one
> bottle says "dilute" tells you nothing about which acid is inside it.
> Read the label, every time — see [[Lab Safety and WHMIS]]. And when
> a solution does need diluting, **acid goes into water, never water
> into acid**, because the heat released has somewhere to go when the
> water is the thing already in the container.
