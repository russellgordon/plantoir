**Run across Unit 3; assessed at the end of it.** Individual, on a
shared bench.

Your own section of the propagation bench, planted, labelled, watered,
and kept alive.

## What you run

- **Seed**, sown at the correct depth and spacing for the species.
- **Cuttings**, taken and prepared correctly, with the right medium and
  humidity.
- **Transplanting**, at the right stage, without wrecking the roots.
- **Care**, twice weekly: water, light, air, and inspection.

## The record

A running log, kept as you go: dates, what you did, what you observed,
what changed. Germination percentages and strike rates calculated
honestly.

## What is marked

| Criterion | The standard |
| --- | --- |
| Technique | Sowing depth, cutting preparation, and medium are correct for the species |
| Care | Watering judged by the plant and the medium, not by the timetable |
| Observation | You notice a problem in week two, not week four |
| Record | Complete, dated, and honest about losses |
| Result | Living plants at the end, in a state someone would accept |

> [!success]- What "watered properly" actually means (click to expand)
> Not a schedule. Check the medium's weight and feel, the plant's turgor,
> the weather, and the stage of growth, then decide. Overwatering kills
> more propagation benches than drought does, because it is the mistake
> that feels like care — see [[Watering Properly]].

[[Propagation From Seed]], [[Propagation From Cuttings]], and
[[Transplanting and Establishment]] are the methods.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 3, Day 2, sticking cuttings
  A struck cutting and one that is quietly rotting look identical for
  about three weeks, and by the Day 19 inspection the strike rate tells
  you the outcome without telling you a thing about the technique that
  produced it. This period is where B1.2 is actually visible.
  Watch for: where the cut is made relative to the node; how much leaf
  is left on; whether the blade is wiped between species; and above all
  what happens to the cuttings that are prepared but not yet stuck — in
  the shade and damp, or lying on a dry bench while the holes are
  dibbled.
  Going well: a small batch prepared, stuck, and watered before the next
  batch is cut.
  Stuck: thirty cuttings prepared at once, wilting in a row.
  Record: initials in two columns on the bench plan — kept them damp,
  wiped the blade.

TALK — Unit 3, Day 18, during bench care, the period before the
inspection
  Not Day 6, tempting as it looks: root handling and timing ARE that
  period's lesson, so anything you hear at the transplanting bench is
  ten minutes old and belongs to you rather than to them. Not Day 8
  either — the production line is being set up in the front half of
  that period and you are watching it, and the bench walk on that
  agenda is the formative check, not this.
  Day 18 is the day before the inspection, and by then the losses have
  happened, which is what makes the question answerable.
  Ask: "Some of what you moved on Day 6 did not make it. Which ones,
  and did it go the way you would have guessed at the time?"
  Then: "Show me the one you decided not to move. What was it waiting
  for, and what told you it was ready — or that it never would be?"
  A strong answer puts a cause to a loss — a torn root, a medium too
  wet, no hardening off — rather than calling it bad luck, and defends
  the one held back by its stage rather than by a date on the calendar.
  That is A3.3, understanding the process, and B1.3, which names
  transplanting outright. Tomorrow's inspection counts what survived;
  only this tells you whether the student knows why.
  Record: two words per name on the bench plan for the cause they gave.
  Take it into the inspection with you.

The product evidence is the bench itself and the log, inspected Day 19.
