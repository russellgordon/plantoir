A thesis is a claim about how a text works that somebody informed could
dispute. Everything else in an essay exists to earn it.

## What a Grade 11 thesis has that a Grade 10 one may not

- **A mechanism.** Not just *what* the text does but *how*: the
  technique, the structure, the pattern.
- **A stake.** Why the reading matters to how we read the whole text.
- **A built-in complication.** The best theses already anticipate the
  strongest counter-evidence.

| Weaker | Stronger |
| --- | --- |
| *Hamlet* is about ambition | The play locates ambition in the marriage rather than in either spouse, which is why its collapse is grammatical: they stop finishing each other's lines |
| Lisamarie is grieving | *Monkey Beach* organises grief as a structural problem — the novel refuses chronological order because Lisamarie cannot narrate the loss straight through |

## Building one

1. **Start from your annotations**, not from a topic. Find a pattern.
2. **Ask what it does**, not what it means.
3. **Write the claim in one sentence** with a *because* or a *which* in
   it.
4. **Find the best counter-evidence** and write it down. If you cannot,
   the claim is probably a summary.
5. **Test it against the ending.**

## Structuring the argument

The order of your body paragraphs is itself an argument. Three
defensible orders: by strength, by chronology through the text, or by
technique. Say which you chose in the outline; an essay whose order is
accidental reads as accidental. See [[The Extended Essay]].
