> [!abstract] At a glance
> **Format:** a designed investigation run over three weeks, plus a full
> report. **Groups of three**, with the analysis and the research page
> written by you alone. **This is the culminating task**, and part of the
> final thirty per cent — see [[How Marks Work]].
> **Assessed on:** all four categories.

Design and run an investigation into something that affects plant growth.
You choose the question; I approve the procedure before anything is
planted.

## The rules

- **One manipulated variable.** Everything else controlled, and the
  controls listed.
- **At least three levels** of that variable, plus a control group.
- **At least five plants per group** — biology varies, and three is not
  enough to see past it.
- **A measurable response**: height, mass, leaf area, germination rate.
  Say how you will measure it before you start.

## Questions that have worked

Light colour and stem elongation · salt concentration and germination ·
crowding and final height · soil pH and leaf area · day length and
flowering.

## The report

Purpose, hypothesis with reasoning, method, observations (a table and a
graph), analysis, conclusion, and a section on what you would change —
see [[Writing a Lab Report]].

## Why this plant matters

One page, researched and sourced, alongside the experiment:

- **In Canada.** What role does this plant — or its crop family — play in
  Canadian agriculture, food supply, or economy? Real figures, from
  Statistics Canada, Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada, or a producers'
  association.
- **In a culture.** How has a society or culture used this plant, or one
  like it — as food, medicine, material, or in ceremony? If you write
  about an Indigenous use, use sources from that nation or community
  wherever you can, name them, and present the knowledge as theirs.
- **What your experiment implies.** If your variable matters to growth,
  what would that mean for somebody growing it at scale?

[[Plants and Agriculture]] is the starting point and not the finish;
[[Finding and Judging Sources]] is how you judge what you find.

## What your group does, and what you do alone

The question, the design, the plants and the raw measurements are your
group's, and one set of them is handed in. **Written by you alone:** the
analysis, the conclusion, the section on what you would change, and the
"why this plant matters" page. Put your name on each. Three people can
share a growth table; a conclusion drawn from it is one person's
reasoning, and this is the culminating task, so it has to be yours.

## Why three weeks

Because that is how long the biology takes. Nothing here is marked for
being late — lateness is a conversation, and it belongs on the other half
of the report card. What starting late actually costs you is data, and
there is no version of this task that works without three weeks of
growth. The plants do not care about your other deadlines.

## Success criteria

Published here on launch day. Read them again with
[[Judging Your Own Work]] on Day 12, while there is still a working
period left in which to act on what you find.

| Quality | What it looks like in your investigation |
| --- | --- |
| A question with a reason behind it | The hypothesis says what you expect AND what biology would make it happen |
| One variable moved | Three levels plus a control, five plants each, and every controlled variable listed |
| A measurement that stayed the same | The protocol says where and when and by whom, and week three is measured the way week one was |
| Variation treated as data | The spread within a group is reported, and the conclusion does not claim a difference smaller than it |
| Numbers stated to the precision you had | Decimal places match the instrument, not the calculator |
| The graph the comparison needs | Chosen before the data existed, axes labelled with units |
| A conclusion the data allows | Including, where that is the honest answer, "nothing I can claim from this" |
| The plant placed in the world | The Canadian figures are real and sourced; the cultural use names the nation or community it belongs to |
| Terminology exact | Stomata, epidermal tissue, germination, propagation — used precisely and not swapped |

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 5, Day 10, the ten minutes of measuring at the start
  Watch for: whether the measurement is being made the way it was made on
  Day 7 — the same point on the stem, the same time relative to watering,
  the same person holding the ruler. Three weeks of quietly drifting
  technique produces a clean-looking graph that means nothing, and the
  finished report cannot show you the drift.
  Going well: a written measurement protocol on the bench, being
  consulted rather than remembered.
  Stuck: "how did you do it last time?"
  Record: one tick per group. The groups with no protocol are the ones to
  reach first at the checkpoint on Day 12 — there is still time to save
  the data.

TALK — Unit 5, Day 6, at the design conference the agenda already has
  Measurement and precision are what the agenda promised this conference
  would cover, and they will have that ready. Settle it in thirty seconds
  and spend the rest here.
  Ask: "Why this question rather than one of the ones on the list — what
  do you actually expect to happen?"
  Then: "And what would make biology behave that way? What would surprise
  you?"
  A strong answer has a mechanism underneath the prediction rather than a
  hunch: light quality changes what the pigments absorb, salt changes
  what the seed can take up. That is A1.1 — an educated hypothesis rather
  than a guess — and the written report will contain a tidied-up
  hypothesis whether or not there was ever a reason behind it. In a group
  of three this is also the two minutes in which you find out who owns
  the question.
  Record: three columns — mechanism, hunch, copied. Revisit the hunches
  on Day 12.

The product evidence is the report handed in on Day 14. The presentations
that day are not evidence-gathering time: you are running the room.
