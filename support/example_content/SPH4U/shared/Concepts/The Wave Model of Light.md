Light travels in straight lines, reflects, and refracts — all of which a
particle model explains perfectly well. The wave model earns its place by
explaining what a particle model cannot: interference and diffraction.

## The argument in one experiment

Two narrow slits, one light source, and a screen showing BANDS. Particles
would give two bright strips. Waves give a pattern of light and dark,
because two waves arriving out of step cancel. Nothing that travels as
particles alone does that — see [[Young's Double-Slit Experiment]].

## Wavelength sets the scale

$$v = f\lambda \qquad \text{where } c = 3.00 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s}$$

Visible light runs from about 400 nm (violet) to 700 nm (red). Diffraction
is only noticeable when the obstacle or opening is comparable to the
wavelength — which is why light seems to travel in straight lines in
everyday life and sound bends round corners.

## Wave generation: oscillating dipoles

Electromagnetic radiation is generated whenever an electric charge undergoes
acceleration. An oscillating electric dipole — whether an alternating current
in a transmitting antenna, an oscillating electron in a microwave cavity, or an
atomic electron transitioning between quantum states — radiates synchronized,
transverse electric and magnetic wave fronts that propagate through the vacuum
at speed $c$. See [[Electromagnetic Radiation]].

## The honest caveat

In Unit 5 this model breaks too: the photoelectric effect refuses to fit a
wave picture. Light is not "really a wave" — it is something for which
both models are limited descriptions. That is the punchline of the course
and it is worth waiting for.
