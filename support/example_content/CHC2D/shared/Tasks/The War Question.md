**Due: the end of Unit 1.** Individual. An argument of about 900 words.

One question about Canada in the First World War, answered with
evidence.

## The questions on offer

- Was conscription necessary in 1917, or was it a political choice with
  other options available?
- Did the war make Canada more independent, or more tied to Britain?
- Vimy Ridge is often called the moment Canada became a nation. Is that
  history or commemoration?
- Who bore the cost of this war at home, and was that distribution
  defensible?

You may propose your own, agreed with me first.

## What the argument must do

1. **State a claim** that someone could disagree with.
2. **Support it** with at least four pieces of evidence, at least two of
   them primary.
3. **Take the strongest objection seriously** and answer it — not a weak
   version of it.
4. **Say what would change your mind.**
5. **Use the concepts.** Whichever question you take, name where you are
   arguing about significance, cause and consequence, continuity and
   change, or perspective.

## How it is marked

| Criterion | What earns the marks |
| --- | --- |
| Claim | Arguable, specific, and answered |
| Evidence | Relevant, cited, and weighed rather than listed |
| Counter-argument | The strongest version, genuinely engaged |
| Concepts | Historical thinking used to reason, not named as decoration |
| Writing | Clear structure; the reader always knows where they are |

> [!tip] The counter-argument paragraph is where the marks are
> Almost every strong essay in this course has one paragraph that
> concedes something real and then explains why the claim survives
> anyway. See [[Building an Argument]].

[[Conscription and the Country It Split]] and
[[Canada Goes to War]] are the ground; the seminar
[[Was It Worth It|Was It Worth It?]] is where you will have tested your instincts.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 1, Day 16, while the class reads the state's response to the
1919 strike wave
  Most of the room is writing about something else — Vimy, independence,
  who paid for the war — so nobody is performing for their own essay here.
  That is what makes the period worth watching.
  Watch for: who treats the state's account of the strike as a source with
  a purpose, and who treats it as the record of what happened.
  Going well: "who wrote this, and what were they defending?" comes from a
  student rather than from you; the official figure gets asked about before
  it gets used.
  Stuck: the government's number repeated as the number.
  Record: two columns on your day plan — read it as evidence, read it as
  fact. One pass while they read.
  That is B2.3 — the causes and consequences of political conflict in this
  period, the Winnipeg General Strike among the Ministry's own examples —
  reasoned in public. An essay on Vimy will never show it to you.

TALK — Unit 1, Day 14, the checkpoint conference on that agenda
  Ask: "Who, at the time, would have said you were wrong? Name a person or
  an organization, not a position."
  Then: "What did they have in front of them that you have not read yet?"
  A strong answer produces a name and a record — Bourassa and his paper,
  the Trades and Labour Congress, a farm organization's petition, a
  recruiting sergeant's returns — and treats the gap as work still to do. A
  weak one produces "some people would disagree". This is B2.3 again, heard
  three classes before the essay is due rather than after it, which is the
  only point at which it can still change what a student reads.
  Record: one line each on your class list — named a source, named a
  position, neither. That list is who to find during Day 15's work period.

SEMINAR — Unit 1, Day 12, "Was It Worth It?", the day before this task is
launched
  You are chairing, so do not try to observe as well. Just record. The
  seminar asks students to bring one soldier and one figure for Canadian
  dead, with sources, and to argue three positions — which means the room
  is talking about exactly what B2.2 and B3.1 ask for, on the last day
  before anyone has an essay to defend.
  Listen for two things, and be careful which code you write beside each.
  B2.2 covers 1914 to 1929 ONLY, so it is the separate signing of the
  Treaty of Versailles and the seat at the League that count towards it —
  a student who reaches for the week Parliament debated in 1939 is arguing
  continuity and change, which is good history and is not B2.2.
  B3.1 is about people who CONTRIBUTED to Canadian society and politics,
  so an ordinary private's attestation paper used as evidence about cost is
  not B3.1 however well it is used; what is B3.1 is a student who brings
  Borden, Bourassa, Macphail, F. O. Loft, the Trades and Labour Congress or
  the League of Indians into the argument and says what they changed.
  Some students will say this here and never write it — several of them
  will choose a different question entirely tomorrow.
  Record: your class list, one column, one phrase per student who spoke.
  Then a second column for who did not, because that is who to reach in
  Day 14's conference.

The product evidence is the essay handed in on Unit 1, Day 17.
