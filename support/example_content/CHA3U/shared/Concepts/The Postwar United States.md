The United States came out of the Second World War with its industry intact,
its rivals ruined, and a quarter-century of growth ahead of it. That growth
is the foundation of almost everything Americans now describe as the
post-war good life. It was also distributed by rules, and the rules decided
who got a house, a degree and a pension — which is why the same decades look
entirely different depending on which family you follow.

## Prosperity, delivered by policy

| Programme or trend | What it did, and for whom |
| --- | --- |
| GI Bill, 1944 | Tuition and loans for veterans; about eight million used the education benefits within seven years |
| Federal mortgage guarantees | Made suburban ownership affordable — in neighbourhoods that excluded Black buyers |
| Interstate highways, from 1956 | Connected the suburbs, and cut expressways through city neighbourhoods |
| Union contracts | Wages, pensions and health coverage in manufacturing, mainly for men |
| Consumer credit | Appliances, televisions and cars bought on instalments |

Line two is the one to argue with. The National Archives, describing the GI
Bill's results, notes that "Black vets were often unable to get bank loans
for mortgages in Black neighborhoods, and they faced prejudice and
discrimination that overwhelming excluded them from buying homes in 'white'
suburban neighborhoods." A universal law, administered through banks and
local practice, produced an unequal outcome — and since housing equity became
most families' main asset, the gap did not stay in the 1950s. It compounded.

Suburbanization drew households, retail and tax revenue out of the cities and
left older neighbourhoods with the costs and less of the base. Birth rates
rose sharply for about two decades, producing a generation large enough to
reshape schools, then universities, then markets, simply by existing.

## Then the growth stopped behaving

From the 1970s the pattern broke. Oil supply shocks pushed up prices while
growth slowed. Manufacturing employment fell in the steel and automotive
regions as production moved south and offshore, and a stretch of the country
acquired the name Rust Belt from what was left. Union membership in the
private sector declined steadily. High-technology and financial industries
grew fastest, concentrated in particular metropolitan areas rather than
spread across industrial towns.

The share of income and wealth going to the highest earners rose over the
following decades, and stopped tracking the growth of the economy as a whole.
Deregulation, tax changes, trade agreements and the decline of bargaining
power all appear in the explanations, weighted differently by different
economists — which is a genuine disagreement about causes, not a fact you can
look up.

The financial crisis of 2008 concentrated its damage on households with the
least margin, and on Black and Latino homeowners in particular, undoing much
of what post-war housing policy had eventually let them accumulate.
