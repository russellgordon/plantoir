Fourteen lines, spoken by the Chorus before anyone else is on stage. The
method is on [[Reading Shakespeare]], and the play's own page is
[[Romeo and Juliet]]. Every answer is folded away under its question: write
yours first.

| Line | The Prologue |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Two households, both alike in dignity, |
| 2 | In fair Verona, where we lay our scene, |
| 3 | From ancient grudge break to new mutiny, |
| 4 | Where civil blood makes civil hands unclean. |
| 5 | From forth the fatal loins of these two foes |
| 6 | A pair of star-crossed lovers take their life; |
| 7 | Whose misadventured piteous overthrows |
| 8 | Doth with their death bury their parents' strife. |
| 9 | The fearful passage of their death-marked love, |
| 10 | And the continuance of their parents' rage, |
| 11 | Which, but their children's end, naught could remove, |
| 12 | Is now the two hours' traffic of our stage; |
| 13 | The which if you with patient ears attend, |
| 14 | What here shall miss, our toil shall strive to mend. |

### Question 1

Paraphrase lines 1 to 4 in your own words, one line at a time.

> [!answer]-
>
> Two families of the same high rank, in Verona, where this play is set,
> break out of an old grudge into new fighting, and citizens' blood
> stains citizens' hands.

### Question 2

The word "civil" appears twice in line 4. Give two meanings the word can
carry, and say why the line needs both.

> [!answer]-
>
> "Of citizens", as in civil war, and "polite" or "well behaved". The
> people fighting are neighbours in one city, and they are the
> respectable families. Hands that should be civil are the ones covered
> in blood.

### Question 3

Read lines 5 to 8. What are we told will happen to the two lovers, and
what will that do to their parents' quarrel?

> [!answer]-
>
> They will die: they "take their life" (line 6). Their deaths "bury
> their parents' strife" (line 8): the feud ends, and only because of
> the deaths.

### Question 4

What does "star-crossed" (line 6) suggest about who or what is to blame?

> [!answer]-
>
> That the stars are against them: fate or bad luck, decided before they
> act. Hold on to that claim. The play goes on to show a great many
> people making choices.

### Question 5

Mark the rhyme at the end of each line with a letter, starting with *a*.
What pattern do you get, and what is a poem in this form called?

> [!answer]-
>
> *abab cdcd efef gg*: dignity and mutiny, scene and unclean, foes and
> overthrows, life and strife, love and remove, rage and stage, attend
> and mend. Three groups of four lines and a closing pair, fourteen
> lines in all: a sonnet.

### Question 6

"The two hours' traffic of our stage" (line 12). Who is speaking, and to
whom? What does that tell you about how this play expects to be met?

> [!answer]-
>
> The Chorus, speaking for the actors, straight to the audience.
> "Our stage" and "our toil" admit that this is a performance, and
> "patient ears" asks you to listen. It was written to be heard.

### Question 7

The ending has been given away by line 8. In two sentences: what is left
for you to find out?

> [!answer]-
>
> No fixed answer. Bring yours to class. One place to start: not what
> happens, but how it happens to people who could have stopped it.
