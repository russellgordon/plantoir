Nobody marks your checker but you. Every mathematician you will ever
meet runs one — a quiet second pass that catches errors before they
matter — and the earlier you build yours, the sooner tests stop being
scary. Four habits make up the whole machine.

## Substitute back — into everything

Solved an equation? The equation itself will referee. Believe
$x = 3$, $y = -2$ solves a system? Feed the pair into **both**
equations — a point that satisfies only one of them is not a solution,
just a point on one line. When each substitution lands as a true
statement — left side equal to right side, an identity — the system
has certified your answer, no key required.
[[Solving Systems Algebraically]] builds this in from day one, and it
costs less than a minute.

## Estimate first, compare after

Before solving, write down a rough expected size — the reflex
[[Estimation Duels]] trains. The flagpole in
[[How Tall Is the Flagpole]] should come out near 8 m; if the tangent
ratio hands you 412, one of you is wrong, and now you know to look.
This check only works if the estimate came *before* the answer did.

## Check the units

Units are a free error detector. If a break-even answer arrives in
dollars when the question asked how many tickets, something upstream
broke — and the units caught it without you rereading a single step.
Carry them through every line, as [[Showing Your Thinking]] insists.

## Ask whether the vertex belongs on the sketch

After completing the square, put the vertex on a two-second sketch.
$y = x^2 - 8x + 3$ opens upward with vertex $(4, -13)$ — does that
sit sensibly with a $y$-intercept of 3? It does. Had the vertex landed
at $(4, 13)$, an upward parabola could never come back down to cross
the $y$-axis at 3 — the sketch objects instantly. A graph that
disagrees with its own equation is the cheapest alarm in
[[The Vertex Form]].
