Every task page publishes a criteria table before the job starts. Most
people read it once, on launch day, and never open it again — then hand
in work that misses two rows they could have closed in twenty minutes
of bench time they already had. This page is those twenty minutes, and
it is a technician's habit rather than a school one: nobody signs off a
job without walking it first.

We do it together the first time, on a sheet that belongs to nobody, so
that the move is not new to you the day it is your own work on the
bench.

## The walk-round

1. Open the task page and put the criteria table beside the thing
   itself — the sheet, the binder, the breadboard, the machine.
2. Take the rows in order. Against each one write **yes**, **not yet**,
   or **partly**, and one line of evidence: the page, the label, the
   reading, the thing a client would actually see. "Yes" with nothing
   beside it is a hope.
3. Find your weakest row. Not the quickest to fix — the weakest.
4. Write one sentence saying what you will change, and in which period.
5. Then change it. This is why a self-check is never scheduled on a
   hand-in day: a judgement you have no time to act on is only a worry
   with a table attached.

Steps 2 and 3 are the whole exercise. Naming your weakest row honestly
is harder than any of the fixing, because it means writing down the
thing you were hoping would not get looked at.

> [!example]- One row, walked honestly
> From [[The Network Job]]: *"Cables that pass — every cable tests
> clean and carries a label."*
>
> **Partly.** Six of our eight test clean and are labelled at both
> ends. Two pass the tester but are labelled at one end only, because
> we ran them before we agreed the naming scheme. Weakest row —
> everything else in the binder points at those labels.
> **Change:** relabel both ends of the two runs and correct the cable
> map, first fifteen minutes of the binder period.
>
> That entry took ninety seconds and is worth more than "looks good"
> written down six rows in a row.

## Then say it out loud

Read your judgement to the person at the next bench. It catches the
rows where you were being generous to yourself, and you will usually
hear it in your own voice before they say a word. Their job is to ask
"what would a client see?" — not to give you a score.

## Where this fits

Neither your judgement of your own work nor a classmate's is any part
of your mark — see [[How Marks Work]]. That is deliberate and it cuts
both ways: nothing you write here can cost you anything, which is
exactly what makes it safe to write it honestly. The same goes for the
bench checks that close the first three units, which you mark yourself.

What it changes is the work. A walk-round that leads to a fix is the
cheapest mark improvement available in this course, and by the end of the
course it is a habit you run without being told — which was the point of
teaching it.
