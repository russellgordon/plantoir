Around the room hang a dozen messages: a parcel that could not be
delivered, a streaming account "on hold", a prize with a countdown
timer, a note from "the principal" asking for gift cards. Every
specimen in the gallery is invented for this activity — the brands
and senders are fictional — but each one is built from the same
techniques used in real attacks arriving in real inboxes today.

## The task

Tour the gallery in groups with two jobs. First, rank the specimens
from crudest to most convincing — argue about the order, because the
argument is where the learning is. Second, for each specimen, catch
the *tells*: circle every detail that gives the game away, and name
the pressure the message is applying. Finish by writing your group's
top three rules for spotting a phish, in your own words.

> [!question]- Getting started (click to expand)
> - Read the sender, not just the message. Where would replying
>   actually go?
> - Hover before you trust: where does that link *say* it goes, and
>   where does it actually point?
> - Every phish sells an emotion before it asks for a click. Name
>   the emotion — urgency, fear, greed, helpfulness — and the
>   request underneath gets easier to see.

## What tends to surface

Ranking forces the uncomfortable finding: the best specimens have
almost no tells. Spelling mistakes and absurd promises mark only the
crude ones; the convincing ones are beaten not by sharper eyes but
by better *habits* — verifying through a second channel, never
acting inside the message itself. Those habits are exactly what
[[Staying Secure Online]] turns into a checklist worth keeping.

## An extension

Design the lure that would catch *you* — the message tuned to your
own life — and trade with a partner. Knowing your personal bait is
worth more than any general rule. Then take the gallery home: which
specimen would be most likely to catch someone you live with?

> [!note] The answer is not on this page
> There is no official ranking and no complete list of tells — real
> attackers retire old tricks the day we learn them. The class
> builds its defences together, and rebuilds them as the specimens
> change.
