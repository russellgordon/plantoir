**The question:** a government says sorry for something the state did.
Does that change anything, and if so, what?

Canada has done this more than once. On 22 September 1988, Prime
Minister Brian Mulroney apologised in the House of Commons for the
internment and dispossession of Japanese Canadians; the settlement
attached to it included \$21,000 to each surviving individual, a
\$12 million community fund, pardons for those wrongfully convicted, and
acknowledgement of citizenship for those wrongfully deported. On
11 June 2008, Prime Minister Stephen Harper apologised for the
residential school system; he had already apologised in the House on
22 June 2006 for the Chinese head tax. On 28 November 2017 Prime
Minister Justin Trudeau apologised for the purge of LGBTQ people from
the public service, the military, and the RCMP.

## Prepare

Choose one of those four. Find the words actually used — apologies are
public documents — and note three things: what the state admits it did,
whom it addresses, and what else came with it. Then find one response
from a person or organization the apology was addressed to.
[[Government Records]] and [[Commemoration and Public Memory]] are the
background; the wording is your evidence.

## Three positions

**An apology is accountability.** A state that names what it did loses
the ability to deny it later. The admission enters the record, and
everything afterwards — funding, litigation, curriculum, this course —
argues from it rather than about it.

**An apology can substitute for action.** Saying sorry is cheaper than
changing a policy, and it can close a file politically while the
conditions that produced the harm continue. The Truth and Reconciliation
Commission's Calls to Action, released 2 June 2015, devote a block
(58–61) to apologies still outstanding — and ninety-odd others to things
that are not apologies.

**An apology is necessary and not sufficient.** On this view the
question is badly posed: an apology is a precondition for redress, not a
competitor with it, and you judge it by what follows within five years.

> [!question] Before you argue
> Who is an apology for — the people wronged, their descendants, or the
> country doing the apologising? Your answer decides most of the rest.

Artists reached some of this first. [[Canada Since 1982]] took shape
alongside work by writers and filmmakers who put these histories in
front of a public that had not asked for them. Consider whether a novel
or a film can do something an official statement cannot — and carry the
question into [[What Should a Country Remember|What Should a Country Remember?]].
