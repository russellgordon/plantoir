## What you are making

A one-page **brief** on a single current or emerging innovation — the
kind of page a busy person could read in three minutes and come away
genuinely informed. Choose from the menu:

- an AI system you have used or heard about, in the spirit of
  [[Automation and Artificial Intelligence]]
- a category of [[Connected Devices]] — doorbells, watches, tractors
- an accessibility technology, connecting to
  [[Bias and Accessibility in Technology]]
- an automation changing a skilled trade — welding, carpentry, auto
  repair, agriculture
- an emerging innovation you spotted through [[Tech Headlines]]

Whatever you choose, the brief answers seven things: what it does in
plain language, **who built it** — the people, communities, or countries
behind it, named — who benefits, who bears the costs, its benefits and
limits, **one job or trade that does this work differently now**, and
**one open question** nobody has settled yet. The who-built-it line
matters: innovation gets credited to a handful of famous names, and
[[Whose Innovations Count]] is about everybody else. Every borrowed
claim is credited, following [[Finding Answers Online]].

## How to work

1. Pick from the menu — the best choice is the one you already have an
   opinion about, because the research can then surprise you.
2. Research with at least two independent sources, and note where they
   disagree; disagreement is where the interesting sentences come from.
3. Draft the seven parts. "Who bears the costs" needs a real someone —
   a job, a community, a user group — not "society".
4. Trade drafts with a classmate in class and apply the grade-six
   test: could a sixth grader follow your "what it does" paragraph? If
   not, the paragraph is not done.
5. Finalise, with sources credited. Most of the research and the
   drafting happen in the working periods — [[How Marks Work]] explains
   why a brief written where I can see it being written is better
   evidence than one that arrives finished.
6. Present it to the room in three minutes: what it does, who built it,
   who pays, and the question still open. No slides needed; the page in
   your hand is enough.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like in your brief |
| --- | --- |
| Plain-language clarity | The "what it does" section passes grade six |
| Builders named | The people, communities, or countries behind it are named, and the naming is sourced |
| Named beneficiaries | Who benefits is someone specific, not "everyone" |
| Named costs | Who bears the costs is someone specific, not "society" |
| Balanced assessment | Benefits and limits both appear, both concrete, and one of them says what changes if this becomes normal |
| Work that changed | One job or trade is named, with what it used to involve and what it involves now |
| Heard as well as read | The room could follow who built it, who pays, and what is still open, without the page in front of them |
| A genuine open question | The question is one your sources could not answer |
| Credit given | A reader can find every source you leaned on |

## Reflect

Write a [[Dev Journal]] entry: what did you believe about this
innovation before researching, and what does the evidence actually
support? A brief that changed its own author's mind is the strongest
kind — and "same impression, better reasons" counts as change too.

> [!success]- If everything on the menu feels distant (click to expand)
> Start from your own pocket, wrist, or kitchen. The phone that
> autocorrects you, the app that recommends your next video, the
> thermostat that learns the schedule — you live inside this menu
> already. Pick the innovation you touched most recently today.

  Watch for: where the second search goes. The finished brief names

  Record: a tick beside each name on the class list for a second

TALK — Unit 4, Day 6, during the working period on the brief
  Ask: "Who else did you consider as bearing the cost, and why did you
