Find out, with calipers and your fingers, how much bigger a printed hole
must be drawn than the thing that goes in it. The class shares three
identical **tolerance coupons**, printed before the unit started
(each is a ten- to fifteen-minute print): a flat strip with a row of
holes drawn at slightly different sizes, a matching peg, and a row of
hexagonal pockets for a ¼″-20 nut. Every crew measures a copy of the
same coupon, and the class agrees one clearance from all of them, so
everyone designs from the same number — which is what makes it a
standard rather than a guess.

> [!warning] Care notes
> **A few coupons, many hands** — each is handled over the table, never
> dropped, and passed crew to crew in the order on the board. **Nothing
> gets forced** — a peg pushed hard into a tight hole cracks the coupon
> for everyone after you; the answer to "does it fit?" is found gently.
> **Calipers are precision tools** — closed gently, never used as a
> hammer or a scraper, and returned to their case.

## What you need

- [ ] One of the three class coupons (about ten minutes per crew, so
      six crews are done in two rounds)
- [ ] Digital calipers, zeroed with the jaws closed
- [ ] A steel ¼″-20 nut from the parts tray
- [ ] Your notebook, with a table ruled for drawn size, measured size,
      and fit

## The work

1. **Read the labels.** Each hole and pocket is labelled with the size
   it was *drawn* at in the model. Copy those into your table.
2. **Measure what printed.** Measure each hole across two directions
   and write down both — printed holes are often not quite round.
3. **Test the fit, gently.** Push the peg into each hole and the nut
   into each pocket. Record one word for each: *won't go*, *tight*,
   *snug*, or *loose*.
4. **Find the line.** Which is the smallest hole the peg goes into
   snugly? Subtract the peg's size from that hole's drawn size: that
   difference is the class **clearance**, the tolerance you will add to
   every fit you design.
5. **Compare crews.** The clearance goes up on the board, crew by crew.
   If two crews disagree, measure again together — the class designs
   from one number, and it should be the right one.

## What can go wrong

- **Calipers not zeroed.** Every number is off by the same amount, and
  it looks like a printer problem. Close the jaws and zero before the
  first measurement.
- **Measuring the rough edge.** The first layer squashes slightly wider
  than the rest. Measure in the middle of the hole's depth, not at the
  bed side.
- **Trusting one number.** One reading is a guess. Three that agree are
  a measurement.

## Level up

Work out, from your table, how much smaller than drawn this printer
makes a hole, on average. Then say whether a hole drawn twice as big
would shrink by twice as much — and design the one-line test print that
would settle it.
