These questions follow [[Copyright and Fair Dealing]] — every one of
them comes up the moment you [[Cut a Highlight Reel]]. Each time,
answer the real question: *may we publish this*, and why or why not?

## Questions

1. An editor wants a popular band's song under the volleyball
   highlight reel. "Everyone uses songs in their reels." May we?
2. A parent emails the newsroom a great photo she took at the final:
   "Feel free to use this!" May we, and what do we do first?
3. A writer wants to illustrate a story with a meme built on a still
   from a well-known movie. It is clearly a joke. May we?
4. A designer generates an image with an AI tool to illustrate the
   phone-pilot story. May we, and what does honesty require if we do?
5. A Grade 9 student submits her own comic strip for publication.
   May we, and who owns it afterwards?
6. **Explain why** none of these is actually an argument: "it was
   already on the internet," "we credited them," "nobody will care."
7. **From the shop.** Your crew finds a phone-to-tripod mount on a
   model-sharing site, licensed CC BY-SA. You want to widen its clamp
   by 4 mm and print it for [[The Rig]]. May you — and what do you owe
   the original maker, and anyone who later remixes yours?

## Answers

> [!success]- Answer 1
> No. The band owns the song and the recording, and the school site is
> public, not private. "Everyone does it" means everyone is unlicensed
> — not that we are licensed. Use music whose licence permits our use,
> and read that licence: "royalty-free" still comes with conditions.

> [!success]- Answer 2
> Yes — she took it, so she owns it, and she is offering permission.
> First: save the email (that *is* the permission record), confirm she
> is happy being credited by name, and run the credit. We gain a
> licence to publish the photo; the copyright stays hers.

> [!success]- Answer 3
> Probably not. Fair dealing exists, and parody can qualify — but it
> is a defence to be argued, not a permission slip, and a published
> school site is a weak place to test it. Safer: make our own image.

> [!success]- Answer 4
> Genuinely unsettled — who owns an AI image is an open legal
> question, and the tools were trained on other people's work. A
> defensible line: never for news photos; if one illustrates a story,
> label it plainly as AI-generated. Honesty here is not negotiable.

> [!success]- Answer 5
> Yes, with her written consent to publish — and she keeps the
> copyright. Publication is a licence the creator grants, not a
> purchase. Credit her the way we credit any contributor.

> [!success]- Answer 6
> Being visible is not being free — posting a photo does not waive its
> copyright. Credit is attribution, not permission; crediting a band
> does not license its song. And "nobody will care" concedes the act
> is wrong and hopes not to be caught — a newsroom that reasons that
> way has no standing to hold anyone else to account.

> [!success]- Answer 7
> Yes — BY-SA allows changes. You owe the maker credit (name, where
> the model came from, and its licence), and "SA", share-alike, means
> your remix goes out under the same licence, so the next crew can
> remix it too. A model marked NC would still be fine for a class
> project; one marked "all rights reserved" would not be yours to
> change at all. Your process record says which it was, and why you
> were allowed.
