There is a mirror in this room, and for hours a week you work in front of it in
clothes that show what your body is doing. That is not a small thing, and
pretending it is would be dishonest. So this page says plainly what the course
expects, and what it does not.

What we are after is **healthy practice and a positive body image** — the
working relationship you have with your own body while you are learning. It is
about what your body can do, what it needs, and what you say to yourself about
it. It is not about what shape you are.

## What actually improves your dancing

- **Fuel.** A body that has not eaten cannot learn a sequence. Arriving
  underfed does not make you lighter; it makes you slower to pick things up and
  much easier to injure.
- **Sleep.** Motor learning — the process that turns a sequence you can follow
  into one you can perform — consolidates while you sleep.
- **Water.** Dehydrated muscle fatigues sooner and recovers more slowly.
- **Rest.** Adaptation happens between sessions, not during them.
  [[Cool-Down and Recovery]] is part of the training, not the bit at the end.

None of those are body-type instructions. They apply identically to someone who
has trained for ten years and someone whose first class was last week.

> [!note] Dance has a history here, and you should know about it
> Some dance traditions have, in the past and sometimes still, pushed a single
> narrow body type and treated pain and hunger as professionalism. That history
> is real, it caused genuine harm, and it is not the standard in this studio.
> If something you have been told elsewhere conflicts with what you read here,
> raise it — with your teacher, or with an adult you trust. Persistent pain,
> dizziness, or anxiety about eating are things to tell someone about, not to
> work through.

## Comparison is the actual problem

The mirror is a tool for checking alignment, not for auditing yourself against
the person behind you. Everyone here has a different skeleton, and range of
motion at a joint is largely structural — some people's hips simply rotate
further, and no amount of effort transfers one person's hip socket into
another's. Compare your work to your own work three weeks ago. That comparison
is fair, and it is the one being assessed.

Your progress is measured by control, clarity, and understanding, not
appearance. Set goals you can reach — the fifth thing [[The Safe Dancer]] asks
you to write — and hold to [[Our Studio Agreement]], which covers how we speak
about each other's bodies and our own.
