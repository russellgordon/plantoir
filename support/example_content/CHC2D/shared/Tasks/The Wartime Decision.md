**Due: the end of Unit 2.** Individual. About 1,000 words.

One decision made by Canada or Canadians during the Second World War,
judged.

## The decisions on offer

- The internment of Japanese Canadians and the sale of their property.
- The refusal of the MS *St. Louis* and Canada's refugee policy of the
  1930s and 1940s.
- Conscription, again, and the 1942 plebiscite.
- The recruitment and treatment of First Nations, Métis, and Inuit
  service members.
- The decision to intern Ukrainian, Italian, or German Canadians.

## What the essay must do

1. **Establish what was decided**, by whom, when, and under what
   authority.
2. **Reconstruct the reasoning at the time** — what decision-makers said
   and what they knew. This is historical perspective, and it is not the
   same as agreeing.
3. **Establish what was known and by whom.** Distinguish what could not
   have been known from what was known and set aside.
4. **Judge it**, on stated grounds, and say what standard you are
   judging by.
5. **Say what followed** — the consequence, including any later apology
   or redress.

## How it is marked

| Criterion | What earns the marks |
| --- | --- |
| Accuracy | Dates, authorities, and figures correct and attributed |
| Perspective | The reasoning of the time reconstructed, not caricatured |
| Judgement | Grounds stated; you are not merely disapproving |
| Precision about uncertainty | Where evidence is contested, you say so |
| Citation | Sourced properly throughout |

> [!warning] Understanding a decision is not excusing it
> The point of reconstructing what people believed is to explain how the
> decision became possible — which makes the judgement stronger, not
> softer. That distinction is the whole of
> [[Historical Perspective]].

[[The Holocaust and Canada's Response]] and the investigation
[[Could Canada Have Done More|Could Canada Have Done More?]] are where several of these decisions
are worked through first.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 2, Day 18, during item 1's work period, before the
conferences start
  This is the task whose sources speak in the language of the state that
  made the decision — "evacuation", "protective custody", "enemy alien",
  "relocation". Nothing has taught them what to do about that yet: the page
  that does, "The Words Historians Use", is not reached until Unit 3, Day 4.
  So what you are watching here is instinct rather than compliance, and it
  is worth knowing which students have it before you teach the rule.
  Watch for: whether a student stops on one of those words, checks what it
  was doing, and then writes their own sentence around it — or lets it
  stand as the neutral description.
  Going well: a student asks what the order itself said, or sets the
  official word and a plain description of the same act side by side.
  Stuck: the state's word used as the neutral one, all the way down a
  paragraph.
  Record: one column on your day plan with a name in it — ten seconds each,
  and it is the list to reach during Day 19's revision.
  The Ministry's own wording for C2.5 puts "enemy aliens" inside quotation
  marks. A student who can say why is doing the expectation; a student who
  cannot is repeating it.
  What you are assessing is the handling of the evidence, never the
  community the decision was made about, and never a student's own family
  history. If a student is writing about something done to their own
  people, the conversation about care comes first and the assessment second.

TALK — Unit 2, Day 20, the conferences on that agenda
  Day 18's conferences are already spoken for: they are on the "what was
  known" section, which the task page prints, so those answers arrive
  prepared. Use Day 20 for these.
  Do not ask them to name a contemporary dissenter. Day 17's concept page
  works that exact move, with Bryce, Bourassa and Heaps as its examples, so
  the answer arrives rehearsed. Ask about the record instead.
  Ask: "Which part of this decision is missing from the record — and how
  would you find out whether the gap was deliberate?"
  Then: "What could the people this was done to have found out, at the
  time, about why it was being done?"
  A strong first answer names a specific silence and a way to test it: a
  minute that refers to a discussion nobody minuted, a file closed for
  fifty years, an instruction given by telephone and confirmed afterwards.
  A strong second answer is where historical perspective separates from
  hindsight. Both are C2.1 — the key issues that led to those interactions
  — approached from the side the essay is not organised around, because the
  task asks it to be built around the decision-makers.
  Record: one line on the conference list; the name they give you is the
  whole record.

The product evidence is the essay handed in on Unit 2, Day 21.
