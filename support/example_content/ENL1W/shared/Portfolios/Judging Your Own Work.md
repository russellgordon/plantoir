Every task page carries a table called **How it is marked**, published
before you start. Most people read it once at launch, hand the piece in,
and only look at the table again when the mark comes back — by which
time it is a verdict instead of a tool.

This page turns it back into a tool. It takes about fifteen minutes, and
we do it together the first time, on a draft of mine, so that the first
piece you ever judge this way is not your own.

## The routine

1. Put the task page beside your draft, with the criteria table visible.
2. Take one row at a time and write **yes**, **not yet**, or **partly** —
   and beside it, the evidence: a line number, a paragraph, the sentence
   the reader would point at. "Yes" with nothing beside it is a hope.
3. Read the piece out loud. Half the rows in an English course are
   audible before they are visible: a sentence that runs out of breath, a
   paragraph that ends where it should have started, a voice that goes
   flat in the middle.
4. Name your **weakest** row. Not the easiest one to fix — the weakest.
5. Write one sentence about what you will change, and when.
6. Change it, in the time the schedule gives you for exactly this. A
   judgement you have no time to act on is not a judgement, it is a
   worry, which is why this is never scheduled on the day something is
   due.

Steps 4 and 5 are the whole exercise. Everything before them is warm-up.
Naming the weakest row honestly is harder than the fixing, because it
means saying out loud the thing you were quietly hoping nobody would
notice.

> [!example]- One row, judged honestly
> From [[Who You Are, in Three Hundred Words]]: *"A reader is in the
> moment with you; the sentences vary and hold together."*
>
> **Partly.** The first half puts you in the kitchen — the radio, the
> burnt toast, my grandmother not looking up. Then paragraph three
> explains what it all meant, and the reader leaves the kitchen and
> never gets back in. Weakest row.
> **Change:** cut paragraph three to one line and give the words to the
> moment my grandmother finally looks up. Tomorrow, in the cutting
> period.
>
> That is worth more than "looks good" written beside all three rows,
> and it took less time.

## What this is not

It is not a mark, yours or anybody's. Nothing you write here counts
towards your grade, and neither does what a partner tells you in a
workshop — see [[How Marks Work]]. That is deliberate and it cuts both
ways: an honest "not yet" cannot cost you anything, which is precisely
what makes it worth writing.

It is also not a substitute for a reader. Your judgement tells you
whether the piece does what you meant; a partner tells you what actually
reached them. You need both, and they disagree more often than you would
think.

## Where it shows up later

Two of the six questions in [[The Portfolio Conversation]] are this
routine in another form: the sentences you are proudest of, and what
still does not work along with your plan for it. By January you will
have run it often enough that the answers are already written down, in
your [[Reading and Writing Journal]], in your own words. People who have
never done it spend the first five minutes of that conversation
inventing opinions about their own writing, and it shows.
