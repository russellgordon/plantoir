From a provided bundle of stories and images, lay out a front page —
deciding what leads, what supports, and what waits inside. Hierarchy,
headlines, captions, and white space are the whole craft here; it is
[[Design Principles]] with real stakes, and a dress rehearsal for
[[The Front Page]], where the stories will be your newsroom's own.

> [!warning] Care and consent notes
> **Every image keeps its credit** — the provided photographs arrive
> with credits attached, and a layout that drops one is incomplete,
> not minimalist; [[Copyright and Fair Dealing]] explains why.
> **People in published photos need consent** — before any layout
> leaves as a proof, confirm the [[Releases and Consent|releases]]
> exist. **Save versions as you go** — a layout file has hours in it
> and no card to back up; keep yesterday's version. **The printer is
> shared equipment** — proof one page, and a jam means ask, never pull.

## What you need

- [ ] The story bundle: four stories, six images, all with credits
- [ ] Your page layout software, page size set before anything is placed
- [ ] Paper and pencil for thumbnail sketches — yes, first
- [ ] Your notebook, open to the seven tests from [[News Values]]

## The work

1. **Rank the stories before touching software.** Which story leads
   is a news judgement call, not a design call — argue it with the
   seven tests, and write the order down.
2. **Thumbnail three layouts in pencil.** Ninety seconds each, boxes
   and lines only — a sketched weak idea dies in seconds; built in
   software, it gets defended for an hour.
3. **Place the lead story and its image first.** Biggest headline,
   strongest image, top — everything else is placed in relation to it.
4. **Size headlines to the hierarchy, not the space.** The type from
   [[Typography]] does the ranking out loud: if the second story
   shouts louder than the lead, the page argues with itself.
5. **Caption every image.** Who, doing what, when — an uncaptioned
   photograph is a question the page refuses to answer, and your
   [[Caption Practice]] reps pay off here.
6. **Protect the white space.** Empty space is how a reader knows
   where one story ends — a page filled to the edges reads as noise.
7. **Now consider the site version.** The same stories on the class
   website scroll instead of spread — note two things your print
   hierarchy must change online, and why.

## What can go wrong

- **Everything is big, so nothing is.** Four large headlines cancel
  out — pick one lead and let it win; hierarchy means someone loses.
- **An image sized up past its resolution.** Sharp on screen, soft in
  print. Check the image's real size before it anchors the page.
- **The page is "done" and a caption is missing.** Layouts hide gaps
  from tired eyes — swap with another desk and proof each other cold.

## Level up

Build the page again with your second-ranked story leading —
sometimes the other argument is better than you thought, and
sometimes the exercise proves your first call for good.
