Your mark reflects what you can do with geography, not what you can
recall about it.

## The seventy and the thirty

Every Grade 9 credit in Ontario is built the same way. **Seventy per cent**
comes from work spread across the whole semester — and it is not an average.
It leans towards your **most consistent** work and towards your **most
recent**, because what you can do with a map at the end of the course says
more than what you could do with one at the start of the course. **Thirty
per cent** comes from a final evaluation at the end, which here is two
things on two different days: [[The Inquiry Examination]], written on paper
about a place you have never studied, and [[The Local Inquiry]], answered
out loud in front of somebody who works on it. The examination is the larger
of the two.

**The seventy** is the work you hand in during the term, in the order
you meet it: [[The Field Record]], [[The Region Study]],
[[The Risk Report]], [[The Resource Investigation]],
[[The Product's Journey]], [[The Population Profile]],
[[The Policy Brief]], and [[The Land Use Proposal]]. They are not
equally weighted — a study that runs for a fortnight is worth more than
a brief that runs for three days, and the field record is four short
entries rather than one large piece. Ask me for the exact split
whenever you want it; it is not a secret, it is just not the useful
thing to carry around.

**The thirty** reaches across the whole course on purpose, which is
why neither half of it is a fifth unit test. The examination puts
unseen maps and unseen data in front of you and asks you to read,
explain, and judge. The local inquiry asks a question about the place
you actually live, and answers it to someone who could act on it.

## Four kinds of thinking, not one

Every task asks for more than one of these, and the balance shifts with
the task.

| What is being judged | What it looks like in geography |
| --- | --- |
| What you know | Landforms, processes, resources, what the symbols on a map mean |
| How you think | Reading a pattern, weighing a source, deciding what evidence can carry |
| How you communicate | A map that argues, a brief someone can act on, ninety seconds on your feet |
| Where you apply it | Doing all of that about a place nobody worked through with you first |

The fourth is why the last unit exists. Explaining a region we studied
together is one thing; standing in front of a planner with a question
about your own street is another, and only one of them resembles what
this subject is actually for.

## Three kinds of evidence, and two of them are not paper

**What you make** is the obvious one: maps, reports, briefs, profiles,
field entries. **What I watch you do** is the second, and in this
subject it is mostly one thing — what you do with data or a map
*before* you decide what it shows. Whether the scale bar gets read
before the boundary gets drawn. Whether you check what year a layer is
from before you build a sentence on it. Whether, when the ground
disagrees with the number, you write down the disagreement or quietly
drop one of them. A finished map looks equally confident either way,
so this evidence exists only while you work. **What you tell me** is
the third: the conferences on the working days, the checkpoint before
the culminating inquiry, the answer you give when I ask where a figure
came from.

If your report is unfinished and you are quiet in seminars, the
conference is often where the strongest evidence of the week comes
from. That is not a consolation prize. It is how the mark is meant to
work.

## Where feedback lands

For every task in the seventy but the field record, there is a period
where I sit with you while the work is still moving, and the class
straight after it has a working period whose job is acting on what we
said. It is a working period, not a whole class — half an hour,
sometimes less. The field record works differently for a good reason:
each entry comes back with comments before the next field day, so the
place you act on the feedback is the next site.

[[Judging Your Own Work]] is the routine for using one of those
periods well. We do it together the first time.

## What earns a high mark here

- **Evidence.** A claim with a source and a date beats a confident
  sentence, always.
- **Specificity.** Named places, real figures, actual distances.
- **Explanation over description.** Saying what a pattern *is* earns
  some marks; saying why it is there earns most of them.
- **Fair treatment of disagreement.** Representing a position you do not
  hold, accurately, is a skill this course marks.
- **Honesty about limits.** "This is what the data cannot tell us" is
  worth marks, not lost ones.

## What is not in your mark

**How you work** is reported separately, on the same report card, as
**E, G, S or N** — six habits: responsibility, organization,
independent work, collaboration, initiative, and self-regulation. They
matter, we will talk about them, and they do not move your percentage.
Some courses have an expectation that is itself about a habit, and
there the habit is curriculum and is marked like anything else in the
curriculum. Geography of Canada has none. The closest thing to
one is A2.2, which asks you to discuss difficult subjects with
consideration for people who see them differently — and that is
Communication, one of the four categories above, rather than a work
habit. So the line here is clean: your mark is about the work, that
column is about the worker.

**Your own judgement of your work, and your classmates',** are not part
of your mark. That is why the four [[Portfolios/index|portfolio]]
entries carry no mark: three of them are you judging your own learning,
which is exactly the evidence a mark may not rest on. They are still
required, still read, and still the fastest way to get better — see
[[Judging Your Own Work]]. The same goes for the peer reviews and the
gallery walks: useful, and not marks.

**Seminars are not a participation mark.** How often you speak moves
nothing. The seminars are preparation — the argument you lose in a
seminar is the one you fix in the brief. Two places do judge what you
say, and both are printed on the task page: [[The Policy Brief]], where
you argue your recommendation on the day it is handed in, and the day 3
checkpoint of [[The Local Inquiry]], where you account for your own
concept. Neither of them counts how often you spoke.

**Practice is not marked.** Most of what is on the "things to do before
our next class" list is practice — a reading, a set of case questions, a
sketch finished off — and none of it is judged. When that list says
"finish the report", it is a reminder that a task is due, not homework
being marked: the periods to build it are in the schedule, and I have
watched you use them. The four field-record entries go further and are
written in class on the day, with the ground still under your feet.

## Late work, and revision

Talk to me before the deadline and we will set a new one. The revision
that matters in this course happens *before* something is handed in
rather than after it comes back — that is what the conference and the
working period after it are for. The one piece that revises across the
term is [[The Field Record]]: four entries, each one written after you
have read the comments on the one before, which is the closest thing
here to a second attempt.

> [!tip] The criteria live on the task
> Every task page ends with what earns the marks for that piece of work.
> Read it before you start, not after you finish.
