This course is one credit. What it opens is wider than most students
expect, and narrower than the phrase "get into tech" suggests. Here is
the honest map.

## Next, while you are still here

- **ICS4U** in Grade 12 — the direct continuation: bigger programs,
  data structures, algorithm efficiency, object-oriented design.
- **Cooperative education** — a real workplace for credits, and the
  single best way to find out whether you like the work before you pay
  tuition for it.
- **A Specialist High Skills Major** in Information and Communications
  Technology, if your school offers one: a bundle of credits,
  certifications, and co-op focused on the sector.
- **The Ontario Youth Apprenticeship Program**, for the trades side of
  computing — network cabling, electronics, and related trades that
  start earning while training.
- **Clubs and contests** — robotics, game jams, hackathons, and the
  Canadian Computing Competition. Two evenings a month, and a portfolio
  by the end of the course.

## After school

| Route | Length | What it is good at |
| --- | --- | --- |
| College diploma (2–3 years) | Programmer-analyst, networking, cybersecurity, game development | Getting hired and building things, hands first |
| University degree (4 years) | Computer science, software engineering, data science, cognitive science | Theory, research, and roles that require the degree |
| College-to-university transfer | Varies | Starting close to home, finishing with a degree |
| Apprenticeship or certification | Months to years | Trades and infrastructure work |
| Self-taught plus portfolio | Ongoing | Real, harder without the structure, and easier with prior credits |

Ontario's colleges and universities publish entrance requirements
plainly, and the requirement for computer science degrees is usually
Grade 12 mathematics rather than Grade 12 computer science. Check the
specific programs, not the general advice — including this page's.

## Work that does not look like "programmer"

Software touches everything, so the jobs do too: quality assurance,
technical writing, IT support, systems administration, database
administration, data analysis, UX research and design, security
analysis, technical sales, teaching, and every one of the collaborative
fields in [[Where the Field Is Going]]. Several of these pay well, hire
steadily, and never require you to write a compiler.

## The habits that decide it

The Ministry's Essential Skills list — reading, document use, numeracy,
writing, oral communication, working with others, thinking, computer
use, and continuous learning — sounds like a poster until you watch a
project fail. The ones that decide who is useful in this field:

- **Reading** — documentation, error messages, and other people's code,
  which is most of the job.
- **Writing** — a usage note somebody can follow, and a status update
  somebody can act on.
- **Thinking and problem solving** — narrowing a fault instead of
  guessing at it.
- **Working with others** — asking a good question, taking a review
  without flinching, saying early that you are stuck.
- **Continuous learning** — the specific tools in this course will be
  replaced; the ability to pick up the replacement will not.

## If the field does not look like you

Several organisations exist specifically to open computing to students
who are under-represented in it — girls and young women, Black and
Indigenous youth, students with disabilities, and students in rural,
northern, and remote communities. Actua's network of university- and
college-based programs, Canada Learning Code, Hackergal, and Black Boys
Code all run events across the country, many of them free or online.
[[Who Gets to Build]] is where we discuss why this matters; the
practical point here is narrower and worth saying plainly: these are
open to you, and the application is usually a form and a date.

> [!note]- If none of this is where you are headed
> Then this credit is still doing something for you. Automating a task
> that used to take an hour is useful in a lab, a kitchen, a stockroom,
> or a studio. Understanding what software can and cannot do makes you
> harder to sell to, and better at asking for what you need from the
> people who build it.

[[Careers in Computer Science]] goes further into what the work is
actually like day to day.
