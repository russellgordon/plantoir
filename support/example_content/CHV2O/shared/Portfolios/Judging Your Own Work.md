Every task page ends with a table of criteria, published before you
start. Most people read it once, on the day the task is launched, and
never again — and then hand in work that misses two rows they could have
fixed in fifteen minutes. This page is the fifteen minutes.

We do it together the first time, on somebody else's brief, so that the
move is not new to you when it is your own work being judged. By Unit 3
you run it without being asked, which is the point of teaching it at all.

## The routine

1. Put the criteria table beside your draft. Not in another tab — beside
   it.
2. Go row by row. For each one write **yes**, **not yet**, or **partly**,
   and one sentence of evidence: the paragraph, the sentence, the figure
   a reader would land on. "Yes" with nothing pointed at is a wish.
3. Find your weakest row. Not the easiest to fix — the weakest.
4. Write one sentence saying what you will change, and in which period.
5. Change it. That is why the self-check is never scheduled for the day
   something is due: a judgement you have no time to act on is just a
   worry.

Steps 2 and 3 are the whole exercise. Naming your weakest row honestly is
harder than any of the fixing, because it means writing down the thing
you were hoping nobody would notice.

> [!example]- One row, judged honestly
> From [[The Rights Case]]: *"Analysis — the limit is tested against the
> actual standard, not against your instinct."*
>
> **Partly.** I set out the section 1 steps and I answer the first two
> properly. On minimal impairment I write "the government could have
> done less", which is my instinct with a heading on it — I never say
> what the less-restrictive option was or why the court thought it
> insufficient. Weakest row.
> **Change:** find the paragraph of the reasons that deals with the
> alternative, and answer it in its own terms, in the revision period.
>
> That entry is worth more than "looks fine" written against all five
> rows, and it took less time.

## The civics version of a generous mark

Three rows in this course are the ones people mark themselves generously
on, every term:

- **"Sourced."** A source is not attached because you read it. It is
  attached because a reader could find the exact thing you are relying on
  in under a minute. Check one at random and see how long it takes you.
- **"The other side, at its strongest."** Read your opposing paragraph
  aloud and ask whether somebody who holds that position would say you
  had been fair, or would say you had picked their weakest supporter.
- **"Specific about who holds power."** Search your own draft for the
  phrase *the government*. Each time it appears, either name the order,
  the body and the position, or delete the sentence.

## Where this fits

Your judgement of your own work is not part of your mark, and neither is
a classmate's — see [[How Marks Work]]. That is deliberate, and it cuts
both ways: nothing you write here can cost you anything, which is exactly
what makes it worth writing honestly.

What it changes is the work. A self-check that leads to one real fix is
the cheapest mark improvement available in a ten-week course, and it is
the same move you will make on the examination when you reread an answer
with twenty minutes left.

One last thing worth doing: read your judgement to the person beside you.
It catches the rows where you were being kind to yourself, and you will
usually hear it yourself before they say anything.
