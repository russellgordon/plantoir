Six poems, read aloud, spread across the semester rather than blocked
into a unit — because a poem beside a play or a novel does more than a
poem beside five other poems.

| Poem | Poet | Read beside |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sonnet 130 ("My mistress' eyes…") | William Shakespeare | *Romeo and Juliet*, as an antidote to its own imagery |
| "Letter to Sir John A. Macdonald" | Marilyn Dumont (Métis) | Perspective, beside the novel: whose account survives |
| "The Bull Moose" | Alden Nowlan | The short-story unit: an ending that reorganises the poem |
| "Blues Spiritual for Mammy Prater" | Dionne Brand | Style and diction: what plain words can carry, and how a photograph becomes an argument |
| "The Cariboo Horses" | Al Purdy | Setting as argument: a landscape that is not scenery |
| "I Am a Canadian" | Duke Redbird (Anishinaabe) | The media unit: identity as a claim rather than a fact |

## How we read a poem here

1. **Aloud, twice**, before anything is said about it.
2. **Annotate**: line breaks, the turn, the repeated sound.
3. **One claim each**, with a line as evidence.
4. **Read it aloud a third time**, by somebody with a different reading.

## What to look for

- **The turn.** Almost every poem changes direction; find the word.
- **The line break.** The word left hanging at the end of a line is being
  pointed at.
- **Sound.** Where the poem slows, and what it slows for.
- **The last line**, which most poems are built backwards from.

> [!note] Where these come from
> Brand's poem is from *No Language Is Neutral* (Coach House Press,
> 1990), written in response to a photograph of a formerly enslaved
> woman taken when she was well over a hundred. Purdy's title poem gave
> its name to the 1965 collection that won the Governor General's
> Literary Award. Dumont's is from *A Really Good Brown Girl* (1996).
> Redbird's was read to the Queen at the Silver Jubilee in 1977, which
> is worth knowing before you decide what its tone is doing.

## Sonnet 130, specifically

Shakespeare spends thirteen lines insisting his mistress is not like the
sun, coral, snow, or perfume — and then lands the fourteenth. Read it
next to Romeo's early speeches about Rosaline and ask which one the play
agrees with.
