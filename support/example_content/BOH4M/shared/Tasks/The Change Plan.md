**Due: the last day of Unit 3.** Individual. A plan of about 1,000 words.

You are given a change that has been decided. Your job is to plan how it
will actually happen.

## The change

One of: moving a 40-person office to a hybrid schedule; merging two
departments after an acquisition; introducing a new scheduling system in
a 24-hour operation; or closing one of three locations. Your teacher
assigns it.

The decision is not yours to argue with. How it is done is entirely
yours.

## What the plan must contain

1. **What is driving this**, and what will happen if nothing changes.
2. **Who is affected, and how** — by group, honestly, including who loses
   something.
3. **The acceptance strategies** you will use for each group, chosen from
   [[Leading Change]] with a reason for each.
4. **The announcement**: what is said, by whom, in what order, and what
   you will say about the rumour that is already circulating.
5. **The legal ground rules** you are working inside — see
   [[The Ground Rules of Change]]. Name the specific obligations, not
   "we'll follow the law".
6. **The control system**: what you will measure, when, and what result
   would make you slow down or stop. Say which kind of control each
   measure is.
7. **A timeline** with the first four weeks in detail.

## How it is marked

| Criterion | What earns the marks |
| --- | --- |
| Realism | A plan a manager could run, with dates and named responsibilities |
| Attention to resistance | Objections anticipated and answered, not dismissed |
| Legal literacy | Specific obligations identified and built in early |
| Control | Measures that would actually reveal a problem in time |

> [!warning] The plan that only communicates
> Most student change plans are an announcement plus optimism. If nothing
> in yours changes what people are asked to do, or gives anyone anything
> in exchange for what they lose, it is not a plan yet.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 3, Day 19, the twenty-minute working period at the end of
  that class, on the affected-groups table and the strategy for each
  group
  Watch for: the group whose loss cannot be compensated — the people
  losing the office they walk to, the shift that disappears. Does the
  student go to the Leading Change table and weigh two strategies for
  that group, or write the same word in every row and come back later?
  The plan carries a strategy AND a reason for every group, because the
  task asks for both, and that reason is your product evidence — this
  corroborates it rather than replacing it. What the reason column cannot
  tell you is whether it was reasoned or retrofitted: a reason written
  afterwards to justify a word already in the box reads exactly like one
  that chose the word. That is D4.3 (evaluate different strategies used
  by managers to bring about acceptance of planned change) — the verb is
  evaluate, and evaluating is a thing you can watch happen.
  Going well: two strategies written and one crossed out, with a reason
  in the margin.
  Stuck: the same strategy down the whole column, or the timeline being
  built before anyone has been listed.
  Record: a tick or a dash beside each name on the seating plan, on the
  same pass of the room as the notes.
  Do not watch for whether their control measures are checkable — that
  was the third item on this same day's agenda, so you would be marking
  them on the lesson they had just been given.

TALK — Unit 3, Day 19, while you go round leaving notes on the plans in
  that same period
  Everybody gets the written note; that is the checkpoint the class page
  promises. For eight or ten of them, ask the question while you are
  already at the desk — the students whose written work has told you
  least so far.
  Ask: "Which group's loss can you not compensate — and what did you
  decide to give them instead?" A strong answer has an honest answer:
  notice, a say in how it happens, first refusal on something else, or
  nothing but the truth said early. That is D4.3, and the written plan
  will not carry it, because a plan contains the objections that were
  answered rather than the one that could not be.
  Then: "If this organization had ignored the pressure for another two
  years, what would have forced the change instead?" A strong answer
  names a different element — a regulator, a retirement, a competitor —
  and shows that the driver was a pressure rather than a whim. That is
  D4.1 (analyse the elements that bring about change in an organization);
  the plan states the driver, so ask for the counterfactual, not the
  driver.
  Record: a letter beside each name you reach, on the day plan, right
  then, and a circle round the ones you did not.

The product evidence is the plan itself, handed in on Day 22 of Unit 3,
after the first fifteen minutes of Day 20 are spent making the change
your note named.
