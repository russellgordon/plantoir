> [!abstract] At a glance
> **Eden Robinson**, Haisla and Heiltsuk writer · *Monkey Beach* (2000) ·
> set in Kitamaat, British Columbia · shortlisted for the Giller Prize
> and the Governor General's Literary Award; winner of the Ethel Wilson
> Fiction Prize

> [!warning] What this text deals with
> Sexual violence, addiction, suicide, and the intergenerational effects
> of residential schools. It is also frequently funny, which is part of
> how it works.

## The premise

Lisamarie Hill's younger brother Jimmy is missing at sea. While her
parents fly north, she takes a boat up the coast herself — and the novel
moves between that journey and her childhood in Kitamaat: her
grandmother Ma-ma-oo, her uncle Mick, the b'gwus, and the things she can
see that other people cannot.

## What to notice

- **Two timelines, braided.** Present-tense journey, past-tense memory.
  Ask what the braid does that a chronological telling could not — see
  [[Structure and Time]].
- **A narrator looking back.** Two voices at once: the girl living it and
  the woman reconstructing it. [[Voice and Narration]] has the mechanics.
- **What is withheld.** The novel declines to explain several things,
  including to the reader. Treat the refusal as a decision.
- **Ma-ma-oo's teaching.** Knowledge given on terms, in a sequence, to
  somebody who has to earn it. Notice what it costs to receive it.
- **Humour beside grief**, often in the same paragraph.
- **Haisla words and place names**, and what they carry that English does
  not.

## Questions we will argue about

1. Is Lisamarie's ability a gift, an inheritance, or a burden? Does the
   novel let you separate them?
2. What is the novel doing with the b'gwus — and what would be lost by
   calling it a symbol?
3. Ma-ma-oo says knowledge has conditions. What are they, and what does
   the novel think of a reader who wants it without them?
4. The ending. Argued as vision, as death, and as neither. Which
   passages carry your reading?

## Read beside

[[The Inconvenient Indian]], in extracts — because King's argument about
who has told stories about Indigenous people in North America is the
context this novel is writing inside. See
