**The question:** does a wealthy country have obligations to poorer ones,
and if so, what discharges them — aid, trade, migration, or something
else?

Start with the number, because it is the one people guess wrong. Canada's
official development assistance in 2025 was USD 7.2 billion, or 0.32% of
gross national income, placing it ninth among OECD Development Assistance
Committee members by volume and sixteenth by share. The long-standing
target endorsed by donor countries is 0.7%. Across all DAC members
assistance fell 23.1% in real terms from 2024, to 0.26% of their combined
income.

## Four answers, and what each takes as evidence

| Position | The claim | What it points to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Aid | Transfers work; the target is a commitment already made | Health and schooling gains where aid was sustained |
| Trade | Market access does more than transfers ever did | Countries that grew through exporting, not receiving |
| Migration | Letting people move is the largest transfer available | Remittance flows against total aid flows |
| Nothing owed | Obligations are to citizens; growth abroad is not ours to buy | Aid captured by governments rather than reaching people |

None of those is a fringe position and each has economists defending it.
Note that the first three are not exclusive, and the fourth is the only one
that is genuinely a values claim rather than an empirical one — which is
worth saying out loud during the seminar.

## The institutions and trade bodies, judged rather than listed

E3.2 asks you to assess intergovernmental responses — the United Nations
Development Programme, the World Health Organization, the International
Monetary Fund, the World Bank. E3.3 asks the same of social movements and
social justice organisations, from the International Labour Organization to
fair-trade, feminist, and anti–child-labour campaigns. E2.4 asks how
individuals and consumer groups attempt to address sweatshops, resource
exploitation, and hazardous working conditions. E1.4 asks how multilateral
trade agreements (WTO, regional trade pacts) and economic forums (G20)
shape trading opportunities and global rules.

E3.1 asks you to analyse the structural causes of global economic
disparities — colonial legacies, resource ownership, illiteracy, gender
inequality, and unequal market access.

Judge these institutions and responses by criteria you set first: reach,
durability, whether the recipients had any say, and what happened after the
programme ended. "Effective" with no criterion attached is not an
assessment.

## The discussion

1. Canada is short of the 0.7% target by more than half. Is that a broken
   promise, a reasonable revision, or a promise that should never have been
   made in that form?
2. Fair trade sets a price floor for producers. Use [[Supply and Demand]]
   to say what a price floor does, then say why anyone would want one
   anyway.
3. Take a Canadian trade decision — a tariff, a quota, a marketing board —
   and identify who outside Canada bears its cost.
4. Land it: name one obligation you think is real and one you think is
   not, and give the reason in each case.

Bring what [[Inequality]] established about measuring disparity, and what
[[Comparative Advantage]] said about trade theories and who captures the
gains from trade.
