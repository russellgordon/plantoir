At the boards, someone plots a rectangle, checks that its diagonals cut
each other exactly in half, and announces "proved it". Have they? They
have *verified* one rectangle — honest work, and the grid tools from
[[Properties on the Grid]] make it rigorous work — but the claim was
about every rectangle that ever was. Between "it worked for my
example" and "it must be so" runs the deepest gap in mathematics, and
it is strangely lopsided: a thousand confirming examples cannot close a
conjecture, yet a single counterexample kills it on the spot. Learning
to feel that asymmetry — and to want the "must", not just the "did" —
is what this conversation is for.

Questions worth arguing about:

1. What exactly has the rectangle-checker shown, and what has she not?
   Is her verification worthless, or is it evidence of *something*?
2. Where does the asymmetry come from — why does one counterexample
   outweigh a thousand confirmations? Say it in your own words until
   your group accepts them.
3. When is checking a specific case genuinely strong reasoning? (Hint:
   you verify solutions in every practice set. Is that the same move
   or a different one?)
4. What actually convinces *you* — algebra, a picture, a pattern that
   never breaks? Are you convincible by things that should not
   convince you, and how would you know?
5. In [[Always, Sometimes, Never]], the whole game is deciding which
   bin a statement lives in. What does "sometimes" demand as evidence
   that "always" and "never" do not?

This stops being talk at [[The Quadrilateral Case File]], where your
group prosecutes a geometric property with coordinates as sworn
testimony — midpoints, lengths, and slopes arguing that a thing is not
just observed, but inevitable.
