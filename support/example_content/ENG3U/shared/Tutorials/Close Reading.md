Close reading is reading slowly enough to notice the choices. At this
level it is not an activity we sometimes do; it is what every assessment
in this course is measuring.

## Annotating

Four marks, used consistently, beat a page of highlighter:

| Mark | Means |
| --- | --- |
| `?` | I do not understand this |
| `!` | This surprised me |
| `↺` | Connects to something earlier |
| `✎` | The writing itself is doing something |

Beside `✎`, write four words on *what* it is doing. Those four words are
the seed of a paragraph.

## The three passes

1. **Read for what happens.** No pen.
2. **Read with a pen**, using the marks. Slower, and this is the pass
   that does the work.
3. **Read only your marks.** Two minutes. The pattern they make is your
   thesis.

## What to notice in prose

- A word too strong or too weak for the moment.
- A repeated image — nothing appears three times by accident.
- Where time speeds up or slows down. Four pages on one afternoon means
  the afternoon is the point.
- What is not said, and by whom.
- Sentence rhythm: a short sentence among long ones is a raised voice.

## What to notice in drama

- Who has the long speeches, and when that changes.
- Who is alone on stage, and what the play lets them admit there.
- Who interrupts whom.
- Asides and soliloquies: the audience is being told something the other
  characters are not. Ask why *now*.
- Stage directions, including the implied ones inside the dialogue.

## What to notice in media

- Framing, colour, editing pace, sound, and what is absent. The full list
  is on [[Reading Non-Fiction]].

> [!example] From a mark to a paragraph
> Mark: `✎ Macbeth says "done" four times in six lines.`
> Paragraph seed: Macbeth's first soliloquy circles the word *done* —
> "if it were done when 'tis done" — and the repetition is a man trying
> to make an act into a completed thing before he has committed it. The
> play's whole argument about consequence is in that failed attempt at a
> past tense.
