> [!abstract] At a glance
> Individual · five class periods · 1,000 words · assessed on Thinking,
> Knowledge, and Communication

## The task

Read one text from [[Short Texts and Essays]] through **two** critical
approaches, and say what each one makes visible and what it costs.

Not "here is a feminist reading, here is a Marxist reading". One
argument, in which the second lens complicates the first.

## What it must contain

1. **A claim about the text** — not about the theory.
2. **The first reading**, argued from passages.
3. **The second lens applied to the same passages**, showing something
   the first could not see.
4. **A paragraph on the cost**: what each approach has to ignore.
5. **A conclusion that says which reading you would defend and why** —
   "both equally" is available only if you can show why the text requires
   it.

## How it is built

| Period | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Text chosen and reread without a lens; the first lens applied to the passages |
| 2 | The second lens applied to the same passages; conference with me |
| 3 | Drafting period |
| 4 | The criteria below read together against a paragraph nobody in the room wrote; then the workshop, a partner marking anywhere theory replaced evidence; then your own judgement, and the weakest section rewritten in class |
| 5 | Handed in |

## How it is marked

| Category | What I am looking for |
| --- | --- |
| Knowledge | Both approaches understood well enough to be used rather than named |
| Thinking | The second lens genuinely complicates the first; the cost paragraph is honest |
| Communication | A reader who knows neither approach can still follow the argument |

> [!warning] The failure this task exists to prevent
> The essay that could have been written without the text. If your
> paragraphs work with the quotations removed, you have written about
> criticism rather than about literature — see [[Using a Critical Lens]].

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 1, Day 11, the period where the text and the first lens
are chosen.
  Watch for: the ORDER. Some students read the passages again, mark
  something, and then reach for an approach that answers what they
  marked. Others open the critical approaches page first and then hunt
  the story for a paragraph that will sit still under it.
  This one does surface in the essay eventually — the warning callout on
  this page is about exactly that failure — but not until the draft
  exists on Day 13. Watching on Day 11 buys you two periods in which it
  is still cheap to redirect somebody.
  Going well: the text is the open book, and the lens is named after a
  passage has been marked.
  Stuck: the approaches page is open, the story is shut, and the student
  is looking for permission.
  Record: a class list, one letter each — T for text-first, L for
  lens-first. Thirty seconds while they pack up.
  Evidence for C1.2: exploring and focusing ideas for a piece of writing
  is a set of strategies, and the strategy is only visible here.

TALK — Unit 1, Day 12, the conference already on that agenda.
  Ask: "Your two lenses are going to disagree somewhere in this text.
  Before you have written any of it — point at the passage where you
  think that will happen." Then: "And what would somebody using only the
  first lens say about that passage?"
  A strong answer picks a passage rather than a theme, and says what in
  it — a shift in who is being addressed, an image that recurs, a tense
  that will not settle — is going to make the two readings pull apart.
  That is B1.6 in conversation, and it is audible three periods before
  the essay could show it. A student who cannot predict the collision
  has not applied the second lens yet, whatever the outline says.
  Deliberately not asked: the question printed on Day 12's agenda, and
  "which approach did the text hand you", which they answered in their
  journal on Day 4.
  Record: one line per student on the same class list, right then.

The product arrives on its own: the essay handed in on Day 15.
