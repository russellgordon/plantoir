The last substantial class of the course. The devices come out on the
benches, the investigations go up on the walls, and everybody explains
their work to people who are going to ask about it.

This is not a presentation night with an audience that claps. It is the
part where somebody who was not at your bench asks why you did it that
way, and you answer.

Together with the [[Final Examination]] it is the final thirty per cent
of your mark — [[How Marks Work]] explains the split. Everything on it is
judged individually: your five minutes, your two minutes, your two
panels, and the questions you ask. Your partner's device does not carry
you and it cannot sink you.

It is launched on the same day as [[The Optics Design]], because the two
panels are chosen while you build rather than the night before. They are
then researched and written in class, in the first thirty-five minutes of
Unit 4, Day 16, and handed in on paper.

## The situation

You have spent a semester producing work that only you fully
understand. Today that stops. The room is arranged so that everybody
presents and everybody questions, and the two halves are marked
together, because explaining and defending are the same skill seen from
two sides.

**We run it in rounds.** One person at each bench presents at a time,
five minutes, while everybody else circulates; then we rotate. Six
benches means six people talking at once, so the whole room gets through
its five minutes and still hears five other people.

It also means I am standing in one group at a time. Four of the seven
rows below are **heard** — explaining, language, honesty about results,
answering questions — and I hear them from wherever I am in the rotation.
That is a sample and I am not going to pretend otherwise. The other three
rows arrive **on paper** and are marked cold, away from the noise: the two
panels, handed in at the start of the period, and your question sheet,
handed in at the end.

Almost nothing is built for this: the device and the investigation are
already yours, so preparing them is **selection and explanation**, not
production. The two panels are the exception — they are genuinely new
work, which is why they get a working period on Unit 4, Day 16 rather
than a line of homework.

## What you present

**1. Your device from [[The Optics Design]]**, working if it works and
explained honestly if it does not. Five minutes, spoken, with the
device in your hands rather than a slide of it. Cover: the problem, the
prediction, what you measured, and where the two disagreed.

**2. One earlier investigation or task** of your choosing, in two
minutes. Choose the one where your thinking changed most, not the one
that went most smoothly. A result that surprised you and made you go
back is a better two minutes than a result that came out as expected.

## What you hand in

**One page, at the start of the period, carrying both panels.** These are
written in class on Unit 4, Day 16 and marked from the page rather than
from anything you say — which is what makes them the two rows nobody has
to be lucky to be heard for.

**1. A career panel.** One role connected to the science in your work.
The curriculum's own examples are meteorologist, medical illustrator,
geochemist, and optical physicist, and yours might be an optician, an
optical technician, a camera operator, a lighting designer, an
environmental technologist, a lab technologist. What does the work
actually involve day to day, and what education and training does it
take to get there?

**2. A scientist panel.** One person who contributed to a field this
course touched. The curriculum document offers **Sheela Basrur**,
**William Richard Peltier**, **Alice Wilson**, and **Willard Doyle** as
starting points, and you are free to choose someone else. Find out what
they actually did, from a source that says where its information came
from — a name is not a summary, and a one-line label copied from
somewhere is worth nothing here.

## The questioning

Every presenter answers **at least two questions**, and every student
asks **at least two questions** of other people. Both are marked, and
they are gathered differently.

Answering is heard: I mark it from the round I am standing in. Asking is
read. **Carry a question sheet** — at every bench you visit, write down
the question you asked and, in a line, what came back. Hand it in at the
end. That sheet is the only way a question you asked at a bench I was
nowhere near can count for you, and it is the reason this row does not
depend on where I happened to be standing.

> [!important] "I don't know" is a good answer when it is finished
> Nobody expects you to have an answer to everything about your own
> work, and pretending is the only thing that will actually cost you.
>
> A weak answer: "Um, I'm not sure."
>
> A strong answer: "I don't know. My data can't distinguish those two
> explanations, because we only ran it at one object distance. If I
> repeated it, I would vary that and see whether the disagreement
> tracked it."
>
> Same three words at the front. The difference is that the second one
> says **what** you do not know, **why** your evidence cannot settle
> it, and **what would**. That answer earns more than a confident
> guess, every single time.

Good questions to ask other people, if you are stuck for one:

- What would have made you abandon your prediction?
- What is the weakest part of your evidence?
- What did you control, and what could you not control?
- If you had one more class, what would you measure?
- What would somebody who disagreed with your conclusion say first?

## How it will be judged

| What I am looking for | Level 3 sounds like | Level 4 sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Explaining the work | Clear, accurate, in the right order | Pitched at whoever is standing there, and it lands with all of them |
| Language | Uses the correct terms | Uses the correct terms **and** can restate any of them plainly on request |
| Honesty about results | Reports the disagreement | Reports it, quantifies it, and offers a mechanism for it |
| Answering questions | Answers accurately | Distinguishes what is settled from what is not, and says which is which |
| Asking questions | Two relevant questions on your sheet, with what came back | Questions that made a presenter change what they said next, rather than questions with known answers |
| Career panel | On the page: a real role, correctly described | What the work is actually like day to day, and the specific route that leads to it |
| Scientist panel | On the page: a real contribution, correctly attributed, with the source named | The contribution explained well enough that a reader can see why it mattered |

## What sinks an otherwise good showcase

- **Reading from a page.** You did the work; you already know it. Notes
  on a card for the numbers are fine, a script is not.
- **Hiding the part that did not work.** It is the most interesting
  thing on your bench and everybody in the room has one.
- **A career or scientist panel copied from the first search result**,
  with no source and no sign you understood it.
- **Asking a question you already know the answer to**, to get the mark
  out of the way. It is obvious, and it wastes the presenter's turn.
- **Vanishing during the questioning half.** One of the seven rows above
  is earned entirely on that side of the room, and it is the cheapest of
  the seven.

In the last twenty minutes of today, write [[Final Reflection]] in your
[[Science Journal]] and hand it in before the bell. It is the entry I read
first.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

On the day itself you are running the room, so neither prompt below points
at it. Both live in the preparation period, which is the only time this
task is visible while it is still being made.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 16, the thirty-five-minute writing period
  Watch for: the career that gets ABANDONED. Almost everybody starts with a
  role they like the sound of, discovers they cannot find out what
  qualification it actually needs, and then does one of two things. The
  finished paragraph shows you only the role they ended up with; it never
  shows you the one they gave up on, or what they did at the moment they
  gave up.
  Going well: a student says out loud that they cannot find the training
  route, and changes to a role they can source — often a narrower, more
  local one, which is usually a better paragraph anyway.
  Stuck: a training route appears in the notes with nothing open behind it,
  or "four years of university" written against a role that is a college
  diploma.
  Record: three columns on your seating plan — sourced it, switched and
  sourced it, or has a route with no page behind it. One circuit twenty
  minutes in. That third column is A2.1's second half, the education and
  training, which is the part of that expectation a confident paragraph
  hides best.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 16, after the panels go down, while they choose
  their second piece
  You are moving between desks anyway. Do not ask which investigation
  changed their thinking most — the task page asks exactly that, so the
  answer is already written.
  Pick ONE of the two below per student — the window is short and you will
  not get both from everybody. Start with the students you have
  least conversation evidence for.
  Ask: "Say your device's problem to me in one sentence, as if I were the
  person who has the problem and not a science teacher." Then, when they
  have: "Now say the same sentence to somebody who builds these for a
  living. What changed?"
  Or: "Your scientist — what did people in that field believe before them,
  and what did they have to stop saying afterwards?"
  A strong answer to the pair changes the vocabulary and keeps the claim
  identical, which is A1.11 heard: appropriate language for the audience, in
  a course where every other piece of evidence for it is written. A weak one
  changes the claim, usually by making it larger for the expert. A strong
  answer to the alternative names something the field actually stopped
  saying, which is the difference between identifying a contribution under
  A2.2 and copying a label.
  Record: one tick per student on the seating plan, and a star against
  anybody whose spoken version was better than their written one — they are
  the students the showcase will surprise you with.

The product evidence is the presentation and the questions themselves, at
Unit 4, Day 17. That one arrives on its own, and it arrives once.
