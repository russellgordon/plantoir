Ten minutes ago you were moving. Somewhere in that there were choices — where
your weight went, how fast you got there, whether your arm carried the whole
body with it or travelled alone. Most of them you made without naming them. The
elements of dance are the names: five questions that are already being answered
every second you move, whether you are answering them on purpose or not.

## The five elements

| Element | The question it answers |
| --- | --- |
| Body | What is moving — all of it, or one shoulder? What shape is it holding? |
| Space | Where does it go — high or low, direct or curving, near you or far? |
| Time | How fast, how long, with what pulse — or deliberately against one? |
| Energy | *How* is it done — sharp, sustained, heavy, suspended, collapsing? |
| Relationship | Who or what is it with — a partner, the group, the floor, an object? |

Only one of those is about *what* you do. The other four are about how, where,
when, and with whom — which is why two dancers performing the same step can
look like they are doing entirely different things.

## They move together

The elements are not five separate dials. Turn one and the others shift. Take a
simple reach and slow it down, and the energy changes on its own — sustained
movement is almost impossible to do quickly. Drop that reach to floor level and
the relationship changes too, because now you are dealing with the ground.

This is the most useful thing about the vocabulary. When a phrase is dull,
"make it better" is not an instruction anyone can follow. "Keep the movement
exactly as it is and change only the timing" is. You will hear that kind of
instruction all semester, and it works because the elements can be isolated.

## Where you will use them

Three ways: to *generate* movement when you have none, to *describe* what you
or someone else did, and to *revise* a phrase that is not working yet. All
three arrive at once — in [[Phrase Building]], in [[Your Movement Journal]],
and in [[The Movement Signature]], where the solo has to be recognisably yours.

Get comfortable saying these five words out loud. A course where everyone can
only say "it looked good" is a course where nobody can help anybody.
