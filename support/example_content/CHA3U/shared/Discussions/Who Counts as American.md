**The question:** Citizenship is a legal status, a set of rights in
practice, and a claim about belonging — and in American history the three
have rarely lined up. Who has counted as American, on whose definition, and
what changed after 1945?

Note that the question has two halves that pull apart. One is answerable
from statute and case law. The other is answerable only from what people
said, sang, printed and did, and the seminar works best when the room keeps
noticing which half a given argument belongs to.

## Prepare

Bring **one legal fact and one lived one**, sourced. The legal fact might be
an immigration statute, a court decision, a constitutional amendment, or the
terms of a wartime order. The lived one might be testimony, a photograph, an
organisation's founding document, a song, or an advertisement — anything
that shows belonging being asserted or refused.

> [!question]- A place to start if you are stuck (click to expand)
> Take one exclusion and one inclusion from the same decade. The National
> Archives records that Congress repealed the Chinese exclusion acts in
> 1943, "when China was a member of the Allied Nations during World War II,"
> and left a yearly limit of 105 Chinese immigrants; the same war saw some
> 122,000 people of Japanese ancestry removed under Executive Order 9066,
> nearly 70,000 of them American citizens. Two decisions, one war,
> opposite directions. Explaining how both were possible at once is a
> complete seminar contribution on its own.

## Three positions

**The circle widened.** Citizenship rights were extended by amendment,
statute and decision across the twentieth century, and the direction of
travel is real and documented. On this account exclusion is the history and
inclusion is the trajectory.

**The line moved rather than widened.** Each extension has been accompanied
by a new category of people held outside it — by status, by security
classification, by the kind of work they do. What changes is who occupies
the position, not that the position exists.

**Belonging was never the law's to grant.** Groups asserted membership
through churches, unions, newspapers, service, and culture long before any
statute recognised it, and the legal record is the trailing indicator. This
position needs the sources from [[Arts, Culture, and Consumer Society]] as
much as from [[Government Records]].

This is the last of [[The Four Seminars]], and the one that counts most.

Open with your position and name the evidence that would move you. Comparing
with Canada is welcome and must be evidenced, not flattering — see
[[Our Working Agreement]]. The argument feeds [[The Long Argument]].
