A goal that lives only in your head is a wish. The difference is not
motivation — it is that a written goal can be checked, and a wish
cannot. This page is about writing them so that checking is possible,
and about revising them without treating revision as failure.

## What a usable goal contains

Four parts, and it is short:

1. **The thing.** Specific enough that someone else could tell whether
   it happened. "Get better at math" fails this. "Hand in every math
   assignment on the day it is due" passes.
2. **The by-when.** A date, not "this term".
3. **The first step.** The one action you can take this week.
4. **The reason.** Why it matters to you, in your own words. This is the
   part that gets you back to it weeks later.

## Short, medium, and long

Keep all three, because they do different jobs. A long-term goal points;
a medium-term goal is what you can actually plan; a short-term goal is
what tells you this week whether the plan is real. A long-term goal on
its own produces a good feeling and no evidence.

## Revising is the job, not the failure

The curriculum asks you to document how your goals change. That wording
is deliberate. Goals change because you learn things — that a subject is
harder or more interesting than you thought, that a pathway you dismissed
suits you, that the job you wanted involves a part you would hate.
Changing a goal for those reasons is the process working.

Changing a goal because it got difficult in week three is a different
thing, and you can usually tell which one you are doing by asking what
new information you have. If there is none, you have not revised a goal;
you have quit one. Both are allowed. Only one should be written down as
a revision.

## Keeping the record

Write each goal once, then add dated notes underneath it — what you
tried, what got in the way, what you changed. Three lines a fortnight is
enough. When [[Your Plan So Far]] asks what you have learned about your
own goal setting, that record is the answer, and it cannot be
reconstructed from memory.

> [!warning] Do not write goals you think you are supposed to have
> A goal chosen to look good to a teacher, a parent, or a friend will be
> abandoned quietly and you will feel bad about it, which is a poor
> return on the effort. If the honest version of your goal is "find out
> whether I actually want this", write that. It is a real goal and it
> can be worked on.
