By the end of [[The Five-Shot Sequence]] you had discovered the trap of
video: the single shot that looked great on location and turned out to
be unusable alone. Video is not shots. Video is *coverage* — a set of
shots that fit together — and you shoot it for the editor you will
become next week.

## Coverage: wide, medium, close

Each shot size does one job, and no size does the others:

- **Wide** answers *where* — the whole gym, the crowd, the scale.
- **Medium** answers *who* — a person and what they are doing.
- **Close** answers *what it felt like* — hands, faces, the detail
  that carries the emotion.

A story told in one size is monotone; the cut from wide to close is
where video gets its rhythm. The classic drill packs all of this into
five deliberate shots of a single action:

> [!note]- The five shots, for reference
> Close-up on the hands doing the action; close-up on the face;
> wide shot placing person and action in the room; over-the-shoulder
> shot joining the two; and one more from anywhere unexpected — low,
> high, or through something.

## Shooting to edit

Hold every shot steady for a slow count of ten — tuck your elbows,
brace against a wall, let the action move through a still frame rather
than chasing it. Ten seconds feels absurd while recording and barely
enough while editing, because the edit is where these decisions cash
out: [[Cut a Highlight Reel]] is only possible if the game was covered
in sizes, with clean beginnings and ends to cut on.

Shooting to edit means collecting for a sequence, not hunting one
perfect shot. Before leaving any location, ask what the edit will beg
for tomorrow — a wide to establish, a close for the turning point, a
[[Record a Standup|standup]] to carry the explanation — and get it now,
while the location still exists. And remember that half of every shot
is sound, which has its own honest rules in [[Clean Audio]].
