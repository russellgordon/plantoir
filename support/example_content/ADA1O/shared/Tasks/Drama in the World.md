> [!abstract] At a glance
> Pairs · launched on Unit 3, Day 9 and presented on Day 18 or 19 · four
> working periods · one form, where it came from, and five minutes in
> which you teach it to us by making us do it

## What you are making

A **teaching presentation** about one drama form or tradition: where it
came from, what it was for, and what it can still do. The rule that
makes this drama rather than a report — **we have to be on our feet for
at least two of your five minutes.** You teach the form by running the
room in it.

## Choosing your form

From [[Where Drama Comes From]], or another you can research properly:
the Greek chorus, commedia dell'arte, mask work, Noh, Kabuki, shadow
puppetry, melodrama, physical theatre, verbatim theatre, forum theatre,
puppetry in contemporary Canadian companies, or a form from your own
community's traditions. Register your choice with me — two pairs may not
take the same one.

If your form belongs to a living cultural tradition, particularly an
Indigenous one, the standard is higher, not lower: use sources from
people inside that tradition, name them, and present the form as
somebody's living practice rather than a technique to borrow. If you
cannot find those sources, choose another form. That is not a failure —
it is the correct decision, and it is worth saying so in your
presentation.

## What must be in it

- **Where and when it comes from**, in a sentence a Grade 9 audience
  follows.
- **What problem it solved** for the people who made it — the audience,
  the space, the occasion, what the community needed.
- **One thing it does that our other tools cannot.**
- **Two minutes of the room doing it**: a short exercise, an image, a
  chorus, a mask walk. Plan it as you would a warm-up, with the
  instruction sentence written down in advance.
- **One contemporary trace**: a film, series, play, or performer where
  the form is still visible today. [[Drama and the World Right Now]] is
  the way of looking that finds these.
- **Your sources**, at least three, named where you use them. At least
  one must be more substantial than a summary page.

## The working periods

| Day | What it is for |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Choose and register the form; find your first two sources |
| 2 | Research: what it was for, and one contemporary trace |
| 3 | Design the two minutes we spend on our feet, and test it on one other pair |
| 4 | Rehearse the whole five minutes to time; fix what ran long |

## How this is assessed

> [!note] You present in a pair, and you are marked as yourself
> There is no shared mark. Split the five minutes into named halves and
> tell me who is taking which before you present — one of you carries the
> origins and the purpose, the other runs the two minutes on our feet, or
> whatever division suits your form. Name the sources you personally read
> where you use them. I mark your half and your sources.

| Quality | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Researched | Specific, sourced, and beyond one summary page |
| Understood, not recited | You can say what the form was FOR |
| Taught, not told | The room did it, and knew what to do |
| Connected | A contemporary trace we can actually go and watch |
| Respectful | Living traditions presented as somebody's practice, with sources from inside |
| Held to time | Five minutes, rehearsed, not discovered live |

## Reflect

A [[Drama Journal]] entry: what surprised you about why this form
existed, and which of our own class conventions you now see differently
because of it.
