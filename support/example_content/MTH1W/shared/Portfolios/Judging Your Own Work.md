Every task page publishes a table of success criteria before the work
starts. Most people read it once on launch day and never again — and
then hand in something that misses two rows they could have fixed in a
single period. This page is that period, spent properly.

The first time, we do it together on a piece of work that belongs to
nobody in the room, so the move is not new to you when the work being
judged is your own.

## The routine

1. Put the task's criteria table beside what you have made — the
   poster panel, the machine, the design, the story.
2. Go down the table one row at a time. For each row write **yes**,
   **partly**, or **not yet**, and one sentence of evidence: the step,
   the axis, the line of code, the thing a reader would actually see.
   A "yes" with no evidence is a hope.
3. Name your weakest row. Not the quickest to fix — the weakest. This
   is the whole exercise, and it is harder than any of the fixing,
   because it means saying out loud the thing you were hoping nobody
   would look at.
4. Write one sentence: what you will change, and in which period.
5. Change it — in what is left of this class, or in the next working
   period. A judgement you have no time to act on is just a worry, so
   this is never scheduled on a hand-in day.

> [!example]- One row, judged honestly
> From [[Design Under Constraints]]: *"Conversions shown — unit
> changes are visible steps, not silent leaps."*
>
> **Partly.** The millimetres-to-centimetres change is written out on
> the diagram. The supplier's sheet was in inches and I did that one
> in my head, so the 30.5 appears from nowhere. Weakest row, because
> it is also the number the whole costing sits on.
> **Change:** write the inch conversion in as its own step, and check
> the costing still works, in tomorrow's working period.
>
> That entry took two minutes and is worth more than "looks fine to
> me" written once against the whole table.

## Judging is not the same as marking

Nothing you write here becomes part of your mark, and neither does
anything a classmate says about your work — [[How Marks Work]] is
plain about that. It cuts both ways: an honest **not yet** cannot cost
you anything, which is exactly what makes it worth writing.

What it changes is the work. In this course the criteria are published
before you start precisely so you can do this, and a self-check that
leads to one real fix is the cheapest improvement available to you all
semester.

> [!tip] Say it to somebody
> Read your judgement of one row out loud to the person beside you.
> You will hear where you were being generous to yourself before they
> have time to say anything — the same way a wrong answer at the
> boards is usually caught by explaining it, not by staring at it.

Your [[Math Journal]] is where the pattern shows up over time: if the
same row is weak on three tasks running, that is not bad luck, that is
the thing to work on. [[Showing Growth]] is how you use that later.
