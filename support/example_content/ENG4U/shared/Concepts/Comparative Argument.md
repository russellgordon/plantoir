A comparative essay is not two essays with a bridge. It is one argument
that could not be made with either text alone.

## The test

Write your thesis. Now delete one text from it. If the claim survives,
you do not yet have a comparative argument — you have an essay about one
text with a second one visiting.

## Three shapes that work

| Shape | Looks like |
| --- | --- |
| **One question, two answers** | Both texts confront the same problem; the comparison is what each solution costs |
| **A claim about form** | What a play can do that a novel cannot, proved in both directions |
| **A development** | The later text answers, revises, or refuses the earlier one — only if you can show it |

## Organising it

**Point by point** beats **text by text** almost every time at this
level, because it forces the comparison into every paragraph rather than
into a summary at the end. Use text-by-text only when the two arguments
genuinely need separate development first — and say why in your outline.

## Making the comparison do work

Every comparative paragraph should end with a sentence that neither text
could have produced alone:

> Both texts make testimony conditional on a hostile audience — Hamlet on
> Horatio's survival, Offred on the tapes being found. The difference is
> who is imagined to be listening: Hamlet's audience is a friend, and
> Offred's is a scholar two centuries later who will find her funny.

## The trap

Similarity-hunting. Two texts both contain grief, family, and death; so
does everything. A comparison is worth writing when the two texts
*disagree* — about a method, a value, or what a reader is for.
