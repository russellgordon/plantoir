## What you are making

Your team measures three heights nobody can reach — the flagpole, the
gym ceiling, the tall tree by the fence — without leaving the ground.
The rule that makes it interesting: every height must be found by
**two independent methods**, and the report must say how closely the
two answers agree.

You finish with a **measurement report**: for each target, the two
methods, the two answers, and an error bracket — the interval of
heights that both methods can defend.

## How to work

1. Choose three targets, at least one whose base you cannot walk to —
   a tree behind a fence, a wall across the creek. That one is the
   whole reason the sine and cosine laws exist.
2. For each target, pick two genuinely independent methods. Shadows
   and [[Similar Triangles]] — the move from
   [[How Tall Is the Flagpole]] — is one. A clinometer angle and
   [[The Primary Trigonometric Ratios]] is another. Two clinometer
   readings from the same spot is one method counted twice.
3. For the unreachable base, take two stations a measured distance
   apart and use [[The Sine Law]] — or [[The Cosine Law]] when your
   known angle sits between two sides you have measured.
4. Measure, and record everything: distances, angles, eye height, and
   which numbers you estimated rather than measured.
5. Compute each height twice, drawing the triangle each time. Where
   the two answers disagree — they will — chase the reason: a coarse
   angle reading, tape sag, a slope you assumed was level ground.
6. Report each height as a bracket, not a verdict: "between 11.2 m
   and 12.1 m, and here is why the spread is that size."

The working periods are class time, and the planning conference is where
the thinking behind a measurement gets recorded, since the report shows
only the number — see [[How Marks Work]].

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like in your report |
| --- | --- |
| Independent methods | The two routes to each height share no readings |
| Visible triangles | Every calculation begins with a labelled diagram |
| Honest records | Estimates are flagged as estimates |
| A chased disagreement | Each gap between answers has a suspected cause |
| A defensible bracket | The interval follows from the measurements |
| The hard target handled | The unreachable base got a lawful method |

The team files one report; each of you is marked on your own. Your mark
comes from the target whose calculation you signed, the diagram you
drew, your journal entry, and what you tell me at the planning
conference when I ask each of you separately.

## Reflect

Each member writes a short journal entry: which of your measurements
do you trust least, and what would you change to tighten your widest
bracket? Real measurement is an argument with uncertainty — this task
is where you learn to win it.

> [!success]- If your two answers are far apart (click to expand)
> Good — that is the interesting case. A clean disagreement you can
> explain is worth more than a suspicious agreement you cannot. Check
> the units first, then the angle, then the assumed level ground.
