A shoreline is a place where you can watch geography happen. Water
moves material, and it does it at a speed you can measure in an
afternoon.

## What we do

Working in pairs along a marked stretch:

1. **Draw a profile.** A side-on sketch from the water's edge up to the
   vegetation, with rough measurements. Mark where the material changes.
2. **Sample the material.** At three points, record what the ground is
   made of — sand, gravel, cobble, bedrock, fill — and the size range.
3. **Look for evidence of movement.** Undercut banks, exposed roots,
   fallen trees, deposits behind obstacles, debris lines from high
   water.
4. **Record the setting.** Which way does this shore face? What is the
   fetch — the distance of open water the wind crosses to get here?
5. **Photograph with purpose.** Note where you stood and which way you
   faced. Otherwise it is a nice picture.

## What to write in the notebook

Two columns, always: **observation** on the left, **inference** on the
right. "Roots exposed 30 cm above current level" is on the left.
"Bank is retreating" is on the right.

> [!tip] Measure something, even roughly
> Paces are a measurement if you know your pace length. A photograph
> with your notebook in it for scale is a measurement. Precision is
> less important than having a number to compare against at the end of the course.

## The questions we come back to

- What process is working here — moving material, dropping it, or both?
- Where does the material come from, and where is it going?
- What has been built or placed here to stop it, and is it working?

We return to this shore in Unit 4 with a different question: not what
the water does, but who can reach the water, who uses it, and what it is
protected from. Bring today's notebook when we come back.
