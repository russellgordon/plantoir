Almost every question on this page is done with the same tool, so build
the habit now: **draw the ICE table before you do anything else**, even
when the question looks too easy to need one.

| | Reactant | Product |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **I**nitial | what you put in | usually 0 |
| **C**hange | $-x$ times its coefficient | $+x$ times its coefficient |
| **E**quilibrium | initial $-$ change | initial $+$ change |

The bottom row is the only one that goes into a $K$ expression. The top
row almost never does, and substituting initial concentrations into an
equilibrium expression is the single most common error in this unit.

Two conventions this course uses. **Equilibrium constants are quoted
without units.** And **every $K$ belongs to a temperature** — a constant
with no temperature attached is not a number you can use, which is the
first thing [[Reading an Equilibrium Table]] will tell you.

**1.** Define *homogeneous*, *closed system*, *reversible reaction*, and
*equilibrium concentration*. Then write the equilibrium expression for
each of the following, naming the constant in each case.
*(a)* $\ce{N2(g) + 3H2(g) <=> 2NH3(g)}$
*(b)* $\ce{CaCO3(s) <=> CaO(s) + CO2(g)}$
*(c)* $\ce{Ca(OH)2(s) <=> Ca^2+(aq) + 2OH-(aq)}$
*(d)* $\ce{CH3COOH(aq) + H2O(l) <=> CH3COO-(aq) + H3O+(aq)}$

> [!success]- Answer 1
> **Homogeneous**: every substance in the equilibrium is in the same
> phase. (a) is homogeneous; (b), (c) and (d) are not.
>
> **Closed system**: matter cannot enter or leave. Equilibrium can only
> be reached in one — an open beaker letting a gas escape never settles,
> because one product keeps being removed.
>
> **Reversible reaction**: one that proceeds in both directions under
> the same conditions, so that products re-form reactants as well as the
> other way round.
>
> **Equilibrium concentration**: the concentration of a substance once
> the forward and reverse rates have become equal and the concentrations
> have stopped changing. Not the concentration you started with.
>
> | | Expression | Constant |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | (a) | $K_c = \frac{[\ce{NH3}]^2}{[\ce{N2}][\ce{H2}]^3}$ | $K_{eq}$, or $K_p$ if written with partial pressures |
> | (b) | $K_c = [\ce{CO2}]$ | $K_{eq}$ |
> | (c) | $K_{sp} = [\ce{Ca^2+}][\ce{OH-}]^2$ | $K_{sp}$, the solubility product |
> | (d) | $K_a = \frac{[\ce{CH3COO-}][\ce{H3O+}]}{[\ce{CH3COOH}]}$ | $K_a$, the acid ionisation constant |
>
> **Two omissions that are deliberate rather than careless.**
>
> **Pure solids and pure liquids are left out.** In (b) both solids
> vanish from the expression and only the gas survives; in (c) the solid
> calcium hydroxide is absent; in (d) the water is absent. Their
> concentrations are fixed by their density rather than by how much
> there is, so they cannot change and there is nothing for them to
> contribute. Expression (b) makes a genuinely surprising prediction as
> a result: at a given temperature that system reaches the same carbon
> dioxide concentration regardless of how much solid is in the vessel.
>
> **Coefficients become exponents.** The 3 on hydrogen and the 2 on
> ammonia in (a), and the 2 on hydroxide in (c), are not decorative —
> forgetting the exponent on hydroxide is worth a factor of ten or more
> in a $K_{sp}$ calculation.

**2.** For $\ce{H2(g) + I2(g) <=> 2HI(g)}$
at a certain temperature, the equilibrium concentrations are
$[\ce{H2}] = 0.0400$ mol/L, $[\ce{I2}] = 0.0600$ mol/L, and
$[\ce{HI}] = 0.360$ mol/L. Calculate $K_c$ and say what its size tells
you.

> [!success]- Answer 2
> $K_c = \frac{[\ce{HI}]^2}{[\ce{H2}][\ce{I2}]} = \frac{(0.360)^2}{(0.0400)(0.0600)} = \frac{0.1296}{0.00240} = 54.0$
>
> **$K_c = 54.0$** at that temperature.
>
> **What the size means.** $K_c$ is comfortably greater than 1, so at
> equilibrium the products are favoured — there is considerably more
> hydrogen iodide than there is hydrogen or iodine. It is not, however,
> a huge number, so meaningful amounts of both reactants remain, and a
> tube of this mixture would be visibly purple with iodine vapour.
>
> **What the size does not mean.** It says nothing about how long the
> system took to get there. A reaction can have an enormous $K_c$ and
> take a decade, and this is the same separation of "which way" from
> "how fast" that Unit 3 was built around. Nothing about $K_c$ is a rate.
>
> Note that the exponent went on $[\ce{HI}]$ and not on the others,
> because only hydrogen iodide has a coefficient other than 1. Dropping
> that square gives 6.00 instead of 54.0, which is a ninefold error from
> one missing exponent.

**3.** 0.200 mol of $\ce{H2}$ and 0.200 mol of $\ce{I2}$ are
placed in a 1.00 L vessel and allowed to reach equilibrium. At
equilibrium, $[\ce{HI}] = 0.320$ mol/L. Calculate $K_c$.

> [!success]- Answer 3
> The vessel is 1.00 L, so moles and mol/L are numerically the same
> here. Do not let that become a habit — check the volume every time.
>
> **The ICE table**, for
> $\ce{H2(g) + I2(g) <=> 2HI(g)}$:
>
> | | $\ce{H2}$ | $\ce{I2}$ | $\ce{HI}$ |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | Initial | 0.200 | 0.200 | 0 |
> | Change | $-x$ | $-x$ | $+2x$ |
> | Equilibrium | $0.200 - x$ | $0.200 - x$ | $2x$ |
>
> **The change row carries the coefficients.** Two moles of $\ce{HI}$
> form for every one mole of $\ce{H2}$ consumed, so if $x$ is the
> amount of hydrogen consumed, $2x$ is the amount of hydrogen iodide
> formed.
>
> From the data, $2x = 0.320$, so $x = 0.160$ mol/L.
>
> $[\ce{H2}] = [\ce{I2}] = 0.200 - 0.160 = 0.040\ \text{mol/L}$
>
> $K_c = \frac{(0.320)^2}{(0.040)(0.040)} = \frac{0.1024}{0.0016} = 64.0$
>
> **$K_c = 64.0$.**
>
> **This is a different temperature from question 2**, and you can tell
> without being told: the same reaction has a different constant, and
> for a given reaction only temperature can do that. If a question ever
> gives you two different $K_c$ values for the same equation, something
> about the conditions has changed and it is worth saying so.
>
> The check that costs nothing: substitute your equilibrium row back
> into the expression, as done above. If it does not reproduce $K_c$,
> the arithmetic is wrong.

**4.** At the temperature of question 3, 0.500 mol/L of $\ce{H2}$ and
0.500 mol/L of $\ce{I2}$ are mixed. Find all three equilibrium
concentrations.

> [!success]- Answer 4
> | | $\ce{H2}$ | $\ce{I2}$ | $\ce{HI}$ |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | Initial | 0.500 | 0.500 | 0 |
> | Change | $-x$ | $-x$ | $+2x$ |
> | Equilibrium | $0.500 - x$ | $0.500 - x$ | $2x$ |
>
> $K_c = \frac{(2x)^2}{(0.500 - x)^2} = 64.0$
>
> **Both sides are perfect squares, so take the square root of the whole
> equation** rather than expanding it. This turns a quadratic into a
> linear equation and it is worth looking for every time the
> stoichiometry allows:
>
> $\frac{2x}{0.500 - x} = \sqrt{64.0} = 8.00$
>
> $2x = 8.00(0.500 - x) = 4.00 - 8.00x$
>
> $10.00x = 4.00 \quad \Rightarrow \quad x = 0.400\ \text{mol/L}$
>
> $\begin{aligned} [\ce{H2}] &= [\ce{I2}] = 0.500 - 0.400 = 0.100\ \text{mol/L} \\ [\ce{HI}] &= 2(0.400) = 0.800\ \text{mol/L} \end{aligned}$
>
> **Check:**
> $\frac{(0.800)^2}{(0.100)(0.100)} = \frac{0.640}{0.0100} = 64.0$ ✓
>
> **Take the positive root only.** The square root of 64.0 is $\pm 8.00$
> mathematically, and the negative root gives a negative $x$, meaning
> the reaction ran backwards from a starting mixture containing no
> product at all. That is impossible, so it is discarded — and
> discarding a mathematically valid root for a physical reason is
> something you should do explicitly rather than silently.
>
> **A sanity check on the size:** 80% of the starting material has been
> converted, which is what a $K_c$ of 64 ought to look like. If your
> answer had come out at $x = 0.05$, the small conversion would have
> contradicted the large constant.

**5.** For $\ce{N2O4(g) <=> 2NO2(g)}$,
$K_c = 4.66 \times 10^{-3}$ at a certain temperature. A vessel initially
contains $\ce{N2O4}$ at 0.100 mol/L. Find the equilibrium
concentrations. Try the small-$x$ approximation first and check whether
it is allowed.

> [!success]- Answer 5
> | | $\ce{N2O4}$ | $\ce{NO2}$ |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | Initial | 0.100 | 0 |
> | Change | $-x$ | $+2x$ |
> | Equilibrium | $0.100 - x$ | $2x$ |
>
> $K_c = \frac{(2x)^2}{0.100 - x} = \frac{4x^2}{0.100 - x} = 4.66 \times 10^{-3}$
>
> **Try the approximation.** $K_c$ is small, so very little reacts, so
> perhaps $0.100 - x \approx 0.100$:
>
> $4x^2 = (4.66 \times 10^{-3})(0.100) = 4.66 \times 10^{-4}$
>
> $x^2 = 1.165 \times 10^{-4} \quad \Rightarrow \quad x = 1.079 \times 10^{-2}$
>
> **Now apply the 5% test**, which is the step people skip:
>
> $\frac{1.079 \times 10^{-2}}{0.100} \times 100 = 10.8\%$
>
> **Over 5%, so the approximation is not allowed here.** Report that,
> discard the value, and solve properly.
>
> $4x^2 = (4.66 \times 10^{-3})(0.100 - x)$
>
> $4x^2 + (4.66 \times 10^{-3})x - 4.66 \times 10^{-4} = 0$
>
> With $a = 4$, $b = 4.66 \times 10^{-3}$, $c = -4.66 \times 10^{-4}$:
>
> $\begin{aligned} x &= \frac{-b + \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a} \\ &= \frac{-4.66 \times 10^{-3} + \sqrt{2.1716 \times 10^{-5} + 7.456 \times 10^{-3}}}{8} \\ &= \frac{-4.66 \times 10^{-3} + 8.6474 \times 10^{-2}}{8} = 1.0227 \times 10^{-2} \end{aligned}$
>
> $\begin{aligned} [\ce{N2O4}] &= 0.100 - 0.010227 = 0.0898\ \text{mol/L} \\ [\ce{NO2}] &= 2(0.010227) = 0.0205\ \text{mol/L} \end{aligned}$
>
> **Check:**
> $\frac{(0.0205)^2}{0.0898} = \frac{4.20 \times 10^{-4}}{0.0898} = 4.68 \times 10^{-3}$, which
> recovers $K_c$ to within rounding. ✓
>
> **How much did the approximation actually cost?** It gave
> $x = 0.01079$ against the true $0.01023$, an error of about 5.5% in
> $x$. Not catastrophic — and that is exactly why the test matters. An
> approximation that fails quietly, by an amount you would not notice
> without checking, is more dangerous than one that fails obviously.
>
> **Only the positive root is kept.** The negative root gives a negative
> concentration of $\ce{NO2}$, which does not exist.

**6.** For the system in question 5, a vessel is found to contain
$[\ce{N2O4}] = 0.050$ mol/L and $[\ce{NO2}] = 0.050$
mol/L. Is it at equilibrium? If not, which way will it move?

> [!success]- Answer 6
> Calculate the **reaction quotient** $Q$ — the same expression as
> $K_c$, evaluated at whatever concentrations the system happens to
> have:
>
> $Q = \frac{[\ce{NO2}]^2}{[\ce{N2O4}]} = \frac{(0.050)^2}{0.050} = \frac{0.0025}{0.050} = 0.050$
>
> Compare: $Q = 0.050$ and $K_c = 4.66 \times 10^{-3}$.
>
> $Q > K_c$, by about a factor of ten.
>
> **The system is not at equilibrium.** There is too much product
> relative to reactant for this constant, so the system moves to reduce
> the top of the fraction and increase the bottom — it shifts to the
> **left**, converting $\ce{NO2}$ back into
> $\ce{N2O4}$, until $Q$ falls to $4.66 \times 10^{-3}$.
>
> **How to reason it without memorising a rule:** $Q$ is where the
> system is and $K$ is where it is going. If $Q$ is too big, the
> fraction has to shrink, and a fraction shrinks when its numerator
> falls and its denominator rises — which is the reverse direction. If
> $Q < K$ the argument runs the other way and the system moves right.
> If $Q = K$ it is already there and nothing net happens.
>
> Visibly, the brown colour of this mixture will fade as it settles.

**7.** For
$\ce{N2(g) + 3H2(g) <=> 2NH3(g)}$,
the forward reaction is exothermic. For each change, state which way the
system shifts and whether $K_c$ changes.

> [!success]- Answer 7
> | Change | Shift | Does $K_c$ change? |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | Add $\ce{N2}$ | Right | **No** |
> | Remove $\ce{NH3}$ as it forms | Right | **No** |
> | Decrease the volume of the vessel | Right | **No** |
> | Raise the temperature | **Left** | **Yes — $K_c$ decreases** |
> | Add a catalyst | **No shift** | **No** |
> | Pump in argon at constant volume | **No shift** | **No** |
>
> **Adding $\ce{N2}$** makes $Q$ smaller than $K_c$ — the denominator
> grew — so the system moves right until $Q$ climbs back to the same
> $K_c$. It arrives at different concentrations satisfying the **same**
> constant.
>
> **Removing ammonia** is the industrial trick: the system chases an
> equilibrium it can never reach, and product keeps forming.
>
> **Decreasing the volume** raises every concentration, and the system
> relieves that by moving toward the side with fewer gas particles. Four
> moles of gas on the left, two on the right, so it shifts right.
>
> **Raising the temperature is the only one that changes $K_c$**, and
> this is the item that separates understanding from memorisation. Treat
> energy as a product for an exothermic reaction; adding heat therefore
> pushes the system left. But this shift is different in kind: the
> system does not return to the old $K_c$, because the constant itself
> has moved. **Concentration and volume changes move the system along a
> fixed $K$; only temperature moves $K$.**
>
> **A catalyst does nothing to the position.** It lowers the barrier for
> the forward and reverse reactions equally, so the two rates still
> become equal at exactly the same concentrations. The system arrives
> sooner and arrives in the same place.
>
> **Argon at constant volume changes nothing at all.** The total
> pressure rises, but none of the concentrations in the expression
> changed — the nitrogen, hydrogen and ammonia are still spread through
> the same volume in the same amounts. $Q$ is untouched, so there is
> nothing to correct. This is the case that catches people who have
> memorised "increasing the pressure shifts toward fewer moles" without
> asking *why* it does.

**8.** Four claims from a study group. Correct each.
*(a) "We added more nitrogen and got more ammonia, so $K_c$ went up."*
*(b) "The reaction has reached equilibrium, so the concentrations of
reactants and products are equal."*
*(c) "$K_c$ is very large, so this reaction will be fast."*
*(d) "At equilibrium the reaction has stopped."*

> [!success]- Answer 8
> **(a) The system moved; the constant did not.** Adding nitrogen
> increased the denominator, making $Q$ smaller than $K_c$, so the
> system responded by making ammonia until $Q$ climbed back to the
> **same** $K_c$. New concentrations, same constant.
>
> This is the most important distinction in the unit and it is worth
> stating as a rule: **for a given reaction, $K_c$ depends on
> temperature and on nothing else.** Not on concentration, not on
> pressure, not on volume, not on a catalyst, not on how much you
> started with.
>
> **(b) Equilibrium means the concentrations have stopped changing, not
> that they are equal.** Question 2 is the counterexample:
> $[\ce{HI}] = 0.360$ mol/L sits beside $[\ce{H2}] = 0.0400$
> mol/L, a factor of nine apart, at equilibrium. A system with
> $K_c = 10^{20}$ is at equilibrium with essentially nothing but
> product, and one with $K_c = 10^{-20}$ with essentially nothing but
> reactant.
>
> "Balanced" describes the **rates**, never the amounts.
>
> **(c) $K_c$ says nothing whatever about rate.** It compares the
> concentrations at the destination; it does not describe the journey. A
> reaction can have a huge $K_c$ and an activation energy so high that
> nothing measurable happens in a human lifetime — carbon in air is the
> standard example, and it is why paper does not spontaneously burn in
> an oxygen-rich room.
>
> The two questions are answered by two different quantities. $K_c$
> answers "how far"; $k$ and the activation energy answer "how fast".
> Nothing links them, and every conceptual question in this unit is
> checking whether you have kept them apart.
>
> **(d) Nothing has stopped except the *change*.** The forward and
> reverse reactions are both running, continuously, at equal rates, so
> every molecule consumed is replaced as fast as it disappears. That is
> what the word **dynamic** is doing in the name.
>
> The evidence is available and it is not subtle: disturb the system and
> it responds within moments, which a stopped system could not do. Put
> a labelled isotope into an equilibrium mixture and it appears on the
> other side of the equation shortly afterwards, with no net change in
> any concentration at all.
