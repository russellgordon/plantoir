Margaret Atwood is one of Canada's most decorated living writers — poet,
novelist, and essayist, and a two-time Booker Prize winner. "Happy
Endings" is a short piece of metafiction — fiction that is openly about
the mechanics of fiction — first published in her 1983 collection *Murder
in the Dark*.

## Why we're reading it

Every text so far in this unit has asked you to follow one story from
beginning to end. This one refuses to let you do that: it gives you the
same two characters and hands you several different versions of what
happens to them, labelled with letters instead of told as one continuous
plot. It's the strangest-looking piece on the page in this unit, and the
easiest one to enjoy once you see what it's doing.

## What to watch for on a first read

Read every version in order before you decide what you think of the whole
piece — the point doesn't arrive until you can compare them.

| What you'll notice | What it's doing |
|---|---|
| The versions get shorter as you go | The story is showing you how little the specific events actually matter |
| One version is treated as more "real" than the others | Atwood is making a claim about which stories our culture calls satisfying |
| Every version, however different, arrives at roughly the same place | This is the story's actual argument — read the last section carefully |

## After you've read it

Atwood is doing, in miniature and on purpose, something every story you've
read this unit does without announcing it: choosing which events matter
and which order to put them in. [[Conflict and Plot Structure]] — the
concept the class met back on Day 3 — is worth revisiting once you've
finished this text; "Happy Endings" is, among other things, an argument
about exactly what that concept describes.
