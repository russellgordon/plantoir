By the time [[Boys and Girls]] ends, the narrator has been told who she is —
and the story cares more about the gap between what she actually does and
what everyone around her decides that means, than it does about any single
event. This seminar is about naming exactly where that gap opens up, not
about retelling the plot back to each other.

Bring the story open somewhere near you. You'll want to point at specific
lines, not remember them from across the room.

## The questions

1. The narrator doesn't decide to become "a girl" in the way her father
   means it — something happens, and then she goes along with what everyone
   else makes of it afterward. Where, specifically, does that happen? Could
   you defend a different moment as the real turning point instead?
2. Her father's last line in the story — "She's only a girl" — doesn't
   surprise us by the time it arrives. What has the story already done to
   make sure we expect it?
3. She lets Flora go. Is that a choice, an accident, or something in
   between — and does the story actually want you to be sure which?
4. By the end, has the narrator changed, or has the way the people around
   her read her changed? Those aren't the same question, and the story
   might have two different answers to them.

A strong answer to question 1 doesn't just point at the horse-gate scene and
stop there. It says what makes that moment different from the earlier ones —
the fox pelts, the "Robert Louis Stevenson" story she narrates about
herself, being sent inside to help her mother — and argues why THIS is the
moment the story treats as the hinge, using a line from the text as the
evidence, not just a summary of what happens.

Munro tells almost the whole story from close inside the narrator's own
head, using the technique we named in [[Point of View and Narrative Distance]].[^1]
That's exactly why question 4 is worth arguing about: you're
getting her account of what happened to her, told by someone who already
knows how the story ends.

[^1]: Narrative distance is how close or how far a third-person narrator
    sits from a character's own thoughts. Munro keeps that distance almost
    closed for most of the story, which is part of why the final line lands
    the way it does — it isn't only the father's judgement you're hearing.
    It's the narrator, grown, choosing to end her own story on it.
