A ten-year-old washing machine gets fixed with a wrench; a
three-year-old laptop with a tired battery often gets replaced,
because the battery is glued in, the parts are not sold, and the
manual does not exist. None of that is an accident of physics —
thin, sealed, and unrepairable are design choices, and choices
have beneficiaries. Right-to-repair advocates argue that owning a
thing should mean access to the parts, tools, and information to
fix it; manufacturers answer with safety, security, and quality.
Both sides claim to be on your side — which is exactly when
evidence matters most.

Questions worth arguing about:

1. Glue makes devices thinner and water-resistant *and* nearly
   unrepairable. Where is the line between designing for the user
   and designing against the owner — and who should draw it?
2. The repair quote is $250 on a $500 device, so replacing feels
   rational. Who arranged the prices that make it feel that way,
   and what do those prices leave out?
3. Manufacturers say independent repair risks safety and
   security. Sometimes that is even true. What evidence would
   separate the honest version of the claim from the convenient
   one?
4. Repair cafés, refurbishers, and certified recyclers already
   exist in most Ontario communities. Why are they busier with
   volunteers than with customers?
5. If repair became easy and normal again, who wins, who loses,
   and what happens to the price of new hardware? Argue the
   manufacturer's side first — honestly.

This argument gets a practical test: [[The Refurb Report]] hands
you a machine somebody gave up on and asks what a second life
would take. Argue here first, so your report knows what it
believes.
