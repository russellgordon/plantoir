By the middle of the twentieth century the United States was exporting its
image as successfully as its goods, and the two were the same product. A
film, a song, a car, a franchise and an advertisement are all documents
about what a society wanted to believe it was — and, once they travel, about
what other countries received.

The curriculum treats this as a matter of identity and heritage, not
decoration: it asks you to analyse the role of consumer culture "in the
construction of identity," and the impact of American arts and popular
culture "both in the United States and internationally."

## Three different objects, three different readings

**A work of art** is evidence of what one person made and what an audience
would accept. Read it for its maker and its moment, then for its reception,
which is a separate body of evidence: reviews, sales, bans.

**A product** is evidence of what could be manufactured, priced and
distributed. Its meaning is added later, by advertising, and the addition is
the interesting part.

**An advertisement** is the clearest source in this course, because it
states outright what its makers believed people wanted to be. It is almost
worthless as evidence that they were.

## Whose culture, and who was paid for it

The question that keeps this from becoming a list of favourites: who made
the work, who owned it, and who was allowed into the room where it was
recorded, published, or screened? A national culture that a segregated
industry produced is still a national culture, and saying so precisely is
better history than either celebrating it or dismissing it.

> [!success]- The move that raises a mark here (click to expand)
> Weak answers describe a film, a song or a brand and assert that it
> "reflected the times." Strong answers name a specific audience and a
> specific effect, and then find the disagreement: someone at the time
> objected to this work, in print, and their objection is a source. Set the
> work, its promotion, and one contemporary complaint side by side, and you
> have an argument instead of an appreciation. [[Using Evidence]] shows how
> to spend all three inside a single paragraph.

## Culture abroad is foreign policy by other means

When a country's films, brands and music arrive somewhere else, they carry a
version of that country with them, and the receiving society argues about it
— including in Canada, and including now. That argument is historical
evidence too, and it belongs in [[Is the United States an Empire|Is the United States an Empire?]] as much
as in any account of the arts.

Trace how the retelling changed in [[Interpretation and Historiography]],
then put the whole question to work in [[Whose Story Gets Taught|Whose Story Gets Taught?]].
