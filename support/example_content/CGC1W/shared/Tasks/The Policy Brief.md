**Due: the last day of Unit 3.** Individual. One page, plus sources —
and ninety seconds on your feet.

A brief written to someone who can actually decide something, about one
effect of Canada's changing population.

## The situation

Choose a real, current pressure: an aging population in a small town, a
school board with falling or rising enrolment, housing in a growing
region, health services in a remote community, settlement services for
new arrivals, or a workforce shortage in a particular sector.

## The shape of a brief

```
To:      the person or body who can decide
Subject: the decision, not the topic

The situation — three sentences, with the numbers.
What is causing it — the demographic change underneath.
If nothing is done — one sentence, with a horizon on it.
Options — two or three, each with a cost and a drawback.
Recommendation — one, and what it does not fix.
```

One page. A brief that runs to three is a report, and it will not be
read by the person you wrote it for.

## The argument

Everyone hands the brief in on the same day. Then you put your
recommendation to the room in ninety seconds, without the page in front
of you, and take one objection — half the room on the hand-in day, half
the class after. Ninety seconds because twenty-five arguments do not fit
in one period, and because a recommendation you cannot say in ninety
seconds is not decidable yet. That is part
of what is marked — it is the one place in this course where a spoken
argument is judged directly, and the seminars are where you have been
rehearsing it.

## How it is marked

- **The numbers are right**, current, and cited.
- **Cause, not just effect** — the demographic mechanism is explained.
- **Options are real**, including ones you do not recommend.
- **The do-nothing line is real** — you say what this pressure will have
  done to the community by a stated point in the future, from the trend
  rather than from a feeling.
- **The recommendation is decidable** — someone could say yes to it.
- **Tone** — you are writing about people's lives. Write about them the
  way you would if they were reading it, because they might be.
- **The argument** — you can put the recommendation in ninety seconds,
  and when the objection comes you answer the one that was made rather
  than the one you prepared for. Considering a position you do not
  hold, out loud and in front of the person holding it, is the part of
  this task the page cannot show.

> [!tip] Practise the disagreement first
> Before you write, argue your recommendation out loud with someone who
> takes the other side. That is what the seminar time is for, and it is
> where [[Policy Responses]] stops being a list and starts being a
> choice.

[[What Population Change Does]] gives you the effects; the discussion
[[What Do We Owe the Next Community|What Do We Owe the Next Community?]] is where the values in your
recommendation get tested.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 3, Day 12, the writing clinic
  Watch the ORDER the thinking happens in, which the page order cannot
  tell you: every finished brief puts the options above the
  recommendation because that is the shape asked for, whether the
  options were genuinely weighed first or written backwards from an
  answer decided on Day 11.
  Going well: two or three options on the desk with their costs beside
  them, and nothing yet written under "recommendation".
  Stuck: the recommendation was there on Day 11 and has not moved
  since; the options are being filled in around it.
  Record: class list, one column — options first, answer first, asked.

TALK — Unit 3, Day 13, in the working period after the seminar
  Ask: "Your recommendation costs money. Whose budget does it come out
  of, and what stops being funded?"
  A strong answer names a real holder of a real budget — this council,
  that board, a ministry — and something it currently pays for. That is
  D2.2: evaluating a strategy, which is not the same as liking it, and
  a one-page brief can list a cost without anybody having asked who
  carries it.
  Then: "Leave your recommendation out of it. If nobody does anything,
  what will this pressure have done to the community in five years?"
  A strong answer describes people rather than numbers — who has left,
  who has arrived, what has closed — and hangs it on a trend already in
  the data. That is D2.1: predicting the future impact of a demographic
  change, which the do-nothing line on the brief states in one sentence
  and cannot show the reasoning for.
  Record: one line per student — the budget, and the five-year picture.

The product evidence is the brief handed in on Day 14. The ninety
seconds on their feet is the other half — Day 14 for one half of the
room, Day 15 for the other. It is the one place in the seventy where a
spoken case is marked, so mark it there and then, before the next
student stands up.
