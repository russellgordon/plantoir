**Launched in the second week of Unit 2; delivered on the last day of the
unit.** Teams of four, marked individually.

Your team runs a small, real project for a real audience: an event, a
campaign, a service improvement, or a piece of work for a club or
community group in your school or town. It must involve other people who
are not in your team.

## The deliverables

| Deliverable | When | What it is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Team charter | Two days after launch | How you decide, what "done" means, what happens when someone misses a commitment |
| Stand-up record | Every working period | Three lines per person; the facilitator rotates |
| The project itself | Last week of the unit | Delivered to its actual audience |
| Your own account | With the project | One page: what YOU did, what you changed when the team pushed back, what you would do differently |

## What is marked as yours

**There is no team mark on this task.** Four people can hand in one
project and leave with four different marks, and usually do. Yours comes
from four things, all of them individual:

- **The part of the project you delivered**, named in the charter before
  the work starts, so that it is not decided afterwards.
- **The stand-ups you facilitated.** The facilitator rotates; your turns
  are yours.
- **Your one-page account**, above — about what you did, not a rating of
  anybody else.
- **What you say at the checkpoint conference** in the third week, where
  I ask each of you separately.

Nothing you write about a teammate, and nothing they write about you,
becomes anyone's mark. Say the hard thing to each other anyway — that is
what the charter is for, and it is the answer to social loafing described
in [[What Makes a Team Work]] — but the judging is mine, from evidence I
gather myself.

## How it is marked

- **The charter, and whether you used it.** A charter written and ignored
  is worth less than a short one you actually invoked.
- **Process evidence** — the stand-up record, and what changed because of
  it.
- **The project's result**, judged by whether the audience got what was
  promised.
- **How you worked with the team to get it done** — how you handled the
  week your team disagreed, what you did with a blocker somebody raised.
  This one is curriculum, not a work habit: expectation B2.4 asks you to
  *apply business teamwork skills to carry out projects and solve
  problems*, so applying them is marked here in a way that being pleasant
  to work with is not. [[How Marks Work]] explains where that line sits.

> [!tip] Expect the storming stage
> Your team will have a bad week. Teams that named it in the charter
> handle it in a meeting; teams that did not handle it in the last
> forty-eight hours before the deadline. See [[How Groups Form]].

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 2, Day 15, the stand-up run by a new facilitator, and the
  twenty-minute working period straight after it
  Watch for: what happens in the four minutes AFTER a blocker is named.
  Does somebody change their own commitment out loud, or does the meeting
  move to the next person and the blocker survive to the following week?
  The stand-up record reports what was raised and even what changed, so
  the record corroborates this rather than replacing it — what the record
  cannot show is WHO made the change. In most teams it is the same person
  every time, and that is the thing worth knowing. B2.4 (apply business
  teamwork skills to carry out projects and solve problems) is an
  applying expectation, and applying is only ever visible live.
  Going well: the new facilitator runs it their own way and the team lets
  them; somebody says "then I'll move mine to Thursday" without being
  asked.
  Stuck: the previous facilitator is still running it from the side, or
  the blocker is answered with sympathy and nothing else.
  Record: four columns on the day plan, one per team, and the initials of
  whoever moved. Thirty seconds per team.

TALK — Unit 2, Day 13, the conferences with each team already on that
  agenda — but ask each member on their own, not the team together
  Ask: "Which of this team's rules was never written into the charter,
  and how do you know everyone has it?" A strong answer names something
  real — nobody messages after ten, the person who says "I'll do it"
  means this week — and says how it got established. That is B2.3
  (analyse the factors that contribute to the success or failure of a
  team; shared norms is the first example the expectation gives), and it
  is invisible in every document the team produces, because unwritten
  norms are by definition not in the charter.
  Then: "Who in this team has taken a role nobody assigned them — and
  what does the team lose if they stop doing it?" A strong answer names
  the person and the function, not the personality: the one who writes
  everything down, the one who asks the question everybody was avoiding.
  That is B2.1 (analyse the stages of group development and the roles
  individuals assume within a group structure), and unassigned roles
  appear in no document the team produces.
  Do not ask about communication techniques here. Improving Communication
  is not taught until Day 15, two classes after this one, so on Day 13
  you would be testing them on a lesson they have not had. Do not ask
  whether they used the charter when somebody missed a commitment,
  either; the criteria print that and they will arrive with it.
  Record: a grid on one sheet — teams down, members across — and a letter
  in each cell.

The product evidence is the project delivered to its real audience on the
last day of the unit, the charter, the stand-up record, and each
student's one-page account.
