"The Thirteen Colonies" is a phrase that flattens thirteen very different
places into one. They were founded at different times, by different sorts of
people, under different legal instruments, for different purposes. Reading
them as a single unit hides the thing worth explaining — why the regions
that emerged had such distinct characters, and why those characters outlasted
the empire that produced them.

## Three questions that separate them

Ask of any colony: who owned the land, who did the work, and who was allowed
to belong. The answers diverge sharply.

New England began with English Puritan families who arrived together, settled
in towns, farmed modest holdings, and built schools and meeting houses
because their religion required literate members. The Chesapeake began as a
commercial venture — the Virginia Company sold shares — and organized itself
around tobacco, a crop that exhausts soil and demands constant labour. That
demand was met first by indentured servants from England and then, as the
seventeenth century wore on, by enslaved Africans held for life and by law.
The middle colonies were the most mixed from the start: Dutch, Swedish,
German and Scots-Irish settlers, several religions, and in Pennsylvania a
Quaker proprietor who wrote toleration into the founding.

> [!note]- Charter, proprietary, royal — and why it mattered
> A **charter** colony was governed under a grant to a company or to the
> settlers themselves. A **proprietary** colony belonged to an individual or
> family who appointed the governor — Pennsylvania under William Penn,
> Maryland under the Calverts. A **royal** colony was governed by a governor
> the Crown appointed directly. Most colonies became royal eventually. The
> distinction decided who could tax, who could grant land, and how much
> distance there was between a colonist and the Crown — which is why it
> shaped the political habits that survived into independence.

## Life inside the difference

A woman in a wealthy New England merchant household and a woman on a frontier
farm shared a legal status and almost nothing else. Both lost most property
rights on marriage. One had servants, imported goods and a settled town; the
other had unbroken labour, isolation, and a much higher chance of burying a
child. An enslaved woman in the Chesapeake had neither — no legal claim to
her own children, who from 1662 in Virginia inherited her condition rather
than her father's. That statute is worth remembering: it made slavery
heritable through the mother, and it made the sexual violence of enslavers
profitable.

Urban and rural slavery differed too. In Charleston or New York, enslaved
people worked as artisans, dockhands and household servants, moved through
the city, and built networks. On a rice or tobacco plantation the work was
field labour under supervision, and the isolation was the point.

[[Historical Significance]] to decide which of the differences actually went
on to matter.
