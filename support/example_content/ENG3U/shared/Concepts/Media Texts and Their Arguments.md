Documentaries, podcasts, long-form journalism, and campaigns are texts
with arguments, and at this level they are read exactly as closely as a
novel — with two additional questions: what does the form permit, and who
paid for it?

## The questions

1. **What is the claim?** State it in a sentence its makers would accept.
2. **Who made it, for whom, and with whose money?**
3. **What evidence carries it** — documents, interviews, footage,
   statistics, or the audience's existing feelings?
4. **What techniques carry it** — see the table below.
5. **What is conceded, and what is left out?**

## Techniques

| Technique | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Interview selection** | Who speaks, at what length, and who is described instead |
| **Sequence** | Order creates causation the material may not support |
| **Score and silence** | Music tells the audience how to feel before evidence arrives |
| **Archival footage** | Borrows the authority of the past; check the caption |
| **Voice-over** | The only unopposed voice in most documentaries |
| **Framing and grade** | Warm for sympathy, cold for institutional distance |
| **The reconstruction** | A dramatised scene presented alongside evidence |

## Documentary is not neutral

A documentary chooses what to film, whom to interview, what to cut, and
what to put underneath it. It can be honest; it cannot be neutral. The
useful test is to describe the film an equally honest maker with the
opposite conclusion would produce from the same material.

## Where the argument and the literature meet
