**1.** How much work is done lifting a $12\ \text{kg}$ box
$1.5\ \text{m}$ onto a shelf?

> [!success]- Answer 1
> $W = F d = (12 \times 9.8)(1.5) = 176\ \text{J}$, which is also the
> gravitational potential energy it gained.

**2.** A $0.15\ \text{kg}$ ball is dropped from $2.0\ \text{m}$. How
fast is it moving just before it lands, ignoring air resistance?

> [!success]- Answer 2
> $mgh = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, so $v = \sqrt{2gh} = 6.3\ \text{m/s}$.
> The mass cancels — which is the same fact as everything falling at the
> same rate.

**3.** That ball rebounds to $1.4\ \text{m}$. What fraction of its energy
came back, and where did the rest go?

> [!success]- Answer 3
> $1.4/2.0 = 70\%$. The other 30% became heat in the ball and the floor,
> and sound — the thud is energy leaving.

**4.** A $60\ \text{kg}$ student climbs a $3.2\ \text{m}$ staircase in
$4.5\ \text{s}$. Find the power developed.

> [!success]- Answer 4
> $W = mgh = 1880\ \text{J}$; $P = W/t = 1880/4.5 = 420\ \text{W}$.
> About the same as five bright old-style light bulbs, sustained.

**5.** How much energy is needed to raise $0.50\ \text{kg}$ of water from
$20^\circ\text{C}$ to boiling? ($c = 4180\ \text{J/kg}^\circ\text{C}$)

> [!success]- Answer 5
> $Q = mc\Delta T = 0.50 \times 4180 \times 80 = 1.7 \times 10^5\ \text{J}$.

**6.** A $1500\ \text{W}$ electric water heater operates for $12\ \text{minutes}$ to heat $3.5\ \text{kg}$ of water from $15^\circ\text{C}$ to $75^\circ\text{C}$.
- (a) Compute the electrical energy input ($E_{in}$) and the useful thermal energy absorbed by the water ($Q$).
- (b) Calculate the thermal transfer efficiency and explain why real thermal transformation technologies never achieve $100\%$ efficiency.

> [!success]- Answer 6
> (a) Input energy: $E_{in} = P\Delta t = 1500\ \text{W} \times (12 \times 60\ \text{s}) = 1.08 \times 10^6\ \text{J}$.  
> Useful thermal energy: $Q = mc\Delta T = 3.5 \times 4180 \times (75 - 15) = 8.78 \times 10^5\ \text{J}$.  
> (b) Efficiency $= (Q / E_{in}) \times 100\% = (8.78 \times 10^5 / 1.08 \times 10^6) \times 100\% = 81.3\%$. The remaining $18.7\%$ is dissipated as unrecoverable waste heat through conduction and radiation to the surrounding environment and the heater casing.

**7.** A $0.25\ \text{kg}$ sample of solid water (ice) at $-10^\circ\text{C}$ is heated at a steady rate of $250\ \text{W}$ until it completely converts to steam at $100^\circ\text{C}$. ($c_{ice} = 2100\ \text{J/kg}^\circ\text{C}$, $L_f = 3.33 \times 10^5\ \text{J/kg}$, $c_{water} = 4180\ \text{J/kg}^\circ\text{C}$, $L_v = 2.26 \times 10^6\ \text{J/kg}$).
- (a) Calculate the energy required for each segment of the heating curve: warming ice, melting, warming liquid water, and boiling.
- (b) Using kinetic molecular theory, explain why the temperature remains constant during the two plateau regions despite continuous thermal energy input.

> [!success]- Answer 7
> (a) Warming ice: $Q_1 = (0.25)(2100)(10) = 5250\ \text{J}$.  
> Melting ice ($0^\circ\text{C}$): $Q_2 = (0.25)(3.33 \times 10^5) = 83\ 250\ \text{J}$.  
> Warming water ($0^\circ\text{C} \rightarrow 100^\circ\text{C}$): $Q_3 = (0.25)(4180)(100) = 104\ 500\ \text{J}$.  
> Boiling to steam ($100^\circ\text{C}$): $Q_4 = (0.25)(2.26 \times 10^6) = 565\ 000\ \text{J}$.  
> Total energy $= 5250 + 83\ 250 + 104\ 500 + 565\ 000 = 758\ 000\ \text{J}$ ($758\ \text{kJ}$).  
> (b) Temperature measures average molecular kinetic energy ($E_k$). During phase change plateaus, added thermal energy does not accelerate particles; instead, it does work against intermolecular hydrogen bonds (increasing potential energy $E_p$), separating molecules without raising temperature.

**8.** In a nuclear fission reaction inside a CANDU reactor, uranium-235 ($\ce{^{235}_{92}U}$) absorbs a thermal neutron and splits into barium-141 ($\ce{^{141}_{56}Ba}$), krypton-92 ($\ce{^{92}_{36}Kr}$), and 3 neutrons:
$$\ce{^{235}_{92}U + ^1_0n -> ^{141}_{56}Ba + ^{92}_{36}Kr + 3 ^1_0n}$$
The total mass of the reactants is $236.0526\ \text{u}$ and the total mass of the products is $235.8665\ \text{u}$ ($1\ \text{u} = 1.6605 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$).
- (a) Compute the mass defect $\Delta m$ in kilograms and the energy released ($E$) per fission event in joules.
- (b) Compare nuclear fission with nuclear fusion of deuterium ($\ce{^2_1H}$) and tritium ($\ce{^3_1H}$) in terms of reactant availability, energy density, and radioactive waste.

> [!success]- Answer 8
> (a) Mass defect: $\Delta m = 236.0526 - 235.8665 = 0.1861\ \text{u} = (0.1861)(1.6605 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}) = 3.090 \times 10^{-28}\ \text{kg}$.  
> Energy released: $E = (\Delta m)c^2 = (3.090 \times 10^{-28}\ \text{kg})(3.00 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s})^2 = 2.78 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{J}\ (\approx 173\ \text{MeV})$.  
> (b) Fission uses heavy, scarce fissile isotopes ($\ce{^{235}U}$) and yields long-lived radioactive fission products requiring geological storage. Fusion uses abundant hydrogen isotopes extracted from water (deuterium) or bred from lithium (tritium), releases higher energy per unit mass, and produces non-radioactive helium ($\ce{^4He}$) with minimal activation waste.

**9.** An inquiry into the specific heat capacity of an unknown metal alloy is conducted using a coffee-cup calorimeter. A $0.180\ \text{kg}$ metal cylinder is heated in boiling water to $99.5^\circ\text{C}$ and quickly transferred into a calorimeter containing $0.220\ \text{kg}$ of water at $18.0^\circ\text{C}$. The mixture reaches a thermal equilibrium temperature of $24.5^\circ\text{C}$. ($c_{water} = 4180\ \text{J/kg}^\circ\text{C}$, negligible calorimeter heat loss).
- (a) Determine the specific heat capacity of the metal alloy.
- (b) Identify two experimental sources of systematic error that cause the calculated $c_{metal}$ to deviate from accepted reference values.

> [!success]- Answer 9
> (a) Heat gained by water $=$ Heat lost by metal:  
> $Q_w = m_w c_w \Delta T_w = (0.220)(4180)(24.5 - 18.0) = 5977.4\ \text{J}$.  
> $Q_m = m_m c_m \Delta T_m \implies 5977.4 = (0.180)(c_m)(99.5 - 24.5) = 13.5 c_m$.  
> $c_m = 5977.4 / 13.5 = 443\ \text{J/kg}^\circ\text{C}$ (consistent with iron or steel alloy).  
> (b) Systematic errors: (1) Thermal energy lost to the surrounding air during the physical transfer of the hot metal drops its starting temperature below $99.5^\circ\text{C}$; (2) Thermal energy absorbed by the calorimeter container and stirrer is omitted, causing the measured equilibrium $\Delta T_w$ to read low.

**10.** Technetium-$99\text{m}$ ($\ce{^{99\text{m}}_{43}Tc}$) is a gamma-emitting radioisotope used in medical diagnostic myocardial perfusion imaging with a half-life of $6.0\ \text{hours}$.
- (a) If a hospital receives a freshly prepared $800\ \text{MBq}$ dose, calculate the activity remaining after $24\ \text{hours}$.
- (b) Compare alpha, beta, and gamma radiation with respect to mass, charge, ionizing power, and necessary radiation protection shielding.

> [!success]- Answer 10
> (a) Number of elapsed half-lives: $n = t / t_{1/2} = 24 / 6.0 = 4$.  
> Remaining activity: $A = A_0 (1/2)^n = 800 (1/2)^4 = 800 / 16 = 50\ \text{MBq}$.  
> (b) Alpha ($\alpha$, $\ce{^4_2He^{2+}}$): high mass ($4\ \text{u}$), $+2e$ charge, highest ionizing power, stopped by paper/skin; Beta ($\beta^-$, $\text{e}^-$): negligible mass ($1/1836\ \text{u}$), $-1e$ charge, moderate ionizing power, stopped by aluminium plate; Gamma ($\gamma$, photon): zero mass, neutral ($0$), lowest ionizing power but highest penetrating capability, requiring lead aprons or dense concrete shielding.
