Practice, with the answer to each question folded up underneath it.

These are not marked, not counted, and not collected. Ever. They are how
you find out whether you understood something before a checkpoint — or
the examination — tells you that you did not.

> [!tip] How to use these pages
> Answer the question **first**, on paper, all the way to a number with
> a unit. Then click the answer to unfold it.
>
> Reading a worked answer feels like learning and mostly is not —
> recognising an answer is a completely different skill from producing
> one, and only one of them is any use in an exam.

> [!success]- Try it: click this line
> That is what every answer on these pages looks like. Nothing is hidden
> from you — it is hidden from your *eye*, so that a page of questions
> stays a page of questions until you have had a go.

Every answer here is **worked**, not stated. The number at the end is
the least interesting part; the reasoning above it is the part worth
holding against your own.

## Three rules that apply to every set

**Carry the units through the working, not just onto the answer.** If
the units do not cancel to what you wanted, the arithmetic is wrong and
the units have just told you so before you lost a mark for it. This is
the cheapest error check in chemistry and almost nobody uses it. It
matters more this year than last, because kilojoules per mole,
litres squared per mole squared per second, and volts are all quantities
where a unit slip produces a plausible-looking wrong answer.

**Round once, at the end.** Every intermediate value stays at full
precision in the calculator, and the final answer is rounded to the
number of significant figures the *data* allow. A number rounded twice
is a number you can no longer defend. Logarithms have their own
convention and it catches everybody once — the digits **after** the
decimal point in a pH are the significant ones. See
[[Significant Figures and Units]] and
[[Working with Logarithms in Chemistry]].

**Sanity-check before you move on.** Is the answer roughly the size you
expected? Is an equilibrium constant positive? Is a pH between about
$-1$ and 15? Is an exothermic $\Delta H$ negative? Did the rate go up
when you raised the concentration? A five-second check catches most of
what a whole page of working can get wrong.

## Where the data comes from

Some questions on these pages hand you a value — an enthalpy of
formation, a $K_a$, a standard reduction potential — inside the question
itself. That is deliberate: those questions are about the method, and
the number is given so that everyone is working from the same one.

For anything else, **the course data booklet is the source**, not this
site and not your memory. The three reference pages exist to teach you
how to read the booklet's tables rather than to duplicate them:
[[Reading an Equilibrium Table]],
[[Reading a Reduction Potential Table]], and
[[Significant Figures and Units]]. A value quoted with no conditions
attached is not a value — an equilibrium constant belongs to a
temperature, and a standard potential belongs to a defined set of
conditions.

## What each set is shaped like

Each set ramps. The first questions are routine and the last ones are
not, and somewhere near the end of every set is a question asking you to
say what is wrong with somebody else's answer. Those are the ones worth
doing slowly — finding the flaw in a plausible piece of reasoning is the
skill the examination is actually built around, and it is also what you
will be doing to your own lab data all semester.

Two habits that Grade 12 rewards and Grade 11 did not need:

- **Say which model you are using, and where it stops.** A rate law
  read off a balanced equation is wrong unless the step is elementary.
  A shape predicted by electron counting gets the angle wrong whenever
  there is a lone pair. Naming the boundary is worth more than getting
  the answer.
- **Separate "which way" from "how fast".** A negative $\Delta H$ says
  nothing about rate. A large $K_c$ says nothing about rate. Half the
  hard questions in this course are hard only because they blur those
  two, and half the marks are for keeping them apart.

Stuck partway through a set? That is the set doing its job. Start with
[[Getting Unstuck]], and bring the specific question to
[[Help Sessions]] rather than "I don't get equilibrium".
