For years the honest answer ended in a deferral: *you need it for
calculus*. This is calculus. The bill comes due here, and it pays.
The question "how fast is it changing *right now*?" is asked daily
by every science, every market, and every public-health office alive
— and this course does not walk you to its edge, it hands you the
answer, in the form [[The Derivative]] makes precise. Optimization —
the art of finding the best of all possible somethings — runs
package design, flight schedules, dosing curves, and half the
software you touched today; [[The Packaging Brief]] puts a real one
in your hands. And vectors are the native language of anything that
moves through space: navigation, wind, forces, the graphics pipeline
drawing this very screen. [[The Flight Path]] flies on them.

The second answer is about transfer: you may never compute a cross
product after this course, but you will spend a lifetime modelling messy
situations, checking claims before trusting them, and arguing from
constraints. That training happens here whether the algebra
resurfaces or not.

Questions worth arguing about:

1. "When will I use this?" is sometimes an honest question and
   sometimes code for something else — frustration, being lost, a
   fair complaint. How could a classmate tell which they just heard?
2. A headline says "cases are still rising, but more slowly." That
   sentence is about a derivative *and* about the derivative of the
   derivative. Who benefits when the public cannot tell *falling*
   from *rising more slowly* — and who pays?
3. List three quantities from your own week whose *rate of change*
   mattered more than their value. Whose job is it to notice?
4. Nobody asks a basketball player when they will use wind sprints.
   What is different about mathematics — or is anything?
5. For once, nobody can answer "you need it for next year" — the
   deferral has run out of road, because this is the course the
   deferrals pointed at. Does arriving change what the question
   means? Defend your position.

The test of today's talk is what you do at [[The Math Symposium]] —
where the mathematics is not dressed up as useful, but pointed at
something you already care about.
