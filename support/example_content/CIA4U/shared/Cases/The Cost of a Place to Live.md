Housing is the case where every tool in this course meets at once —
supply, demand, elasticity, externalities, three levels of government, and
a household budget. It is also the case where you are most likely to
already hold a view, which is why it is worth arguing carefully.

## What is measured, and what it says

| Measure | Latest figure | Source and date |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Housing starts | 259,028 in 2025; 241,400 forecast for 2026 | CMHC |
| Units needed each year to 2035 | 430,000 to 480,000 | CMHC supply-gap framework, 2025 |
| Purpose-built apartment vacancy rate | 3.1% in 2025, up from 2.2% in 2024 | CMHC Rental Market Survey |
| Forecast sales and average price, 2026 | 457,200 sales, \$675,200 average | CMHC outlook, July 2026 |
| The same, forecast independently | 463,336 sales, \$686,710 average | CREA forecast, July 2026 |

Read those rows against each other before you read them separately. The
2025 starts figure was the fifth-highest annual total on record, and it is
roughly half what CMHC says is needed — up to 4.8 million homes over the
decade to restore the affordability of 2019. At the same time the rental
vacancy rate rose and both forecasters expect prices to soften. Building
close to a record and falling further behind is not a contradiction; it is
what a supply gap measured against a target looks like.

## Where economists genuinely disagree

CMHC's framework treats the binding constraint as the number of units, so
its remedy is anything that raises completions — zoning, development
charges, construction labour, financing. Others argue that the demand side
dominates: the cost and availability of credit, investor purchases, and
the pace of household formation, which has slowed with the fall in
non-permanent residents (down 117,879 in the first quarter of 2026, and
Canada's population fell 55,025 over the same quarter, released
17 June 2026).

Both sides use the same starts and completions data. What differs is which
lever they think moves the price, and that is an argument you can actually
adjudicate with evidence rather than preference.

## What to do with it

Take one municipality — ideally this one — and answer: who bears the cost
of the current arrangement, and who would bear the cost of changing it?
Existing owners, prospective buyers, renters, builders and the municipal
budget all sit on different sides, and a policy that helps one is rarely
neutral for the others. That is the trade-off structure
[[The Intervention Argument]] asks you to name.

Bring [[Supply and Demand]] and [[Elasticity]]: housing supply is highly
inelastic in the short run, which is the single fact that explains why
demand-side measures move prices faster than they move construction.
