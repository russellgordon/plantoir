The success criteria go up on the task page on the day the task launches,
which means you have them for every working period. Almost
nobody looks at them twice. Work then arrives missing two rows that
twenty minutes would have repaired, and the twenty minutes were
available all along. This page is those twenty minutes, written down as
a routine so that you do not have to invent one under pressure.

The first time, we do it as a room, on somebody else's page and with
nothing at stake, so that judging is a familiar move before it is ever
your own reasoning on the table.

## The routine

1. Put the criteria table beside your work — the task page open on one
   side, your write-up on the other.
2. Go row by row. For each one write **yes**, **not yet**, or **partly**,
   and one sentence of evidence: the line, the graph, the number a reader
   would point at. "Yes" with nothing to point at is a wish.
3. Find your **weakest** row. Not the easiest to fix — the weakest.
4. Write one sentence saying what you will change, and when.
5. Change it, in the time that is left in this period. This is why the
   self-check is never scheduled on a due date: a judgement you have no
   time to act on is just a worry.

Steps 2 and 3 are the whole exercise. Naming your weakest row honestly is
harder than any of the fixing, because it means saying out loud the thing
you were hoping nobody would look at closely.

> [!example]- One row, judged honestly
> From [[The Packaging Brief]]: *"A proven optimum — the critical point
> confirmed, not assumed."*
>
> **Partly.** We set the derivative to zero and got a width of
> $7.4\text{ cm}$, and the box it gives really does use less card. But
> the only reason we believe it is a minimum is that the number came out
> nicer than the alternatives. We never ran a sign chart and we never
> checked the ends of the domain. Weakest row.
> **Change:** sign chart either side of $7.4$, then both endpoints, in
> the next twenty minutes — and if the endpoint wins, the recommendation
> changes.
>
> That entry is worth more than "looks good to me" on all five rows, and
> it took less time to write.

## Judging is not the same as checking

[[Checking Your Own Work]] asks *is this right?* — units, sign, order of
magnitude, does the answer even make sense. That is arithmetic hygiene
and you should run it constantly.

Judging asks a different question: *is this the work the task asked
for?* An answer can be perfectly correct and still fail three rows,
because the rows are about the reasoning being visible, the model being
honest, and the reader being able to follow. Run both. They catch
different things.

## Where this fits

Nothing you write on this page reaches your mark. Neither your judgement
of your work nor a classmate's counts towards it — [[How Marks Work]]
says why, and the consequence is the useful part: a "not yet" you write
here is free. It cannot cost you anything, which is precisely what makes
it worth writing truthfully rather than diplomatically.

What it changes is the work itself. Twenty minutes of judging that ends
in one repair is the cheapest improvement available anywhere in this
course, and by the vectors unit most of you run it without being asked.
That habit is not an extra. *Reflecting on and monitoring your thinking*
is one of the seven mathematical processes this course is built on, and
the curriculum's own examples of it are judging whether a result is
reasonable and verifying a solution — which is this page, in two
sentences. It is the one work habit that is also curriculum.

A last trick, and it costs nothing. Say your judgement out loud to your
partner rather than only writing it. The rows where you were being kind
to yourself tend to catch in your throat on the way out, and you will
hear them before anybody else has to point at them.
