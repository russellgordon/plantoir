> Whole newsroom, with rotating roles one last time · the last stretch

> channels published together and presented live to invited readers ·
> this is the final evaluation, worth thirty per cent

## What you are making

The culminating beat: the newsroom publishes its term. Together we
select the strongest work from every beat, polish it to publication
standard, and ship it across all four channels — the site, the social
feed, the paper, and the news program — as one coordinated release.

Then we open the doors. Invited readers — families, staff, the people
our stories covered — come to see the work, and each member speaks to
one piece: what it is, what it took, and what changed in you.

This is the whole newsroom working at once, and there is still no team
mark. You are evaluated on the role you hold in the run sheet, the
pieces you polished and can show me the before and after of, the
portfolio you select, and the two minutes you spend beside your piece.
Publication day is ours; the mark is yours.

## How to work

1. Curate as an editorial board: every candidate piece is re-read
   against [[Our Newsroom Standards]] — accuracy, consent, and credit
   are re-checked now, not assumed from the first publication.
2. Build the production schedule backwards from publication day: who
   polishes what, in which role — writer, photographer, editor,
   producer — and by when. The schedule is itself part of what is
   evaluated — it is [[B1.1|the planning expectation]] in the flesh —
   so write it down, and revise it in writing when reality moves.
3. Polish. Every selected piece gets one more edit pass; fixes are
   logged so the improvement is visible.
4. Assemble the channels so they speak to each other — the paper and
   the program point to the site, the social posts carry readers in —
   and rehearse the running order once, end to end. Set the room up
   the way a working crew does: cables run and taped, table edges
   clear, nothing to trip the readers who arrive tomorrow.
5. Prepare your talk: choose the piece you will stand beside, use
   [[Showing Growth]] to say what got better with proof, and name one
   work habit this newsroom could not have published without — and why
   a working newsroom needs it, not just ours.
6. Publish, present, and thank your readers by name.
7. Afterwards, select five pieces from your term as a portfolio, each
   captioned with what it shows about you. Five chosen is a portfolio;
   everything you made is an archive.

The final desk periods are class time for curating, polishing, and
setting up. Everything I evaluate on this beat is either made or said
in this room — [[How Marks Work]] explains why that matters.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like on publication day |
| --- | --- |
| Curated honestly | The term's strongest work, polished measurably further |
| Standards met | Every piece passes a fresh check, not a remembered one |
| Schedule in writing | Written down, then revised when reality moved it |
| Four channels, one voice | Site, social, paper, and program cohere |
| Accuracy | Every republished name, fact, and quote re-verified |
| Consent | Every face and name re-cleared for the wider audience |
| Credit | Every contributor named, in every channel, every time |
| Growth spoken | Your talk names a real change, with evidence for it |
| A habit named | Your talk names a habit the job needs, and why it does |
| Set up safely | Cables run, edges cleared, before anything is plugged in |
| Portfolio, not archive | Five pieces chosen, captioned with what each shows |

## Reflect

Your reflection is the capstone of your [[Newsroom Journal]]: the
[[Final Reflection]], written after the readers go home. What kind of
newsroom member did you become — and what is the evidence? The talk
was the spoken draft; the journal entry is the record.

> [!success]- If speaking to a room scares you (click to expand)
> You are not performing — you are standing beside work that already
> exists and telling the truth about it. Two minutes, one piece, one
> honest sentence about what changed. One rehearsal, and it will hold.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.
Note that this is the final evaluation, so the observation and the
conversation below carry thirty per cent weight and deserve the time.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 12, final assembly and the room set-up
  Not Day 13. On Day 13 you are running a room with guests in it and
  will see almost nothing. Day 12 is assembly, the run sheet, and the
  tables and cables.
  Two things are visible here and nowhere else. First, what happens to
  the schedule drafted on Day 2 and revised on Day 8 when it meets a
  channel that is not ready: is it revised again in front of everyone
  as the run sheet gets built, or worked around while the Day 8 version
  stays as written? The criteria row "Schedule in writing"
  asks whether it was revised in writing, and a revised schedule is a
  document you can read afterwards — so what this period adds is
  corroboration: you see the revising happen, or you see a tidy-up
  written up later as though it had. Either way it is B1.1, the same
  planning expectation the schedule itself answers.
  Second, who walks the cable runs and clears the table edges before
  anyone plugs anything in: D1.2 is applying safe work practices, and a
  published package can never show you a trip hazard that was moved.
  Going well: somebody reads the old schedule aloud, somebody else
  crosses a line out, and the new time goes onto the run sheet.
  Stuck: the run sheet is being written from memory, with the Day 8
  schedule still in a folder nobody has opened.
  Record: on your own copy of the run sheet, initial each revision as
  it is made, and note the two or three people who set the room up.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 10, while the editorial board curates and polishes
  Two minutes each at their desks. You will not reach a full class in
  one period, so take half the room here and the other half during the
  proofing desk time on Day 11.
  "This publication has one person it could not run without this week.
  Who, and what do they do that nobody notices?"
  Then: "If this newsroom hired one more person tomorrow, what would
  you tell them to do in their first week that nobody would think to
  say out loud?"
  A strong answer names the habit and the reason a newsroom needs it —
  a producer who confirms call times in writing so nobody arrives at
  the wrong door; a photographer who clears cards the night before,
  because the card that is full is always the one at the final whistle.
  That is D2.4, which asks for an understanding of the industry's work
  habits, not for a report on their own reliability — so a student who
  is often late can answer it superbly, and should.
  Record: one name and one habit per student you reach, in the margin
  of the curation list, written while you are standing there.

The product evidence is the four channels published on Day 13, and the
portfolio selected on Day 17. Those arrive on their own.
