For the length of one speech, Hamlet stops performing for the other
characters in [[Hamlet]] and talks directly to you. Nobody else on stage
hears "O, that this too too solid flesh would melt" — you are the only
audience for it, and that changes what you know about Hamlet that the
rest of the court does not.

## Soliloquy

A **soliloquy** is a speech delivered by a character alone on stage, or
believing themselves unheard, that reveals their private thoughts
directly to the audience rather than to another character. It is
distinct from a monologue, where other characters are present and
listening, and from an aside, which is a brief remark rather than a
sustained speech. A soliloquy is the closest a play gets to letting you
inside a character's head — for its duration, you have privileged access
nobody else in the play shares.

> [!question]- Self-check: soliloquy, monologue, or aside? (click to
> expand)
> A servant delivers a long speech to a full room describing their
> master's cruelty — that is a **monologue**, because the room is
> listening. A character mutters "this will not end well" while others
> talk over them, unheard — that is an **aside**. Hamlet, alone, working
> through whether to keep living — that is a **soliloquy**.

## Dramatic irony

**Dramatic irony** occurs when the audience knows something a character
on stage does not. A soliloquy is one of the most direct ways a
playwright builds it: once you know what Hamlet is actually thinking, you
watch every scene afterward differently than the other characters do,
because you are carrying information they do not have. That gap between
what you know and what they know is where a lot of the play's tension
lives.

## Watch for it

Every time Hamlet speaks to Claudius, Gertrude, or Polonius after this
soliloquy, you know something about his state of mind that they are only
guessing at. That gap is worth tracking scene by scene — it is a large
part of why the play feels tense even in its quieter conversations.
