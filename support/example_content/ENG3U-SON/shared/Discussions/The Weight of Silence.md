*[[Indian Horse]]* is Saul's own account of his own life, told in his own
words — and it still isn't a story that tells you everything. There are
places where you feel the shape of something the text won't say directly.
Today's seminar is about finding those places and asking what the silence
around them is doing.

## How the fishbowl works

Today the class splits into two circles: an inner circle that talks, and an
outer circle that listens and says nothing until it's their turn. Halfway
through, the circles trade places, so everyone both talks and listens
before the period ends.

| Segment | Inner circle | Outer circle |
|---|---|---|
| First 5 minutes | Silently reread your marked passages | Silently reread the same passages |
| Next ~20 minutes | Discusses the questions below, out loud | Listens; notes one idea worth handing back |
| Handoff, ~5 minutes | Listens | Offers the inner circle one idea to take further |
| — Circles trade places — | | |
| Next ~20 minutes | (now the other half of the class) discusses, building on what was handed off | Listens; notes one idea worth handing back |
| Final 10 minutes | Whole class together — both circles, one conversation | |

Being in the outer circle isn't a break. Active listening — following an
argument closely enough to hand something useful back to it — is its own
kind of work, and it's worth doing on purpose rather than half-attention
while you wait for your turn to talk.

## The questions

1. Saul narrates his own life, and there are still things he doesn't tell
   us directly. Where in the novel do you feel a gap — a place where you
   sense something happened that the text won't say outright?
2. For most of the novel, Saul says nothing about what happened to him at
   St. Jerome's, not even to people trying to help him. What does staying
   silent cost him? What might it be protecting him from, at the time?
3. The novel is framed as the account Saul finally gives someone at New
   Dawn. Why does it take a place built specifically for that kind of
   telling before the telling happens at all?
4. Is Saul's silence something he chooses, or something residential school
   did to him — and does the novel let those be two different things?

A strong answer to question 2 doesn't treat Saul's silence as simply a
failure to speak up. It names something specific the silence buys him —
distance from what happened, the ability to keep functioning on the ice,
control over a story that was taken from him in every other way — and then
weighs that against what it costs him, using a moment from later in the
novel where the silence visibly starts to break something.
