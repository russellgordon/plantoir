A concept page says one idea cleanly, once, in a form you can reread in
months from now and still follow.

That is deliberately the *second* time you meet the idea. In this course
it almost always arrives first as something you did — a set of element
cards you sorted before anyone told you the rule, five reactions on a
bench with the labels removed, a paperclip you were asked to count the
atoms in. The doing comes first because an explanation you have already
half-built for yourself sticks, and one handed to you cold does not. So
these pages open by naming where the idea was met, and then they say it
properly.

They also do the part a lab period never has time for: the
**mechanism**. Not just that ionization energy rises across a period,
but why it rises and where it does not. Not just that one reactant runs
out first, but why comparing masses will point you at the wrong one.
That is the Grade 11 move, and this is where it lives.

Class pages link here. These pages do not go stale, so start here when
you are studying, when you missed a class, or when a calculation you
followed in the room falls apart at your kitchen table.

## Two kinds of page, and they fail differently

A concept page explains. An exercise page such as [[Naming Practice]]
drills. You need both, and the gap between them is diagnostic.

> [!note] What your stuck point is telling you
> If you can follow the concept page and then freeze on the exercise
> page, the missing piece is **practice**, not understanding — do ten
> more and the freeze goes away. If you can grind through the exercise
> page but could not explain to somebody why the method works, the
> missing piece is **the concept page**, and no amount of extra practice
> will supply it. Those two problems feel identical from the inside and
> have opposite fixes.

Five pages in [[Reference/index|the Reference folder]] are neither: they
are lookup sheets you keep open while you work rather than pages you
read once. [[Polyatomic Ions]] and [[Solubility Rules]] are the two you
will open most.
