Two questions today, and they turn out to be the same question. Does
anything disappear when a reaction happens? And if nothing does, what
does that force a chemical equation to look like?

## What you are trying to find out

Something obviously *leaves* when baking soda meets vinegar — you can
hear it. The question is whether it left the universe or merely left
the beaker.

The measurement is simple and the design is not. You have a balance
that reads to a hundredth of a gram and a reaction that produces a gas.
Getting an honest answer means deciding, before you start, what has to
be inside your measurement for the measurement to mean anything.

## What you have to work with

- Electronic balance, and its resolution written on it
- Baking soda, vinegar, a small beaker, a spatula, weighing boat
- Zip-seal freezer bags, a tray
- Molecular model kits, for the second half of the period

Your design decisions, written down and checked before you run
anything:

- **How you enclose the reaction.** You need the gas included in the
  final mass. Say exactly how, and how you will know whether your seal
  held.
- **How much of each.** Too little and the change hides inside the
  balance's resolution. Too much and your bag becomes a problem — see
  the safety box, which is not decorative here.
- **What counts as "the system".** Bag, contents, and the small
  container inside it are all being massed. Anything that leaves your
  hands between the two readings has broken the comparison.
- **The comparison run.** Design the open version too, in a beaker,
  and predict its result separately.

> [!danger] A sealed reaction that makes gas is a pressure problem
> This is the one part of today that can genuinely go wrong.
>
> - **Never seal this reaction in a rigid container** — no stoppered
>   flask, no screw-top jar, no capped bottle. A bag that inflates and
>   pops is startling and messy. A rigid container that fails is
>   dangerous.
> - Use a **freezer-weight zip bag**, leave plenty of air space, and
>   keep the quantities to the amounts on the card.
> - Keep the bag **flat on the tray**, and keep faces away from it. Do
>   not hold it up to look at it, and do not squeeze it.
> - Eye protection on throughout. Vinegar is dilute acetic acid and it
>   stings badly in an eye.
> - Nothing is tasted, and nothing is combined that is not on the card.
> - Wipe every spill straight away — a wet balance pan gives nonsense
>   readings and a wet floor is how somebody falls.
> - Tell me immediately if a bag fails, however little came out.

## The prediction you write first

In your journal, before the first mass is recorded:

1. The **sealed** run: heavier, lighter, or unchanged after the
   reaction — and by roughly how much, with a reason.
2. The **open** run: same three choices, same requirement of a reason.
3. If the two runs disagree, what the difference between them measures.

Write a number, not a direction, wherever you can. "Unchanged to within
the balance's resolution" is a real prediction that can fail; "about
the same" is not.

## What to collect

| Run | Mass before (g) | Mass after (g) | Change (g) | Larger than the balance's resolution? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sealed | | | | |
| Open | | | | |

Record the balance's resolution before your first reading and put it at
the top of the table. A change you cannot distinguish from zero is
different from a change of zero, and only one of those two sentences is
something you measured.

### Then: counting, with models

Build the reactants from the model kit, take them apart, and rebuild
the products using **every atom and no others**. Then record what you
built.

| Element | Atoms on the reactant side | Atoms on the product side |
| --- | --- | --- |
| | | |

Do it for hydrogen burning in oxygen. Write what you built as an
equation, and then look at what your models force you to write:

$$\ce{2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O}$$

You cannot build one water molecule from one hydrogen molecule and one
oxygen molecule without an oxygen atom left over in your hand. The
coefficients are not a convention somebody imposed — they are the only
way to write down what the models let you do.[^1]

[^1]: The argument is old and it was won by weighing. Antoine Lavoisier
    pressed the case in the 1770s on the strength of careful
    measurements in closed vessels, at a time when reactions were
    routinely run in open dishes and mass appeared to come and go.
    Sealing the vessel was the whole insight.

## What to bring to the consolidation discussion

- Both masses, both changes, and the balance's resolution.
- One sentence saying what the difference between the sealed and open
  runs measures, in grams.
- Your atom-count table, and the equation your models forced.
- If your sealed run did **not** come out unchanged: your best account
  of where the discrepancy came from, ranked by how much of it each
  cause could explain.

## What you should not claim

- **An open beaker losing mass is not mass being destroyed.** It is
  mass leaving through the top of the beaker. Say the second thing.
- **A sealed run coming out unchanged does not prove conservation for
  all reactions**, or even for this one at a different scale. It is one
  reaction, at one size, on one balance.
- **A tiny drop in the sealed reading is probably not a leak.** An
  inflated bag pushes aside more air than a flat one, and a balance
  feels that as a slightly smaller reading. This is worth arguing about
  before anybody writes "the seal failed" — the effect is real, it is
  small, and it points the same way a leak does, which is precisely why
  it is worth naming.
- **Your models are not molecules.** Plastic spheres of the right
  colours settle what the *counting* requires. They settle nothing
  about shape, size, spacing, or what actually holds atoms together.
