The hard rights cases in Canada are almost never a right against no right.
They are a right against another right, held by someone equally entitled
to it. This investigation is about how those get resolved, and about the
fact that somebody loses.

## The question

**When two protected rights conflict, how does a Canadian court decide,
and on what evidence?**

You are not being asked which side you like. You are being asked to
reconstruct the reasoning, and then to say whether it holds.

## The conflicts

| One right | Against | Where it comes up |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Freedom of expression | Equality, and protection from hate | Speech that attacks a group; what a school or platform may restrict |
| Freedom of religion | Equality | Whether a service, a job, or a public role may carry religious conditions |
| Freedom of peaceful assembly | Freedom of movement, and public order | Protests that block a road, a bridge, a rail line, or a border crossing |
| Liberty and security of the person | Public safety | Detention, bail, search, and public health measures |
| Privacy | The public's right to know | Access to records about how a decision was made |
| Aboriginal and treaty rights under section 35 | Provincial regulation | Harvesting, resource development, and the duty to consult |

Every row is live in Canada now. Each has been to court more than once.

## How to work one

**Establish the facts first.** Read the decision's fact section before
anything else — see [[Court Decisions]]. Most disagreement about a case
turns out to be disagreement about what happened.

**Name both rights precisely**, with their section of the Charter, or
section 35, or the statute. "Free speech" is not precise enough to
reason with.

**Find the government's justification.** Under section 1 the government
must show the limit is prescribed by law and demonstrably justified —
what evidence did it bring, and did the court accept it? Look for the
less-restrictive-alternative step; it is where governments most often
lose.

**Say who bore the cost.** Somebody's right gave way. Name them, and say
what it cost them in practice, not in principle.

**Consider international conventions and global parallels.** Does an
international convention — such as the *Universal Declaration of Human
Rights* (UDHR) or the *UN Convention on the Rights of the Child* — speak to
this conflict, and how do international institutions respond when such rights
are violated abroad? (See [[Rights, and What Limits Them]]).

**Then judge it.** Apply the concepts of political thinking: why is this
decision politically significant, whose perspective prevailed, and how does the
ruling affect legal stability in Canada? Answer in a sentence someone could
disagree with, and give the strongest objection to your own answer immediately
afterwards.

## What is handed in

Two pages. One reconstructs the reasoning and international context; one gives
your judgement and its strongest objection. Cite the decision by neutral
citation, and cite the Charter section by number.

> [!warning] Two ways this goes wrong every year
> The first is deciding the case on sympathy for a party and then
> assembling reasons. The second is treating an outcome you dislike as
> proof the court was biased — the reasons are published precisely so
> you can attack them specifically, and "the judges were biased" is the
> one criticism that requires no reading.

[[Rights, and What Limits Them]] is the ground for this, and the argument
continues in [[When Should a Right Give Way|When Should a Right Give Way?]] before it is assessed in
[[The Rights Case]].
