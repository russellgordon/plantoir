A symbol or theme in a novel almost never means the same thing on page 20
that it means on page 200. It develops — and a thesis built around that
development is stronger than a thesis built around what the symbol means
just once. This page is a tool for catching the change while you're still
reading, so you're not scrambling to reconstruct it from memory once the
novel is finished.

## Why "it symbolizes X" isn't enough

Saying "ice symbolizes freedom in *Indian Horse*" is a start, not a
thesis — it treats the symbol as fixed, the same thing on every page.
[[Symbolism and Motif]] already covers how a motif accumulates meaning
through repetition; this page is about catching that accumulation while
you read, one appearance at a time, so you can eventually make a claim
about *how* the meaning shifts rather than just what it is.

## The tracker

Every time the ice or hockey motif appears in a chapter, add a row. Do
this in your reading journal — it costs two minutes and saves you from
flipping back through the whole novel later.

| Where it appears | What's happening | What's changed since last time |
|---|---|---|
| Ch. 3 — the frozen slough behind Saul's grandmother's cabin | Saul first watches the game, alone, before he's allowed to play | Ice is a place of freedom and wonder — pure possibility |
| St. Jerome's rink | Saul is finally permitted to play, and it becomes his one form of control inside an institution built to strip it from him | Ice is now survival — the one place his life still belongs to him |
| The Moose game | Saul's skill draws praise and attention he didn't ask for, alongside racism from opposing crowds | Ice is no longer private — it's public, and the public isn't safe |
| Late in the novel | Saul stops playing entirely | The symbol has inverted: the thing that once meant freedom now carries what he's trying to outrun |

## From tracker to thesis

Once you have four or five rows, look at the "what's changed" column as
its own short story. That column, read top to bottom, is your argument
already forming: ice moves from freedom, to survival, to danger, to
avoidance. A thesis built from that pattern makes a claim about the whole
novel's shape, not just one scene:

> In *Indian Horse*, Wagamese uses the shifting meaning of ice — from
> freedom, to survival, to danger — to trace how the same skill that once
> protected Saul becomes inseparable from the harm he's trying to escape.

That's a claim a reader could push back on, which is what
[[Building a Thesis Statement]] says a real thesis needs to be — and
it's a thesis nobody could write from a single scene.
