Once you have written a dozen electron configurations by hand, as you
did after [[Atomic Structure and Orbitals]], a suspicion sets in. The
filling order is not something you have to remember separately. It is
printed on the wall of every chemistry room in the country.

The periodic table is shaped the way it is **because** of the subshells.
That is not a coincidence to be noted and moved past; it is the reason
Mendeleev's arrangement, built from properties long before anyone knew
about electrons, turned out to be a map of electron structure.

## The shape of the table is the capacity of the subshells

Look at the widths.

| Block | Columns wide | Subshell filling there | Capacity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $s$ | 2 | $ns$ | 2 |
| $p$ | 6 | $np$ | 6 |
| $d$ | 10 | $(n-1)d$ | 10 |
| $f$ | 14 | $(n-2)f$ | 14 |

The $d$ block is ten columns wide because a $d$ subshell holds ten
electrons. Nothing else about the table's shape needs explaining once
that lands.

Two offsets in the middle column of that table are worth pausing on. In
period 4, the $d$ subshell being filled is $3d$ — one behind the period
number — which is the $4s$-before-$3d$ business from
[[Atomic Structure and Orbitals#Where the rules bend]] showing up as
geometry. The $f$ block is two behind, for the same reason one step
further out.

## What each block is like, and why

**The $s$ block** — groups 1 and 2. One or two electrons in an outer
$s$ orbital, weakly held, easily lost. These are soft, low-melting,
strongly reducing metals that form cations of a single fixed charge,
$+1$ or $+2$, and never anything else. There is no choice available to
them: once the outer $s$ electrons are gone, the next one has to come
out of a complete noble-gas core, which costs far more energy than any
chemistry will supply.

**The $p$ block** — groups 13 to 18. This block contains every non-metal,
the metalloids along the staircase, and several metals, which makes it
the least uniform block on the table. What its members share is an outer
$p$ subshell filling towards $np^6$, and the closer an element is to
completing it, the harder it pulls on other atoms' electrons. That
gradient is electronegativity, and it is why the right-hand side of the
$p$ block does all the interesting bonding in
[[Intermolecular Forces]]. At the far edge the subshell is complete, and
the noble gases are unreactive because there is nothing left to gain and
too much to lose.

**The $d$ block** — the transition metals. Hard, dense, high-melting,
good conductors, and different from the $s$ block in one decisive way:
they show **several oxidation states**. Iron gives up two electrons or
three; manganese has a wider range still. The reason is exactly the
energy overlap from the previous page — with $4s$ and $3d$ so close
together, removing a different number of electrons costs about the same,
so more than one ion is chemically reachable. Their compounds are
frequently coloured and they are heavily used as catalysts, which is
why they will reappear in [[Collision Theory and Catalysts]].

**The $f$ block** — the lanthanides and actinides, pulled out and printed
below to keep the page a sensible width. An inner $f$ subshell is
filling, buried under two outer shells, so it barely affects the
chemistry — which is why the lanthanides are famously similar to one
another and famously hard to separate.

## Reading a configuration off the table

The procedure is mechanical once you see it. Start at hydrogen and walk
along the rows in reading order, stopping at your element. Each block
you pass through contributes its subshell, filled as far as you went.

For an element in the $s$ or $p$ block, the subshell number is the
period number. In the $d$ block, subtract one. In the $f$ block,
subtract two.

Selenium sits in period 4, in the $p$ block, four columns in:

$$1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6\,3s^2\,3p^6\,4s^2\,3d^{10}\,4p^4$$

Or, with the previous noble gas standing in for everything up to argon,
$[\ce{Ar}]\,4s^2\,3d^{10}\,4p^4$ — which is the form worth writing,
because the valence electrons are then the only thing on the page and
the valence electrons are what react.

> [!tip] Two counts that are the same count
> A main-group element's group number and its number of valence
> electrons are two readings of one fact. Oxygen is in group 16 and has
> six valence electrons; chlorine is in group 17 and has seven. That is
> the number you feed into a Lewis structure, and it is the number the
> $p$-block column position is *telling* you. Counting valence electrons
> by looking them up in a table you could have read off the wall is a
> habit worth dropping this month.

> [!warning] The block is not the whole story
> A block tells you which subshell filled last. It does not always tell
> you how the element behaves.
>
> **Zinc** sits in the $d$ block, but its $d$ subshell is full in the
> atom and stays full in its compounds. It shows one oxidation state,
> its compounds are colourless, and it does not behave like its
> neighbours — which is why many chemists decline to call it a
> transition metal at all. **Helium** is printed in the $p$ block, above
> neon, because it belongs with the noble gases by chemistry, but its
> configuration is $1s^2$ and it has no $p$ electrons whatsoever.
>
> Both cases are the same lesson. The table is an argument about
> properties that turned out to be an argument about electrons, and
> where the two disagree, the properties are why the column was drawn
> there.
