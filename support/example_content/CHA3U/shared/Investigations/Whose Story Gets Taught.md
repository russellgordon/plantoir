Every country teaches a version of itself, and the United States argues about
its version in public more loudly than most. This investigation treats that
argument as its subject: not which story is nicer, but how a national story
gets assembled, who assembles it, what it has to leave out to work, and what
the leaving out is evidence of.

You are well placed to do this, because you are studying American history
from outside it, in a course written by another country's ministry.[^ours]

[^ours]: That includes this course. The expectations you see in the
    curriculum blocks were chosen by an Ontario committee and printed in
    2015, and they name some groups and events and not others. Comparing
    what this course asks you to study with what a textbook published in
    one American state asks is a legitimate and rather good source
    comparison.

## What counts as a source here

**Textbooks and curricula**, from more than one decade and more than one
place. Compare their tables of contents before you read a word of the prose:
what gets a chapter, what gets a paragraph, and what appears only as a cause
of something else.

**Monuments, museums, parks and holidays**, which are arguments in physical
form, each with a date of installation and a body that paid for it. The
installation date is often more revealing than the event commemorated.

**Public disputes over teaching**, which are themselves historical documents
about the moment they occur in.

**The groups doing the telling.** The curriculum asks how various groups have
contributed to identity and heritage, and how religion and spirituality have
shaped them. Churches, congregations and religious organisations have been
among the most persistent tellers of American history — in schooling, in
publishing, and in the civil rights movement's own account of itself — and
they belong in this inquiry as sources rather than as background.

## The method

Choose one episode from this course that you have studied properly. Find
three accounts of it aimed at a general audience, from three different
decades or three different institutions. Then use
[[Interpretation and Historiography]]: identify whether they disagree about
what happened, what caused it, or what it meant, and account for the
pattern by what each teller needed the past to do.

Say what your own account leaves out. Every selection has a cost, including
yours, and naming it is the difference between doing this analysis and
merely performing it.

## What the class produces

A short paper with a defended claim about how one episode's telling has
changed, plus a one-page appendix comparing the three accounts side by side.
It is direct preparation for [[The Long Argument]], and the judgement rests
on [[Historical Significance]] — whose criteria you must state before you
rank anything.
