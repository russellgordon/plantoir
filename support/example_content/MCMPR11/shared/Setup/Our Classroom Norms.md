This course asks you to break things, be confused in public, and tackle problems rooted in 
our communities. That takes norms — and they bind everyone in the room, including your teacher.

> [!danger] The core agreement
> Nobody's half-working program gets laughed at. Every program you have ever admired was a 
> broken program first, usually hundreds of times.

## What we agree to

- **Predict before you run.** Saying out loud what code will do — and being wrong — is the 
  fastest way to learn to read it.
- **Bugs are information.** An error message is the computer being as helpful as it knows 
  how to be. We read it before we groan at it.
- **Drivers swap.** In pair work the keyboard changes hands on the timer, every time, with 
  no exceptions for "but I am faster".
- **Your accounts are yours; theirs are theirs.** Nobody uses a machine somebody else is 
  signed into, and you lock your screen when you step away.
- **Honest about help.** A classmate's idea, a forum answer, an AI assistant's suggestion — 
  all fine WHEN NAMED, in a comment. Passing off borrowed or generated work as your own 
  thinking is the one unforgivable bug. See [[Digital Citizenship, Etiquette, and Online Literacy]] 
  for where the line actually sits.
- **Data has owners.** When working with community data, especially Indigenous data, we 
  respect the principles of OCAP® (Ownership, Control, Access, and Possession). We don't 
  scrape what isn't ours, and we treat sensitive community information with respect.
- **We argue with ideas, never at people.** That governs every discussion, especially the 
  uncomfortable ones.

## Why this is not just being nice

A room that shares half-broken programs learns faster, because problems get found while 
they are still cheap to fix. That is precisely how professional software teams work. None of 
the above is a school rule with a technology coat of paint; they are the working norms of 
the field, learned early.
