Every task page in this course publishes its success criteria on launch
day, in a table, before anybody has written a line. Almost everyone reads
that table once and never opens it again — and then hands in work that
misses two rows they could have mended in a quarter of an hour. This page
is the quarter of an hour.

We run it together the first time, on a coaster profile of mine, so that
the move is not new to you on the day the work being judged is yours.

It is a different job from [[Checking Your Own Work]], which asks whether
your answers are *right*. This one asks whether your work does what the
task said it had to do — a question that entirely correct mathematics can
still fail.

## The routine

1. Put the criteria table beside the actual work — the page itself, not
   your memory of it.
2. Go down it row by row. Write **yes**, **not yet**, or **partly**
   against each, and one sentence of evidence: the graph, the line, the
   number a reader could point at. "Yes" with nothing to point at is a
   hope wearing a tick's clothing.
3. Find your **weakest** row. Not the quickest to mend — the weakest.
   They are almost never the same row.
4. Write one sentence naming what you will change, and when.
5. Change it, in what is left of this period or in the next one. That is
   why this is never scheduled on a hand-in day: a judgement you have no
   time to act on is only a worry with a table attached.

Steps 2 and 3 are the whole exercise. Naming your weakest row honestly is
harder than any of the mending, because it means writing down the thing
you have been quietly hoping nobody would look at.

> [!example]- One row, judged honestly
> From [[Safe Concentration]]: *"A measured misfit — model-versus-data
> disagreement stated in mg/L."*
>
> **Partly.** My curve sits almost on the data for the first six hours
> and drifts above it after that, and what my write-up actually says is
> "the fit is good". That is a feeling, not a measurement, and "good" is
> exactly the word [[What Makes a Model Good]] tells me to distrust.
> Weakest row. **Change:** read the vertical gap at every hour in the
> table, take the largest, write it in mg/L, and say which hour it
> happens at — and then say whether a gap that size could move either
> edge of my window. This period.
>
> That entry is worth more than **yes** written five times, and it took
> less time to produce.

## Why this is safe to be honest in

Your judgement of your own work is not part of your mark, and neither is
a classmate's — [[How Marks Work]] says so, and it is Ontario policy
rather than a preference of mine. That cuts both ways: nothing you admit
here can cost you anything, which is precisely what makes it worth
admitting.

What it changes is the work. One self-check that leads to one real fix is
the cheapest improvement available in this course, and by the last unit
most people run it without being asked. That is the actual point.

## The two rows that go wrong most

Check these first, every time.

- **The row about restrictions, domains and asymptotes.** It is the row
  people mark **yes** from memory, because they know the rule. Knowing
  the rule is not the same as having written the restriction on the page,
  and in this course the restriction is usually where the mathematics
  lives — the hour your dose model cannot describe, the value your
  identity is silent at, the interval your profile never visits.
- **The row that ties the mathematics back to the situation.** Letters
  explained in metres and hours, a parameter tied to something you can
  hear, an interval turned into advice a person could act on. Work that
  is correct and untranslated reads as finished to whoever wrote it and
  as unfinished to everybody else.

And one general warning: the row you have been calling "basically fine"
is the row you are avoiding. Start there once, and you will notice how
reliably that is true.
