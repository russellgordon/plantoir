Anyone can find information. The skill worth having is deciding what to
believe, and it is a set of habits rather than an instinct.

At this level you will use secondary sources — criticism, interviews,
history — in [[The Independent Study]] and in the non-fiction unit. The
question is never "is this a good source" in the abstract; it is "good
for what claim".

## Where to look, and what each place is good for

| Where | Good for | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Your library's databases | Articles somebody edited before publishing | You need the login; ask the librarian once and you have it for five years |
| Government and agency sites | Numbers, laws, official positions | Written to be defensible, not to be clear |
| News organisations with a corrections policy | What happened, and when | Headlines overstate; read past them |
| Books | Depth, and an argument developed over 200 pages | Slower, and sometimes out of date |
| Wikipedia | Orientation, and the reference list at the bottom | Never cite it; use it to find what to cite |
| Social video and posts | What people are saying | No editing, no correction, no accountability |

## The five questions

In order, and the first failure ends it:

1. **Who made this?** A name, an organisation, a person who can be found.
2. **What is the evidence?** Numbers, documents, interviews — or nothing.
3. **When?** A 2015 statistic about the internet is a historical document.
4. **Who benefits if I believe it?** Not proof of a lie; a reason to look
   harder.
5. **Does anyone independent agree?** Three articles can be one press
   release wearing three hats. Follow each claim to where it started.

## The lateral move

The fastest check is not to read the page harder. It is to leave the page
and search for the organisation that made it. Fact-checkers do this
constantly, and it takes about ninety seconds.

> [!warning] What makes a fake convincing
> Not bad spelling. Real design, a plausible name, a real photograph used
> out of context, and a claim that flatters what you already believe. The
> last one is the dangerous part — the sources you should check hardest
> are the ones you most want to be true. That is what
> [[Whose Reading Counts]] is a whole discussion about.

## AI answers

A language model will produce a fluent paragraph with invented sources in
it, and the invented sources look exactly like real ones. If you use one
to orient yourself, you must then find and read the real source. In this
course, an unchecked citation is treated the same as a made-up one.
