These questions follow [[Interviewing]] and the session where you worked
to [[Record a Clean Interview]]. Good questions open doors; the best
ones make the next question obvious.

## Questions

1. Open this closed question up: "Did you enjoy the tournament?"
2. Open this one up too: "Are you nervous about opening night?"
   What makes it worse than question 1's?
3. Your source, a Grade 12 co-captain, just said: "It was hard at
   first, but the team got me through it." Write two follow-up
   questions that dig into that answer instead of moving past it.
4. **Fix this question.** "Don't you think the new bell schedule is
   unfair?" Name what is wrong with it, then rewrite it.
5. You are interviewing a nervous Grade 9 student about the chess
   club. Put these in order and defend the order: (a) What do you
   wish more people knew about the club? (b) Your name and grade?
   (c) How did you first get involved? (d) Some members say the club
   might lose its room — what have you heard? (e) What does a
   typical meeting look like?
6. **Challenge.** There is a question worth asking at the end of
   nearly every interview. What is it, and why does it work?
7. **From the shop.** For [[The Sign Shop]] you are designing a sign
   for the library door, and your user is a Grade 9 student who wears
   thick glasses. Write three questions that find out what the sign
   must do for *them* — without asking them to design it for you.

## Answers

> [!success]- Answer 1
> Anything that cannot be answered in one word: "What will you
> remember about the tournament?" or "Walk me through the final
> point." Open questions hand the source the microphone.

> [!success]- Answer 2
> "What is going through your head as opening night gets close?"
> The original is worse because it *suggests* its answer — say yes
> and you sound scared, say no and you sound scripted.

> [!success]- Answer 3
> Follow the two vague words: "Hard how — what was the worst week?"
> and "Who on the team got you through it, and what did they do?"
> A follow-up chases the specific hiding inside the general.

> [!success]- Answer 4
> It is a leading question — it announces the reporter's opinion and
> invites agreement, so whatever the source says is contaminated.
> Neutral version: "How has the new bell schedule changed your day?"

> [!success]- Answer 5
> One defensible order: b, e, c, a, d — but any order obeying the
> principle works: easy, factual questions first so a nervous source
> hears themselves succeeding; feelings and wishes in the middle;
> the hard, newsy question (d) only after trust is built. Opening
> with (d) is the one clearly wrong answer.

> [!success]- Answer 6
> "Is there anything I should have asked you about?" It works
> because the source knows their world better than your question
> list does — and because it hands over control, which is when
> nervous people finally say the interesting thing.

> [!success]- Answer 7
> Something like: "Walk me through finding a room you have never been
> to before — what do you look for first?", "Where do you usually stand
> when you read a sign on a door?", and "Tell me about a sign that let
> you down." Each asks about their experience, not their taste, and the
> answers become your criteria — the distance to test at, the height,
> the size of the letters. "What colour do you want?" asks them to do
> your job.
