Everything this newsroom and its shop do, somebody in this province is
paid to do today. The routes into that work are more varied than "go
and study journalism", and several of them start before you leave
school.

## Where the work is

| Field | What the day looks like | What it needs from this course |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Journalism and editing | Finding, checking, and writing what happened | Interviewing, verification, deadlines |
| Photography and photojournalism | Being in the right place with the right settings | [[Making Photographs]] and a portfolio |
| Videography and editing | Shooting and cutting to a story, on schedule | [[Shooting Video]], the five-shot sequence, an NLE |
| Audio production and podcasting | Clean sound, and structure that holds attention | [[Clean Audio]] and interviewing |
| Graphic design | Type, layout, and identity across media | [[Typography]] and [[Design Principles]] |
| Web and interactive | Publishing systems, accessibility, and speed | [[Publish to the Web]] |
| Communications and public relations | Explaining an organisation to the public | Writing, audience, and judgement |
| Live production and events | Cameras, sound, and lighting, with no second take | The studio work, and calm |
| Printing and press operation | Colour, stock, and machines that must run | The print side of [[The Front Page]] |
| Animation and motion graphics | Making type and images move with intent | Design principles, plus patience |

Several of those are apprenticeships or college programmes rather than
degrees, and the pay in the technical trades of this industry —
printing, broadcast engineering, live production — is often better and
steadier than the reporting jobs students imagine first.

## Beyond the newsroom

This course reaches across more than one technology area, and so do the
careers it points at. The shop unit was a taste of several at once:

| Area | Trades and careers | What they share with this course |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Manufacturing | General Machinist (429A), Tool and Die Maker (430A) — both Ontario trades | Measuring to hundredths of a millimetre, tolerances, working to a drawing — [[The Rig]] |
| Construction and electrical | Electrician — Construction and Maintenance (309A) | Safe work first, reading the documents, testing before trusting |
| Industrial maintenance | Industrial Mechanic Millwright (433A) | Keeping machines running — the maintenance step every shop day ends with |
| Design and prototyping | Industrial designer, CAD technician, additive manufacturing (3D printing) technician | Modelling, prototyping, and refining to a specification |

The numbers in brackets are the trade codes Skilled Trades Ontario uses;
its website lists every trade, what the work involves, and how long
the apprenticeship runs.

Technology changes these jobs as well as creating them. Pick one row
from the table and look it up on the federal government's Job Bank,
which reports the wages and the job outlook for that work in Ontario —
on a scale that runs from "very good" to "very limited". Then ask
the question the shop unit
raised: does a tool like the 3D printer or the laser cutter remove
work in this field, create new work, or change what the work is — and
who gains and who loses, here and in the places where the parts used
to be made? Write your answer in three sentences with the numbers
you found.

People from this country have been building these fields for a century.
Reginald Fessenden, born in Quebec, was one of the first to send a
human voice by radio. Elsie MacGill, the first woman in Canada to earn
a degree in electrical engineering, went on to oversee the production of
fighter aircraft in Thunder Bay during the Second World War. More of
them are in [[Made in Canada]].

## Three routes in, compared

The same interest can lead through very different doors, and they cost
different amounts of time and money:

| Route | How long | What it costs | Paid while you learn? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Apprenticeship | Several years, mostly on the job — General Machinist's training standard is 8,000 hours | Modest fees for the blocks of in-school training, and your own tools over time | Yes: an apprentice is an employee |
| College diploma | Usually two or three years | Tuition every year, plus equipment; OSAP grants and loans can help | Only during co-op terms |
| University degree | Usually four years | Higher tuition, plus living costs if you move away | Only during co-op terms, where a program has them |

None of these is the "smart" one. An apprentice may finish with no debt
and a licence; a graduate may finish with a degree and a loan; plenty
of people do one and then another. Guidance has this year's real
figures — ask for them before you choose Grade 11 courses.

## The Essential Skills employers name

Ontario's Skills Passport lists the Essential Skills this industry
hires on, and this course practises every one of them:

| Essential Skill | Where you did it |
| --- | --- |
| Reading | Sources, style guides, releases |
| Writing | Every story, every caption |
| Document use | Layouts, shot lists, run sheets |
| Numeracy | [[Doing the Numbers]] |
| Oral communication | [[Interviewing]] and pitching |
| Computer use | The whole production pipeline |
| Thinking — problem solving, decision making, planning | Every deadline you met |
| Working with others | Every issue this newsroom published |

Copy the table into your notebook and mark each row for yourself —
**strength**, **growing**, or **not yet** — with the piece of work that
proves it. That is the list an employer will ask about, judged by the
person who knows the evidence best.

## The work habits, which get people hired and kept

The Skills Passport names these separately, and editors will tell you
they matter more than talent: **reliability**, **organisation**,
**working safely**, **teamwork**, **initiative**, **customer service**,
and **entrepreneurship**. In a newsroom that means filing on time,
labelling your files, backing up before you are asked, and telling
somebody early when a story is not going to happen.

Your [[Newsroom Journal]] is the evidence for all of it. Claims are
cheap in an interview; a portfolio and a record of what you actually
delivered are not.

## If you would be the only one in the room

Some students look at a camera crew, a press room, or a live-production
booth and see nobody who looks like them. There are programs built to
change that, and they are free through your school:

- **Skills Ontario** runs competitions in graphic design, photography,
  and video production, plus Young Women's Conferences and Indigenous
  youth programs.
- **OYAP**, the Ontario Youth Apprenticeship Program, starts
  apprenticeship hours while you are still in secondary school.
- **Co-operative education** turns a workplace placement into credits,
  and **dual credit** programs let you take a college course that counts
  towards your diploma and a college credential at once.
- A **Specialist High Skills Major** (SHSM) bundles your Grade 11 and
  12 courses, co-op, and certifications around one sector — information
  and communications technology, manufacturing, and construction among
  them — and marks it on your diploma.
- **College pre-apprenticeship and access programmes** offer
  tuition-free routes with the supports built in.
- **Mentorship networks** exist in journalism and design specifically
  for students from communities under-represented in them. Ask me and I
  will find you a name; people in this industry remember who answered
  their own first email.

Talk to me or to guidance before Grade 11 course selection — several of
these have application windows that close early.
