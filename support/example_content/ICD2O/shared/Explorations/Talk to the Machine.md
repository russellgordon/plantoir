Today you interview an AI chatbot — not to use it, but to *study* it.
The assignment is symmetrical and strange: come back with three
things it does astonishingly well, and three ways you made it
confidently wrong. Both halves are required, because the machine's
strangest property is that its brilliance and its nonsense arrive in
exactly the same fluent, assured voice.

## The task

In pairs, run a structured interview and keep a lab record: every
prompt you tried, what came back, and your verdict. For the three
strengths, push until you are genuinely surprised. For the three
failures, "I don't know" does not count — you are hunting for
answers that are *wrong and confident at once*, and you must be able
to show the class evidence of why the answer is wrong.

> [!question]- Getting started (click to expand)
> - For strengths: ask for the same explanation three ways — for a
>   six-year-old, as a poem, in one sentence. Transformation is
>   where it tends to shine.
> - For failures: ask about things you know deeply and it cannot —
>   yesterday's practice, your town's back streets, the score of a
>   game that has not happened yet.
> - Ask it to cite sources for something it told you — then actually
>   check one.

## What tends to surface

The tone never changes. Right or wrong, the answer arrives polished,
confident, and grammatical — which means fluency is no evidence of
truth. That asymmetry sets the terms for using these tools at all:
wonderful where you can check the output, hazardous where you
cannot. [[Automation and Artificial Intelligence]] digs into why,
and what to do about it fuels [[Will AI Take the Jobs]].

## An extension

Swap failure lists with another pair and try to reproduce their
results. Some failures vanish on the second try — which raises the
harder question of what it means to test a machine that does not
answer the same way twice.

> No verdict on AI is printed here. The class assembles its own
> picture from the evidence every pair brings back — and the picture
> will not match last year's, because the machines will have changed.
