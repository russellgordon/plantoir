**Launched in the first week and collected at four points across the
term.** Individual. Your field notebook, four maps with their reflections,
and one closing entry on the skills the whole thing built.

This is the one piece of assessed work that is not finished in a
fortnight. It is where the course's continuity lives: the profile you
surveyed in September is the baseline for the hazard map you draw in the
culminating task, and neither is worth much without the other.

## What is collected

**[[Your Field Notebook]]** — every field day, kept in the two columns,
with the header block filled in before you observe anything. Four field
days feed it: the shoreline in the first weeks, the woodlot at the end of
Unit 1, the farm in Unit 3, and the shoreline again in Unit 4.

**[[A Map of Your Own]]** — the field sketch, the topographic
interpretation with its watershed and profile, the thematic or overlay
map built from published layers, and the hazard map. Each arrives with
its own short reflection.

**One closing entry**, written in the last week, on
[[Skills You Are Building]]: two skills you can evidence with specific
work from this course, one occasion outside this classroom where you used
something from it, and one career you now know enough about to say why it
does or does not interest you.

## What makes it evidence rather than a folder

1. **Your own raw data, organised so somebody else could use it.**
   Readings, sketches, quotations and photograph numbers, in the order
   you took them, with the conditions they were taken under.
2. **Other people's evidence chosen and placed beside it** — the gauge
   record, the climate normals, the soil survey, the regulated-area
   mapping, and at least one account from somebody who works the ground,
   which your farm-visit pages already hold. An agency and a farmer
   describing the same field are two perspectives, and a portfolio that
   carries only one of them is arguing with itself. A portfolio built
   only from your own measurements has half the picture; one built only
   from published layers has none of the part nobody else has.
3. **Interpretation, not collection.** A profile plotted to scale, a
   watershed delineated and defended, a classified thematic map with the
   method named, a change measured between two dates. The tools are on
   [[Reading a Topographic Map]] and [[Using a Web GIS]]; the point of
   them is that they turn a record into an argument.
4. **A source line on everything, including your own fieldwork.** Every
   layer, every figure and every quotation carries who made it, when it
   was made, and — for anything retrieved — when you took it. Your own
   data is cited too: your name, the date, and the station.
5. **The reflection beside each map**, written at the milestone rather
   than reconstructed afterwards.

## How it is marked

| Criterion | What earns the marks |
| --- | --- |
| Knowledge | The record says what was measured, where, when, and under what conditions |
| Thinking | Primary and secondary sources are chosen for a stated question, and the choice is defensible |
| Application | Maps and profiles interpret the data — classified, delineated, measured — rather than display it |
| Communication | Every layer, figure and quotation carries its publisher and its date, your own included |
| The closing entry | Two skills, one occasion outside this classroom, and one career — each with specific work or a specific event attached, not an adjective |

Read down this list before each of the four collection points. Copy it
into the front of your notebook if that is easier than opening the site
at the shoreline in a cold wind.

- [ ] Header block complete on every field page, written before observing
- [ ] Observation and inference in separate columns, and they stay separate
- [ ] Every map carries title, legend, scale bar, north arrow, sources, dates
- [ ] Classification method named wherever data was classified
- [ ] A reflection beside every map, written on the day it was submitted

## Written in class, and why

The marked parts of this portfolio are written where I can see them
happen. Field pages are written in the field and finished in the first
ten minutes of the next class. Each map's reflection is written in the
last part of the period it is submitted in. The closing entry is written
in class in the final week.

That is not a rule about trust. Ontario is clear that ongoing homework —
the reading, the drafting, the practice you do to get ready for the next
class — is never the thing that carries a mark, and that assessed work
should be done with a teacher in the room. Drafting at home is expected
and useful; it just is not what is being judged.

> [!tip] Collection points, so nothing arrives in a rush
> The field sketch is handed in with its reflection the day after the
> first shoreline visit. The topographic interpretation is submitted
> midway through Unit 2, the thematic map near the end of Unit 3 with a
> notebook check beside it,
> the hazard map during the culminating task, and the whole set —
> notebook, four maps, closing entry — the day after the assessment is
> delivered. The Unit 3 check is the useful one: it is the last point at
> which there is still a field day left to repair a gap in.

[[Judging Your Own Work]] is the method for reading your own portfolio
against the criteria above before you hand any of it in.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 3, Day 8, the farm visit, while the farmer is answering
  Watch for: who writes the answer down while it is still being given,
  and who nods, walks on, and reconstructs it on the way back. Both
  notebooks come back with a quotation in them and both quotations read
  fluently; only one of them is what was actually said. That is the
  difference between primary data and a memory of primary data, and it
  is A1.2 seen rather than inferred.
  Going well: pencil moving during the answer, and a follow-up asked
  because the first answer was written down and looked at.
  Stuck: nothing written outdoors at all, and a burst of writing on the
  walk back.
  Record: a tick beside each name on the trip list as you pass them —
  writing / not writing. Thirty seconds while they move between stations.

TALK — Unit 4, Day 17, while the closing entry is being written, which
  is the one period this term where everybody is writing quietly and you
  are free to move
  Ask: "Somebody picks this notebook up in five years and wants to
  re-survey your shoreline stations. What stops them?" A strong answer
  goes to a real omission — the peg was never located, the date is on
  one page in ten, a layer has no source line. That is A1.8 heard rather
  than audited sheet by sheet, and it is the fastest read of whether the
  documentation habit took.
  Then: "Which of these skills did you find out you did not actually
  have?" A strong answer names one and says what went wrong the first
  time. A2.1 asks students to describe how geographic investigation
  develops these skills, and somebody who can name the gap can describe
  the development; somebody who says "I improved at mapping" cannot.
  Record: two columns on the portfolio checklist — the omission they
  found, and the skill they admitted. One line per student, and you have
  the period to do it in.

The product evidence is the whole set, handed in on Unit 4, Day 17. That
one arrives on its own.
