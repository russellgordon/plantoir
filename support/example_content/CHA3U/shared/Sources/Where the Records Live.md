American archives are unusually open and unusually large, which produces its
own problem: it is easy to spend an afternoon searching a catalogue of
descriptions while believing you are searching documents. Everything listed
below is free, needs no account, and was reachable in August 2026.

## The split that costs students the most time

A **catalogue** describes what an institution holds — a series, a box, a
reel — with a summary and a reference number. A **digitised collection**
gives you the page itself. The National Archives Catalog does both at once,
which is convenient and confusing: a hit may be a description of thirty
boxes in Maryland or a scan you can read now. Check before you plan.

## What you can reach from home

| Where | What it holds | What you get from home |
| --- | --- | --- |
| National Archives, Founding Documents | Declaration, Constitution, Bill of Rights | Full images and transcripts; the originals are in the Rotunda for the Charters of Freedom |
| National Archives, Milestone Documents | Key federal documents, 1754 to 1965 | Image, date and a short note for each, including the Emancipation Proclamation and Executive Order 9066 |
| National Archives Catalog | The agency's described holdings | Search across record groups; many items digitised, many not |
| 1950census.archives.gov | The 1950 population schedules | Name-searchable images of individual returns |
| DocsTeach, National Archives Foundation | Teaching activities built on federal records | 13,639 primary source documents, free, no account needed |
| Chronicling America, Library of Congress | Historic newspaper pages published through 1963 | Free full-text search of millions of pages, plus a directory of titles since 1690 |
| Census Bureau, Historical Statistics | Aggregate American statistics to 1970 | Free PDF chapters, more than 12,500 time series |
| National Park Service | Sites where events happened, with research notes | Park histories, and the Network to Freedom listing of documented Underground Railroad sites |

Two of those need a sentence of warning. The National Park Service writes for
visitors as well as researchers, so treat a park page as a starting point and
follow it to its sources. And the Network to Freedom lists places with, in
its own words, "a verifiable connection to the Underground Railroad" — the
listing is a claim about documentation, which is exactly the sort of claim
you should read the reasoning behind.

## Start with the question, not the archive

Write the question first, decide what kind of document could answer it, and
only then choose the building. A question about a law goes to the statute; a
question about a person goes to a census return or a service file; a
question about what a town believed goes to its newspaper.

Practise the walk-in itself in [[Working in an Archive]], and record every
reference number as you go — [[Citing Historical Sources]] explains why the
number you did not write down is the one you will need in January.
