## What you are making

Play two tones a few hertz apart and the room throbs — a slow pulse
musicians call *beats* and use to tune instruments. Your model is a
sum:

$$s(t) = \sin(2\pi f_1 t) + \sin(2\pi f_2 t)$$

Pick two frequencies $f_1$ and $f_2$ a few hertz apart, play them
together (any free tone generator will do), and time the throb with
a stopwatch. Then the mathematics: a sum of two sinusoids looks like
neither of them — until you rewrite it. You finish with the
**model**, a **proof** of the sum-to-product identity it leans on —
built from the compound angle formulas, every step defensible — a
**predicted beat frequency** reconciled with your stopwatch, and one
paragraph on harmony: why does an octave sound calm while two
near-identical tones fight?

## Milestones

- [ ] Frequencies chosen; the throb heard and timed before any
      algebra
- [ ] The sum graphed in [[Using Desmos]]; the envelope conjectured
      from the picture
- [ ] The identity proved from [[Compound Angles]], every equality
      defensible per [[Trigonometric Identities]]
- [ ] The identity tested first at a pair of special angles — exact
      values off [[The Unit Circle]], no calculator — because a claim
      about *every* angle deserves at least one check before you build
      on it
- [ ] Beat frequency predicted from the rewritten model and
      reconciled with the stopwatch
- [ ] The harmony paragraph written — octaves versus semitones,
      explained by your own model

## How it is assessed

Per [[How Marks Work]], the reasoning is the product: a proof with
an honest gap, found and named by its own authors, outranks a
flawless chain copied from elsewhere. On the due date your pair
plays the beats, shows the model, and defends the identity live. The
[[Math Journal]] entry on where the stopwatch and the model
disagreed completes the evidence.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like in your work |
| --- | --- |
| Ears first | The throb timed before the algebra explains it |
| A proof that holds | Each step justified, no "clearly" anywhere, and the claim tested at special angles before it was trusted |
| A tested prediction | Beat frequency predicted, measured, reconciled |
| Letters that speak | Every parameter tied to something audible |
| A musical answer | Harmony and dissonance explained by the model |
| An entry that names the disagreement | The milestone entry says where the stopwatch and the model parted company, and which one you believe |

> [!success]- If the beats will not show
> Frequencies too far apart throb too fast to count — keep them
> within about four hertz of each other. And check the window on
> your graph: a beat envelope is slow, so you may be zoomed in too
> close to see even one full pulse.

Triangulation — the evidence that never arrives on its own.

Pairs, so take them one at a time. Day 16 carries this task's conference; the
questions below are deliberately not the one printed on that agenda.

OBSERVE — Unit 3, Day 14, the trade-and-attack period

  Watch what a pair does in the ten seconds after another pair challenges one
  of their lines. There are two responses and only one of them is
  mathematics: pick a number, put it into both sides of the challenged
  equality, and see; or restate the line more firmly.

  The finished proof shows only the steps that survived. If a challenged
  line was defended by testing, the proof looks the same as if it had been
  defended by insistence — so this corroborates the criteria row about every
  step being justified, which the page can assert and only the room can
  confirm. It is also the difference between B3.3's "prove through the
  application of reasoning skills" and reciting a chain.

  Going well: somebody reaches for a specific angle unprompted, and the
  argument ends in agreement rather than in volume.

  Stuck: the same line read out a second time, louder, with the word
  "obviously" now in it.

  Record: on your class list, a tick for a challenge tested with a number and
  a cross for a challenge answered with assertion. Two crosses is a
  conversation for the next working period, not a mark.

TALK — Unit 3, Day 16, at the conference already on that agenda

  Ask: "Play both your tones an octave higher — double both frequencies.
  Does the throb speed up, slow down, or stay the same?"

  Then: "Real instruments are never exactly equally loud. If one of your two
  tones were half the amplitude of the other, would the throb still fall to
  silence? What would you hear instead?"

  The first has a wrong answer that sounds right, which is why it is worth
  asking. Doubling both frequencies doubles the GAP between them, so the
  throb doubles too — the beat rate is the difference in hertz, not a
  property of the interval as a ratio. A student who has memorised "beats
  come from tones that are close" says "the same" and is wrong; a student who
  can read their own rewritten model says which factor sets the throb and
  which sets the pitch. That is D2.2 heard: knowing which feature of the
  parts controls which feature of the sum.

  On the second, the strong answer is that the cancellation is only ever
  complete when the two amplitudes match, so with one tone at half strength
  the sound dips instead of vanishing — the throb keeps its rate and loses
  its depth. That is D2.1: a key feature of a sum traced back to a parameter
  of the pieces, and it is a question their graph can settle in thirty
  seconds if they think to try it.

  Record: one line per student on your class list. The pair's name is no use
  to you here.

The product evidence is the demonstration on Day 18, the proof handed in with
it, and the milestone entry written in class that day.
