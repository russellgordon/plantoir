## The task

One argument that needs both *Hamlet* and *The Handmaid's Tale*.

## The test your thesis must pass

Delete one text from your thesis sentence. If the claim survives, you do
not have a comparative argument yet — see [[Comparative Argument]].

## Questions that have worked

- Both texts make testimony depend on a hostile or indifferent audience.
  What does each conclude about whether the account survives?
- Both are obsessed with proof. What counts as evidence in each, and who
  gets to decide?
- Both give a protagonist a role written by somebody else. What does each
  text think refusal costs?
- Both end by handing the story to somebody who was not there.

## Requirements

- At least three passages from each text.
- Point-by-point organisation unless you can defend another shape in your
  outline.
- One paragraph where the comparison complicates your own claim.
- Attention to form: a play and a novel do not make arguments the same
  way, and that difference should appear in the essay.

## How it is marked

| Category | What I am looking for |
| --- | --- |
| Knowledge | Both texts known precisely, including the parts that resist the comparison |
| Thinking | One argument requiring both texts; the comparison does work in every paragraph |
| Communication | The reader always knows which text they are in and why |

In the workshop period your partner reads it against this table and then
you do, on your own — [[Judging Your Own Work]] — and you rewrite your
weakest comparison there and then. The gap between that period and
the hand-in is deliberate: it is where the rewriting goes.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 3, Day 11, the ten minutes in which the deletion test is
run in class on a thesis written that period.
  Watch for: whether the test is RUN or asserted. It takes a pen through
  the name of one text and a second reading of the sentence, and it is
  over in fifteen seconds — so either you see it happen or it did not.
  A thesis that would have failed it does show up in the finished essay,
  three weeks later, as a paragraph that is quietly about one book. On
  Day 11 a failed thesis costs nothing to replace. On Day 14 it costs
  the draft.
  Going well: a crossed-out name, the sentence read again, and a face
  that has just realised something.
  Stuck: a thesis written once, neatly, and underlined.
  Record: a class list with two columns, ran it and did not. Ninety
  seconds.
  Evidence for C1.2: focusing an idea for a piece of writing using a
  strategy is the expectation, and the strategy is only visible while it
  is being used.

TALK — Unit 3, Day 13, while pairs trade outlines and mark the
paragraphs that are about one text only.
  Ask: "Somebody is about to tell you these two texts are not arguing
  about the same thing at all. What do you say back?" Then, pointing at
  a body paragraph: "This one needs Hamlet to say something out loud.
  Where does the novel let Offred do that — and if it does not, what
  does it use instead?"
  A strong answer names one thing each text can do that the other
  cannot, and then says which of their own paragraphs is depending on
  it. That is B1.6 in conversation — analysing what aspects of a text
  contribute to the idea it develops — and it is the exact thing a weak
  comparative essay papers over with the word "similarly". Do not supply
  the example; the pair that has one has done the thinking, and the pair
  that repeats the worked case from the Comparative Argument page has
  not.
  Record: one line per pair on the outline-trade list, right then.

The product arrives on its own: the essay handed in on Day 21.
