The fakes in [[The Phishing Gallery]] fooled almost everyone at least
once — that was the point of the exercise. Staying secure online is
not about fear, and it is not about genius. It is a small set of
habits, practised until they are boring. Boring is the goal.

## Passwords that do their job

Length beats cleverness — three unrelated words make a stronger
password than any amount of swapping `@` for `a`. What matters even
more is reuse: when one site leaks, every account sharing that
password leaks with it. Your email deserves the strongest, most
unique password you own, because email can reset everything else.
[[The Password Checker]] had you write the rules in code; the rules
apply to you too.

## Spotting the hook

Phishing runs on urgency — *act now or your account will be closed* —
because urgency switches off checking. The habits that beat it are
unglamorous: slow down, read the actual sender address, and never
follow a link you were pushed toward. Type the site's address
yourself instead. A real organisation will survive you taking sixty
seconds to verify.

## Sharing on purpose

Every profile field, photo, and location tag is a decision, and the
audience is always bigger than the follower count — screenshots
travel. The useful question is never "is this secret?" but "who could
see this, and am I fine with that?" Privacy settings are where that
answer becomes real: who can see posts, who can find the account, and
which apps still hold permissions they no longer need.
[[Who Owns Your Data]] picks up what happens after you share.

## Habits worth building this week

- [ ] Turn on two-factor authentication — email first, then the rest
- [ ] Retire any password doing double duty across accounts
- [ ] Install that postponed update — see [[Software and Operating Systems]]
- [ ] Walk through privacy settings on the app you use most
- [ ] Sixty-second rule: verify urgent messages before acting on them
