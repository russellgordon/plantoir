> [!abstract] At a glance
> Pairs, marked individually · two periods watching together, two
> working periods, and the day it is handed in ·
> one 800-word analysis, yours alone, and one media text made by the
> pair · assessed on Thinking, Application, and Communication

## Part one: the analysis

Choose one scene from *Hamlet* or one sequence from
*The Handmaid's Tale*, and two adaptations of it. Then:

1. Build the table of differences: cuts, casting, blocking, camera,
   sound, and what is shown that the text only reports.
2. For each production, state the reading of the text it serves.
3. Identify the moment where the two productions most clearly disagree,
   and say what is at stake in the disagreement.
4. Say which reading the text better supports — with evidence from the
   page, not from the screen.

## Part two: the text you make

One of:

- **A director's concept** for the same scene: 500 words plus a shot list
  or blocking diagram, arguing for a reading;
- **A ninety-second trailer** for one of our texts, cut to argue a
  specific interpretation;
- **A short essay film or podcast segment**, three minutes, on the
  question your analysis raised.

Attach a 150-word note: your audience, your three techniques, the choice
you are least sure about, and **which parts of this you made**.

## What is yours alone

You watch together, argue together, and build the table of differences
together. After that the marks separate, because there is no group mark
in this course.

- **The 800-word analysis is written by you**, on your own, from the
  shared table. Two people who watched the same two productions should
  not hand in the same argument about them, and usually do not.
- **The made text belongs to the pair**, and your mark for it comes from
  the part you made. Your note says which part that is; I spend the
  making period watching, so the note has something to be true about.
- **The note is yours.**

## How it is marked

| Category | What I am looking for |
| --- | --- |
| Thinking | The analysis reads the productions as arguments about the text, with evidence from both media |
| Application | The part you made uses its techniques deliberately and commits to a reading |
| Communication | The analysis is clear to a reader who has seen neither production; your part of the made text does what your note says it does |

> [!important] The distinction this task exists to teach
> A production is evidence about a reading, not about the play. Getting
> that sentence into your analysis — and meaning it — is most of the
> Thinking mark.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 3, Day 19, the making period. Watch the division of
labour and nothing else.
  This one is not evidence the product hides — the note names who made
  what, so the product does report it. That is exactly why the twenty
  minutes are worth spending: a self-report on a pairs task is a claim,
  and this is the only period in which it can be checked. Without it you
  are marking an individual on a piece two people handed you and a
  sentence saying it was fair.
  Watch for: whether both pairs of hands touch the making, and which
  techniques get tried and dropped. The commonest shape is one person
  editing and the other narrating over their shoulder, and no finished
  trailer can tell you which of them cut it.
  Going well: they swap at some point; the one not making is watching
  the RESULT rather than the maker; a disagreement gets settled by
  trying both.
  Stuck: one keyboard and one spectator for the whole period; a pair
  that has divided the work as "you make it, I will write the note".
  Record: two initials per pair with a slash between them, and one word
  each for what they did. Compare it against the notes on Day 20.
  Evidence for D3.4: producing a media text using the techniques that
  suit it is the expectation. The file records WHAT was produced; only
  this period records who produced it and which techniques they reached
  for and abandoned — and on a pairs task the first of those is what
  makes an individual mark defensible.

TALK — Unit 3, Day 18, while the pairs build the table of differences.
  Ask: "Which decision in this production was made for a reason that has
  nothing to do with the text — money, a running time, an actor who was
  available — and how can you tell?" Then: "Who was this made to be
  watched by, and where? A cinema, a living room, a class like this one.
  What did that decide before anybody chose a shot?"
  A strong answer separates a circumstance from an interpretation, and
  then names something the FORM decided rather than something the
  director believed. D2.1's own example is precisely this — explaining
  why film adaptations differ from what they adapt, and describing the
  nature of the differences in a specific case.
  Deliberately not asked: anything on this page's own list of things to
  tabulate — cuts, casting, blocking, camera, sound — or the "which
  reading does the text better support" question they have already read
  here.
  Record: a line per pair in your day plan.

The products arrive on their own, on Day 20: the analysis, the made
text, and the note.
