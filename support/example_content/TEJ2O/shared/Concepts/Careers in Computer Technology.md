Every skill this course runs at the bench — assembling a machine,
crimping a cable, diagnosing a fault, reading a spec sheet — is a
thing someone in this province is paid to do today. The question is
not whether the work exists; it is which route to it fits you.

## Four routes from here

| Route              | What it looks like                        |
| ------------------ | ----------------------------------------- |
| Direct from school | Entry job plus industry certifications    |
| Apprenticeship     | Paid work plus trade-school blocks        |
| College            | Two- or three-year diploma                |
| University         | Four-year degree                          |

Direct entry leads to help-desk and repair-bench work, with
certifications earned along the way. College diplomas lead to network
administration and electronics technology; university degrees to
engineering and software development. No route outranks the others —
they lead to different rooms, and people cross between them all the
time.

## Apprenticeship, honestly

An apprenticeship means being hired and paid from day one, learning
mostly on the job with blocks of trade school in between, and
finishing as a certified journeyperson — in trades like network
cabling and electrical work that are, underneath, this course's
skills at full scale. The honest parts: you must find an employer
willing to sponsor you, which takes real effort; early wages are a
fraction of journeyperson rates, rising as you qualify; and entry
is competitive, not a consolation prize. [[The Trades Are Tech]]
takes on the tired idea that trades are what you settle for.

## Skills employers actually name

Ontario's Skills Passport lists the Essential Skills employers cite
across this industry: reading, writing, document use, numeracy, oral
communication, computer use, and thinking skills — with work habits
like reliability, safe work, and initiative beside them. Notice that
most are practised every week in this course, not least in your
[[Tech Journal]]. Claims are cheap in a job interview; evidence
travels. [[The Shop Showcase]] exists so you finish this course
holding proof of what you can do.

## Where the learning continues

Nobody in this field finishes learning, because the equipment changes
under them. The people who stay employable are the ones who keep a
habit going, and the habits are cheap:

| Source | What it gives you | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Certification programs — CompTIA A+ and Network+, Cisco's networking track | A credential an employer recognises without meeting you | Exam fees; the studying is free |
| College continuing-education courses, evenings and online | A structured term on one thing, taught by someone doing it | Modest, and often employer-paid later |
| Trade publications and vendor documentation | What changed this year | Free |
| Manufacturer training portals | The specifics of the equipment on your bench | Usually free with an account |
| Skills Ontario competitions, and the workshops around them | A deadline, a standard, and people who do this | Free through your school |
| A local user group or repair café | The thing nobody writes down | Free, plus a bus fare |

Pick one now, while you have a teacher to ask. The first certification
is far easier to start in Grade 10 than in the first month of a job.

## If you would be the only one in the room

Some of you will look at a shop or a server room and see nobody who
looks like you. That is a real thing, it is not your fault, and there
are programs built specifically to change it:

- **Skills Ontario** runs Young Women's Conferences and Indigenous
  youth programs alongside its competitions — hands-on days in trades
  and technology, at no cost through your school.
- **OYAP**, the Ontario Youth Apprenticeship Program, lets you begin
  apprenticeship hours while still in secondary school, through your
  co-op office.
- **Women in Skilled Trades and pre-apprenticeship programs** at
  Ontario colleges are tuition-free routes into a trade, with the
  supports built in rather than assumed.
- **Mentorship and virtual networks** — ask me, and I will find you
  someone in the work who will answer your questions by email. That
  request is not an imposition; people in this industry remember who
  answered theirs.

Talk to me or to guidance before Grade 11 course selection, because
some of these have application windows.
