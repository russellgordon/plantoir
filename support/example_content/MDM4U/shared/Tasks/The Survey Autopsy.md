## What you are making

An autopsy establishes cause of death. Yours establishes how much of a
published conclusion the survey underneath it can actually support.
Bring a real survey — a political poll, a customer-satisfaction form, a
school climate survey, a market-research questionnaire, a government
consultation — with its real questions in their real order, from a
source you can name. Then take it apart.

You are answering four questions in writing, and the fourth is the one
that earns the marks:

1. **Who was meant to be described?** Name the population the
   conclusion is about, in the publisher's own words.
2. **Who was actually reached?** The sampling method, the frame it
   drew from, the sample size, and the response rate if one is
   published — plus the honest note if it is not.
3. **Who was missed?** Name the group the method could not reach, and
   argue which way their absence bends the result.
4. **What did the wording push?** Quote at least three questions
   verbatim and analyse each: loaded words, a missing middle option,
   two questions folded into one, an order that primes the next
   answer.

You finish by **rewriting two of the questions** so they measure the
same thing without the push, and by writing the conclusion you believe
the data can carry — which may be considerably smaller than the one
that got published.

## Milestones

- [ ] Survey chosen and cited in full, with its published conclusion
      quoted exactly
- [ ] Population, frame, method, sample size, and response rate
      recorded — with gaps named as gaps
- [ ] The missing group identified, and the direction of the resulting
      error argued
- [ ] Three questions analysed word by word; two of them rewritten
- [ ] Your own defensible conclusion, stated in one sentence

## How it is assessed

Per [[How Marks Work]], a careful autopsy of a fairly ordinary survey
beats a hasty demolition of a famous one. Sceptical is not the same as
cynical: a survey done properly deserves you saying so. The most
common failure is stopping at "the sample was too small" — a real
finding needs the *mechanism*, meaning who was missed and why that
group would have answered differently. Your [[Math Journal]] entry on
the question you were most confident about, and were wrong about, is
worth writing while it stings.

**Working in a pair, marked as yourself.** No shared mark. Each of the
three question analyses is signed by whoever wrote it, and each of you
writes one of the two rewrites — so the report shows, on its face,
which half is yours. The rest of your mark comes from the conference
on Day 5, from your share of the five-minute presentation on Day 13,
and from what I see across the two working periods. A pair that
divides the survey in half and never reads the other half hands in two
reports stapled together, and it always shows.

Run [[Judging Your Own Work]] against the criteria table in the last
ten minutes of Day 5. Day 6 is the period that exists so the weakest
row can still be fixed.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like in your work |
| --- | --- |
| A real, cited survey | Original questions, original order, named source |
| Frame before sample | Who could be reached, then who was |
| A named absence | The missing group, and the direction of its effect |
| Wording taken seriously | Quoted, analysed, and rewritten cleanly |
| A right-sized conclusion | One sentence the data can actually carry |

> [!warning]- When the survey is one your own school ran
> Fair game, and often the most interesting choice — but the people
> who wrote it are colleagues and classmates, so the critique goes to
> the instrument, never the author. Anonymize respondents completely,
> and if any answer could identify a person, it does not appear in
> your report at all.

OBSERVE — Unit 3, Day 6, the working period on the rewrites
  The agenda asks every pair to try a rewritten question on somebody
  who has not seen the original. What you are watching for is what
  happens NEXT, in the ten seconds after the reader answers. Some
  pairs treat the answer as data and change a word. Others explain to
  the reader why the question was already fine. The rewritten question
  that reaches you at the end looks identical either way, and no row
  of the criteria table reports which one happened — this period is
  the only place it exists. It is C2.4's designing of questionnaires
  caught in the act rather than in the finished draft.
  Going well: a word crossed out within a minute of the reader
  answering, and the question tried again on a second person.
  Stuck: the reader being persuaded that they misread it.
  Record: a tick beside the pair on the class list, and the word they
  changed, in three seconds.

TALK — Unit 3, Day 5, at the conference already on that agenda
  Two questions, once their annotations are underway:
  "Who paid for this survey to be run, and which of its questions
  would they be most pleased with?"
  Then: "Read me one of the original questions out loud, the way a
  respondent hears it rather than the way you are reading it. Where
  does somebody who is tired of being surveyed stop listening?"

  that was missing, an order that primed the answer, a word that made

  about how a bias affects the results of a study rather than about

  Record: three words per pair beside their names, right then.
