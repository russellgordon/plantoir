The [[True or False]] prompt that split the room — *true or false:*
$5 - (-2) = 3$ — comes down to what a negative sign is actually doing.
Integers answer three different questions, and keeping them separate
untangles most sign confusion:

- **Position** — where are you? A balance of −\$20, a temperature of
  $-8$ °C, a parking level of $-2$.
- **Direction** — which way does the sign point? Negative means left
  on the number line, down on a thermometer, owed rather than owned.
- **Change** — how far did you move, and which way? Rising from
  $-8$ °C to $-3$ °C is a change of $+5$, even though both readings
  are negative.

## Why subtracting a negative climbs

Read $5 - (-2)$ as a question about distance and direction: how far,
and which way, from $-2$ to $5$? That journey is $7$ steps to the
right, so $5 - (-2) = 7$ — the statement above is false. Subtracting a
negative removes a debt, cancels a step backward, deletes a
below-zero. Every version of the story climbs.

> [!tip] Zero pairs
> A $+1$ and a $-1$ together make exactly nothing: a **zero pair**.
> Integer tiles work because adding or removing zero pairs never
> changes the value — which is how you can subtract $-2$ from a pile
> that has no negatives in it. First add two zero pairs (still the
> same value), then remove the two negatives. What remains has climbed
> by two.

## Where negative numbers came from, and who resisted them

Negative numbers feel obvious now, which makes it easy to assume they
always did. They did not. They arrived as an *accounting* idea, in
China, roughly two thousand years ago: the *Nine Chapters on the
Mathematical Art* worked with counting rods laid on a board, red rods
for what was owned and black rods for what was owed, and gave rules for
combining them that are the rules on this page. The rods came first and
the number came second — nobody was arguing about whether $-3$ existed,
because it was not a thing, it was a colour of stick.

Several centuries later, in India, Brahmagupta wrote the rules down as
arithmetic rather than as bookkeeping — including that a debt subtracted
from nothing becomes a fortune, which is $0 - (-2) = 2$, the same climb
this page has been about. Meanwhile European mathematicians spent
centuries calling them "absurd" or "fictitious" and throwing away
negative solutions to equations as meaningless answers, well into the
seventeenth century.

It is worth asking why the resistance ran the way it did. Where the
number was tied to something real that could be *owed*, it was accepted
early and without fuss. Where mathematics was understood as the study of
quantities and shapes — and a quantity less than nothing is not a
quantity — it was rejected for a very long time by people who were
extremely good at mathematics. What made the idea acceptable in the end
was not a proof. It was a picture: the number line, which puts the
negatives somewhere, and turns "less than nothing" into "to the left of
here".

**And the accounting reading never went away.** A bank balance, a credit
card statement, a company's books, and the plus-and-minus column on a
hockey scoresheet are all the *Nine Chapters* method with the rods
replaced by a sign — a stored value that is allowed to be less than
nothing, with rules for combining it that somebody had to invent. When
you research a number concept for [[The Math Fair]], this is the shape
to look for: who needed it, what they were counting, and what had to
change before everybody else agreed it was a number.

Signs carry the same meaning when they attach to fractions, decimals,
and rates. A rate of $-3$ L/min means the pool is draining; a slope of
$-\frac{1}{2}$ in [[Slope and Rate of Change]] means the line falls
one for every two across. The sign never changes *how much* — it
changes *which way*, in exactly the sense of
[[B3.3|the effects that positive and negative signs have]] on a value.
