## What you are making

A running record of your thinking: one short entry after every class,
written the same day while the details are warm. Ten honest minutes
beats thirty performed ones — and entries are **private**. I read
them; other students never do. The journal only works if you can admit
in it what you would not say at the boards — that the transformations
lost you the moment $a$, $k$, $d$, and $c$ all changed at once, that
rational exponents still feel like a spell you recite rather than a
thing you understand.

## How entries work

Each entry answers four prompts, in whatever order helps:

- **What we did** — the facts, briefly: the warm-up, the thinking
  task, where your group got to.
- **What clicked** — the moment something connected, and what did the
  connecting: a sketch, a classmate's phrasing, a smaller case.
- **What is foggy** — named precisely. "Functions are confusing" is
  weather; "I lose the sign on $d$ in $f(x - d)$" is a location.
- **What I would try** — one concrete move for next class. Not "focus
  more": something you could actually do.

Conjectures are welcome any time. "I think an exponential function
eventually outgrows *any* quadratic — [[Always, Sometimes, Never]]
made me want to test it" is a perfect journal sentence — dated,
testable, and yours.

## Success criteria and collection

The journal comes in at the end of each unit and is read against these
criteria — read and written back to, never scored.
[[Journal Checklist]] turns them into a self-check before you hand it
in, and [[How Marks Work]] explains the one part of the journal that
does carry a mark: the four entries written in class on task hand-in
days.

| Quality | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Regularity | An entry for every class, written close to the day |
| Honesty | Real fog named, not just victories |
| Precision | Foggy things located, not just felt |
| Growth | "What I would try" items reappear as things actually tried |
