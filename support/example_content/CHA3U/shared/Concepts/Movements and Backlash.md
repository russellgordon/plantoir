The standard account of the civil rights movement runs from a court decision
to a bus boycott to a march to two statutes, and stops. It is not wrong, but
it is short at both ends and it leaves out the part that explains the
present: every advance was answered, sometimes immediately, and some of the
answers succeeded.

## Law, and what it took to make law move

In *Brown v. Board of Education*, decided 17 May 1954, the Supreme Court held
that "separate educational facilities are inherently unequal" and that the
doctrine of separate but equal had no place in public education. A year later
it instructed states to desegregate "with all deliberate speed" — a phrase
that granted delay to anyone willing to use it, and many were. Federal troops
went to Little Rock in 1957 to enrol nine students in one high school.

What moved the law was organized pressure, carried by people who calculated
the risk in advance. The Montgomery bus boycott began with Rosa Parks's
arrest in December 1955 and ran for over a year, sustained by Black
households who walked or car-pooled to work. Parks was a trained activist and
a local NAACP secretary, not a tired seamstress who acted on impulse, and the
campaign had considered other arrests before hers. Freedom Riders were beaten
at bus stations in 1961, and in September 1963 a bomb at a Birmingham church
killed four girls.

Then the statutes. The Civil Rights Act, signed 2 July 1964, entitled all
persons to "the full and equal enjoyment of the goods, services, facilities,
and privileges" without discrimination by race, colour, religion or national
origin, and reached employment and federally funded programs. The Voting
Rights Act, signed 6 August 1965, banned tests and devices, sent federal
examiners south, and required covered jurisdictions to get approval before
changing voting procedures. By the end of 1965 a quarter of a million new
Black voters had registered, a third of them by federal examiners.

> [!question] Why then, and not earlier or later?
> Movements do not succeed because their cause becomes just; the cause was
> already just. Ask instead what changed in the conditions — wartime service
> and industrial employment, a migration that put Black voters in states
> that decided presidential elections, television carrying images of police
> dogs into living rooms, a Cold War in which American segregation was an
> international embarrassment, and decades of NAACP litigation that had
> already built the legal ground. Argue the combination, and set out your
> reasoning in [[Why Did Movements Succeed When They Did|Why Did Movements Succeed When They Did?]].

## The wider field, and the reaction

Other movements formed in the same decades and borrowed each other's methods.
Farm workers organized in California under Cesar Chavez and Dolores Huerta
and used a national grape boycott. The American Indian Movement occupied
sites of symbolic weight and forced treaty obligations into the news. The
women's movement pressed for legal equality through new organizations and the
courts. Gay and lesbian Americans moved from concealment to open political
demand after the Stonewall uprising of 1969. *Silent Spring*, published in
1962, helped convert conservation into a mass environmental politics.

Every one of them met organized opposition, and the opposition was also a
movement. School desegregation was resisted for decades by closing systems,
by relocation, and by district boundaries. The proposed Equal Rights
Amendment was defeated by a campaign led by women. National politics
reorganized around the reaction. And the Voting Rights Act's approval
requirement was narrowed by the Supreme Court in 2013, which is the clearest
demonstration available that a settlement can be undone.

Nothing here is finished, which is what makes [[Continuity and Change]] the
right tool and [[Rights and Movements]] the right place to argue it out.
