> [!abstract] At a glance
> Individual, for a real user · six shop periods after the launch, with
> one laser cutter shared by the class · one laser-cut sign, its
> cardboard prototype, and a settings log

## What you are making

A sign somebody actually needs, cut on the laser: a door sign for a
room people struggle to find, an "On air" sign for the studio, a table
sign that guides readers on [[Publication Day]]. It is designed for one
real person who is not you — your **user** — and it has to work for
them, from where they will actually stand.

The sign is 150 mm × 100 mm, cut from 3 mm plywood or cast acrylic,
with its lettering scored or cut through. A small engraved logo is
allowed; a fully engraved background is not, because it would take the
laser half a period per sign and there is one laser.

The floor is low on purpose: plain words, big letters, one clean cut,
and a user who says "yes, that is what I needed" is a complete success.
The ceiling is high too — a sign so clear that nobody asks for
directions again.

## How to work

1. **Launch (Day 4).** Pick your user and your sign, and interview them
   using the questions you wrote in [[Interview Question Practice]]:
   where will they be standing, what are they looking for, what has let
   them down before? Then write your own criteria — at least one you
   can measure, such as the distance it must be read from.
2. **Settings (Day 5).** Read the class test cards with me and set up
   your file the way [[From Sketch to Cut File]] shows. The letter
   sizes come from [[Typography]], section *Signs*.
3. **Thumbnails and material (Day 6).** Three pencil thumbnails, one
   chosen for a reason from [[Design Principles]]. Choose plywood or
   acrylic and write one sentence justifying it, using what making costs
   in [[The Footprint of Media]]. Then the checkpoint conference: bring
   your criteria, your thumbnails, and your choice.
4. **Prototype and test (Day 7).** Your prototype is cut in cardboard,
   nested four to a sheet with three classmates' — or cut by hand from a
   printed template if the laser queue is full. Take it to your user,
   stand where they will stand, and check it against every criterion.
   Write down exactly what you found, using [[Quality Control]].
5. **Revise and cut (Days 8–9).** Change the file because of what the
   test found — not for the sake of it — and cut the final sign in your
   booked slot. Log the settings you used and anything you would change.
6. **Judge it and hand it over (Day 9).** Judge your sign against the
   criteria the way [[Judging Your Own Work]] shows, read a classmate's
   at distance, and hand the finished sign to its user.

Every period of this task is class time, in the shop, with me in the
room: the work I evaluate is made here, and nothing on a homework list
is the marked thing ([[How Marks Work]]). Machines are run only by
people who have passed the sign-off in [[Safety in the Shop]], and the
laser never runs with nobody at it: whoever's file is cutting stands at
the machine for every second of the job.

## The laser's time budget

One laser, a class of about twenty-four, and seventy-five-minute
periods. The plan works because nothing is cut that does not need the
laser, and it adds up like this:

| Day | What runs on the laser | Laser minutes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5 | Three test cards, one per material, run by me as a demonstration | about 15 |
| 7 | Cardboard prototypes, four to a sheet: six sheets at about 6 minutes | about 36 |
| 8 | Final signs, two to a sheet: eight sheets at about 7 minutes, then 5 minutes of cleaning | about 60 |
| 9 | The last four sheets of final signs | about 28 |

About 140 laser minutes across four periods, and no period needs more
than an hour of the machine — the rest of the period is for designing,
testing, and conferences. The minutes assume each sign takes 4 to 5
minutes to cut and score, plus a couple of minutes to load and focus a
sheet. **Time your own laser on the Day 5 test cards and redo this table
with your numbers.** If your signs run longer, hand-cut the prototypes
and give the laser only the finals.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like in your sign |
| --- | --- |
| Built for its user | Your user can say what the sign is for from where they will stand |
| Readable at distance | Readable at the distance you and your user agreed — measured, not guessed |
| Criteria first | Your criteria were written before the prototype, and one of them is a number |
| Material justified | One sentence says why this material, including what happens to the offcuts |
| Tested, then changed | The final differs from the prototype for a reason the test found |
| A clean file | Right colours, text as paths, cut from a settings-sheet row |
| Settings logged | The row you used, what happened, and what you would change |
| Made safely | Signed off, glasses on for trimming, never away from the running laser |

A clear sign is easier to read for everybody, including readers with
low vision — but a scored sign is not a tactile sign. Signs that must
be read by touch, with raised letters or braille, follow accessibility
standards of their own, and we do not claim this one meets them.

## Reflect

Write a [[Newsroom Journal]] entry: what did your user tell you that
you would never have worked out alone, and where does it show on the
finished sign?

> [!success]- If your user is hard to pin down (click to expand)
> Pick a place, not a person, and then find who uses it: the library
> door, the studio, the room where Publication Day happens. Ask the
> first person you see there what they would need a sign to say. That
> person is your user.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 3, Day 7, the prototype test with the user
  Watch where the student stands when they test. Some walk to the spot
  the user will actually stand in, measure the distance, and ask the
  user to read it cold; some hold the cardboard at arm's length, glance
  at it themselves, and write "readable". The finished sign can look
  identical either way. Only the first has analysed the prototype's
  performance with a real quality check — A3.3 — and the test log is
  written afterwards, so it cannot tell you which happened.
  Going well: a tape measure on the floor and the user's own words
  written down.
  Stuck: the student is the only one who has read the sign.
  Record: a tick in one of two columns on your day plan — tested at
  distance / looked at it — as you walk the room.

TALK — Unit 3, Day 6, the checkpoint conference already on that agenda
  Their criteria are on the table in front of you; do not ask them to
  read them out. Ask:
  "Which of these could your prototype fail, and how exactly will you
  know at next class's test?"
  Then: "Why this material and not the other one — and what would make
  you change your mind?"
  A strong answer turns a criterion into a check — "she has to read it
  from the end of the hall, so I will stand her there" — and weighs the
  material against both the job and its offcuts. That is A1.5
  (criteria, justified, qualitative and quantitative) and A2.3
  (material chosen for its properties, sustainability included) heard
  rather than written.
  Record: one word per student on the conference list — "check" if the
  criterion became a test, a dash if not.

The product evidence is the sign cut on Day 8 or 9 and handed to its
user. That one arrives on its own.
