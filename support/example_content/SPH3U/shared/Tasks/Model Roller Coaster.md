> [!abstract] At a glance
> **Format:** a built track plus a four-page analysis. **Built in threes;
> the analysis is written and handed in by each of you separately.**
> **Build and test:** four classes, with a checkpoint on the third.
> **Analysis:** due the class after the final runs.
> **Assessed on:** Thinking and Investigation, Application, Communication.

Build a roller coaster out of foam pipe insulation, run a ball bearing
down it, and then account for every joule — where it started, where it
went, and where it ended up. The build is the fun part. The accounting is
the physics.

## The brief

Your track must:

1. start from a release point you can return to exactly;
2. include **at least three rises** after the first drop;
3. include **one vertical loop**;
4. keep the ball on the track from release to the end, with no pushing;
5. bring the ball to a stop in a catch at the end, not on the floor.

Length, shape, and theatre are yours. A coaster that barely completes is
more interesting than one that thunders through, because the interesting
physics lives near the margins.

## Materials

Foam pipe insulation split lengthwise makes a channel; a ball bearing
rolls in it well. Tape, card, and retort stands hold it up. One ball
bearing per group, and it stays in the room.

## Safety

> [!warning] The two real hazards here
> A ball bearing that leaves the track at speed is a projectile — the end
> catch is not optional, and nobody stands at the open end. Tracks taped
> above head height come down at the end of every class; nothing is left
> hanging overnight.

## The physics you are being asked to use

At the release point the ball has [[Gravitational Potential Energy]] and
nothing else. Everywhere after that:

$$mgh_{release} = mgh + \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 + E_{lost}$$

The last term is the one that makes this a real investigation. In a
frictionless world it is zero and the ball would return to its release
height forever. In your world it is not.

### The loop has a minimum speed

At the top of a vertical loop the ball must be moving fast enough that the
track does not have to pull it inward — gravity alone must be enough to
bend its path. That sets a minimum:

$$v_{top}^2 \ge gr$$

Feed that into conservation of energy and you get the famous result that a
frictionless loop needs a release height of at least $2.5r$ above the
bottom of the loop. Your loop will need MORE than that, and how much more
is one of the questions below.

## What to measure

| Where | What | How |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Release point | Height above the bench | Metre stick |
| Top of the loop | Height, loop radius | Metre stick |
| Two checkpoints | Speed | Video, frame by frame |
| End | Height where it stops | Metre stick |

Use [[Analysing Video of Motion]] for the speeds. Three runs at each
measurement, and report the spread, not just the mean — see
[[Uncertainty and Error]].

## The analysis (this is what is marked)

1. **The energy table.** For each measured point: height, speed,
   $E_g$, $E_k$, total, and energy lost since release. Show one full
   calculation and put the rest in the table.
2. **The loop.** Compute the minimum release height your loop requires in
   theory. Compare with the release height that actually worked. Explain
   the difference in physics, not in apology.
3. **Where the energy went.** Quantify what you can (friction along the
   track, the fact that a rolling ball carries energy in its spin as well
   as its motion) and name what you cannot.
4. **The efficiency of one section.** Choose the section you are proudest
   of and report its efficiency as
   $E_{out}/E_{in} \times 100\%$ — see [[Efficiency and Power]].
5. **One change, tested.** Change one thing (loop radius, track material,
   ball mass), predict the effect on the release height needed, then test
   it. Report the prediction BEFORE the result, even if the prediction
   was wrong. Especially then.

The track is built by three people and the measurements are shared —
somebody has to hold the metre stick. The analysis is not shared. You
decide what your table shows, you do the loop calculation, and you write
the account of where the energy went.

## How it is marked

| What is judged | What it looks like in your analysis |
| --- | --- |
| The energy table | Complete for every measured point, with one calculation shown in full and the rest tabulated |
| The loop | Theoretical minimum release height computed from YOUR loop's radius, and compared with the height that actually worked |
| The losses | Named by the form the energy ended up in, quantified where you can, and honestly listed where you cannot |
| The efficiency | One named section, with $E_{out}$ and $E_{in}$ both identified — not a whole-track number with no boundary |
| The tested change | Prediction written down before the result, and the result reported either way |
| Communication | A reader who never saw your track could rebuild the argument from your table and your one worked calculation |

## What good work looks like

A group whose coaster failed twice and who can tell me exactly how much
energy each version lost, and why, will out-score a group whose coaster
worked first time and whose analysis says "some energy was lost due to
friction". The number is the point.

> [!note] For teachers
> The build works with any rolling mass, but a steel ball bearing in split
> foam insulation is the most forgiving combination — heavy enough that
> air resistance is negligible, small enough that the channel guides it.
> Budget three build classes and a day of final runs: groups that get one
> class build a ramp, groups that get three build a coaster. The
> checkpoint on the third build day is what stops a group arriving at the
> final runs with a track that works and no numbers.
> The rolling-energy point in question 3 is
> deliberately left for students to discover as a discrepancy; SPH4U picks
> it up properly.
