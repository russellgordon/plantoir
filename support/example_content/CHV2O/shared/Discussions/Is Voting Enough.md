**The question:** if every eligible person in Canada voted in every
election, would that be enough — or is voting the smallest thing a
citizen does, dressed up as the largest?

Turnout in Canadian federal elections has run well below full
participation for decades, and it is consistently lowest among the
youngest voters. Both facts are published by Elections Canada, and both
get used to support arguments that point in opposite directions.

## Prepare

Bring three things: one action you have taken or seen taken that was not
voting; one issue where voting could not have delivered the outcome you
wanted; and a figure from Elections Canada, with the year attached.

Read [[How Change Actually Happens]] first, and come with a view you
would be willing to change.

> [!success]- Try this before the seminar
> Name one decision made in the past year that affected you, and say
> honestly whether any vote you could have cast would have changed it.
> Most people find the answer is no — and then have to decide whether
> that is an argument against voting or an argument for everything else.

## Positions that genuinely conflict

**Voting is the only participation that is equal.** Everyone gets one,
regardless of money, time, articulacy, connections or confidence. Every
other route — deputations, campaigns, litigation, lobbying — rewards
people who already have resources. Low turnout therefore does not mean
voting is weak; it means the one equal instrument is being left unused.

**Voting is too blunt to express anything.** You choose one candidate,
once, and thereby endorse an entire platform you probably disagree with
in part. A ballot cannot say which part. On any single issue, five
minutes at a council meeting communicates more than a vote ever could.

**Between elections is when things are decided.** Most decisions
affecting you are made by ministries, boards, agencies and staff who face
no election at all. A citizen whose participation is entirely electoral
is absent for almost all of the governing.

**Not everyone can vote, and they still live here.** Permanent residents,
people under eighteen, and many others are governed without a ballot. If
voting were sufficient, their civic standing would be nil — which most
people, on reflection, do not accept.

**Turnout is a symptom, not the disease.** People vote when they believe
it changes something. Arguing that non-voters are lazy skips the question
of what they have observed.

## Push it further

If voting is not enough, is it still obligatory? Some democracies make it
compulsory; Canada does not. And is a citizen who never votes but runs a
food bank for ten years participating more or less than one who votes
every time and does nothing else?

Close by writing, in one sentence, what you now think citizenship
minimally requires — and what changed it, if anything did. That sentence
belongs in [[Where You Stand Now]].
