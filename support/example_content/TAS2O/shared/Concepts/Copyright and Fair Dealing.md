The first time you needed a photo you had not taken — a stock crowd
shot, a team logo, a song under a highlight reel — you met the question
this page answers: who owns creative work, and what are the rest of us
allowed to do with it?

## Who owns what

In Canada, copyright is automatic. The moment a photograph is taken or
a paragraph written, its maker owns it — no registration, no © symbol
required. That cuts both ways: the picture you found online belongs to
somebody, and the picture you made at Thursday's game belongs to you.
"It was on the Internet" is a description of where you found it, not
permission to republish it.

## Fair dealing, and its limit

Canadian law allows *fair dealing*: limited use of copyrighted work
without permission for purposes including research, private study,
education, criticism, review, and news reporting — provided the use is
genuinely fair (a short excerpt, credited, for that purpose). Analysing
a news photo inside our classroom leans on the education purpose. But
the moment work goes public via [[Publish to the Web]], the comfortable
classroom umbrella mostly stays behind. Hence the newsroom rule: we
publish only what we made, what is licensed for our use, or what we
have permission for.

## Licences in plain terms

A licence is standing permission from the owner, with conditions:

| The label | In plain terms |
| --- | --- |
| © All rights reserved | Ask first; no answer means no |
| CC BY | Use it, credit the maker |
| CC BY-NC | Credit the maker; nothing commercial |
| Public domain / CC0 | Free to use; credit is still good manners |

The letters after "CC" are the conditions, so read all of them — the
sorting gets quick with practice, which is what
[[Copyright Scenarios Practice]] is for.

## Credit where due — including the machines

Crediting is its own obligation: name the maker, the source, and the
licence, in the credit line or caption. That includes new kinds of
help — if AI tools contributed to research, drafting, or an image, we
say so, the same way we would credit any collaborator. How we disclose
it is written into [[Our Newsroom Standards]]; as these tools spread
through working newsrooms, honest labelling is becoming part of what
"professional" means.
