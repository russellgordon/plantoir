**The culminating task. Nine rehearsal periods in Unit 4, performed for
an invited audience.** Groups of four to six. Three to four minutes.

A finished group work, made by you, run by you, shown to people who are
not in this class.

## The nine rehearsal days

| Day | What it is for |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Concept agreed, and the material you already have gathered |
| 2 | Generating: everyone brings movement |
| 3 | Structuring: the shape of the whole piece |
| 4 | First run, however rough — a walking version end to end |
| 5 | Stagecraft: sound, lighting states, costume, spacing, entrances and exits — each element signed by the dancer who owns it |
| 6 | Feedback showing to another group |
| 7 | Revision: the changes you agreed, put in |
| 8 | Technical rehearsal in the performance space |
| 9 | Dress rehearsal |

The performance itself is the class after that.

## What you deliver

- **The work itself**, performed as rehearsed and full-out.
- **Programme notes** (150 words) your audience reads: what it is, what
  you were working from, and who made what — **each of you writing your
  own line of the who-made-what**, drafted on day 8.
- **A stagecraft plan** — sound cues, lighting states, entrances and
  exits, marked on a plan of the space, with **every element signed by
  somebody and every one of you signing something**, on day 5.

## What is marked

The work is the group's; the mark is yours. Five things are judged as
yours: **your own dancing** in the performance and in the recording we
watch afterwards; the **set sequence from Unit 4, Days 1 and 2**, run
together for a note and then danced from memory, one at a time, on Unit
4, Day 16; **your line of the programme notes**; the **element of the
stagecraft plan you signed**; and **how you work in the rehearsal room**,
which is expectation C3.2 and is watched across the rehearsal days rather
than on performance night. A group that carries you cannot lift your
mark, and a group that falls apart cannot lower it.

| Criterion | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Reproduction | You dance what was set — the group's work, and the set sequence taught in Unit 4, Days 1 and 2 — accurately and from memory |
| Refinement | The final version is visibly better than the walking version on day 4 |
| Stagecraft | The element you signed for serves the work, and you can say what it is doing |
| Programme notes | Your line of the notes says what you made, in words an audience who was not there can use, without explaining the piece away |
| Projection | Mood reaches the back row — eyes, face, breath, energy |
| Collaboration | You bring material to rehearsal, you say what you think when the group disagrees, and you carry out what the group agrees. Marked from the rehearsal days, not from the performance |

> [!tip] The audience is the point
> A studio showing is a rehearsal with witnesses. A performance is for
> people who did not watch it being made and will read only what your
> bodies tell them. Everything on this page follows from that
> difference.

production, and [[Projecting Mood]] the performing.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

OBSERVE — Unit 4, Day 8, day 4 of the nine, the walking version end to end
  Watch for: what the group does the first time the run breaks. Who stops
  it; whether they go back to the top every time or pick up from the
  break; whether the dancer whose section broke is the one who gets to say
  what to do about it. The performance will not break — which is exactly
  why the Collaboration row above cannot be marked from it. C3.2 covers
  both processes; the rehearsal half is the one a performance cannot show
  you.
  Going well: somebody says "from the unison" and five people go to the
  unison.
  Stuck: three restarts from the top, and the same two dancers deciding
  every time.
  Record: group names down the side of the day plan, initials against
  "brought", "proposed", "carried out".

TALK — Unit 4, Day 9, day 5 of the nine, while the stagecraft plan is
being written and signed
  Ask: "Where is the audience going to look first — and is that where you
  want them?"
  Then: "If the lights could only do one thing for the whole piece, which
  state would you keep — and what does the piece lose?"
  A strong answer names the device — a light state, a sound entering, one
  dancer moving while five hold still — and can say what it is for. That
  is A4.2 heard: a stagecraft plan on paper shows you the decisions and
  never whether the group knows what they are for. Ask it beside the
  element each dancer signed.
  Record: one line per dancer, written against the element they own.

The product evidence is the performance on Day 14, the programme notes
handed out there, and the set sequence danced from memory on Day 16.
