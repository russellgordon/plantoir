Ontario sets the structure and it is the same in every course you take:
**seventy per cent** of the final grade comes from work across the term,
and **thirty per cent** from a final demonstration of learning near the
end. In this course that thirty is split between the culminating task and
the final evaluation.

## Where the marks come from

| Component | Share | What it is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Term work | 70% | The six tasks in Units 1 to 3, and [[The Field and Map Portfolio]] |
| [[The Local Hazard Assessment]] | 15% | The culminating task, delivered to an outside audience |
| [[The Data Examination]] | 15% | The three-hour final evaluation |

Every one of those has a page that says, before the work starts, what
earns the marks in it. Nothing else on this site carries a mark.

Within the term work, the most recent and most consistent evidence counts
most. A skill you could not do at the start of the course and can do at the
end of the course is assessed on the most recent evidence, not on the
average of the two. It is also why some of the course's central skills are
assessed twice: choosing and organising sources, synthesising evidence into
a judgement, and assessing whether a protective measure works are each
marked once in Units 1 to 3 and again in the culminating task. The later
reading is the one that counts for more.

## The four categories

Every task on this site is marked against the same four, and each task
page says what earns the marks in each.

**Knowledge and Understanding** — do you know the physical processes, and
can you explain rather than name them?
**Thinking** — can you interpret evidence, judge a source, and reach a
conclusion that carries its uncertainty?
**Application** — can you use the concepts and the data on a real place?
**Communication** — can you write, map and speak to the audience in front
of you?

## Three kinds of evidence

Products are the obvious kind, and they are not the only kind. Ontario
asks for evidence from three sources, and this course is built so that
all three actually exist.

**What you make.** The study, the comparison, the audit, the argument,
the maps, the paper. It arrives, it holds still, and it can be marked at
a desk on a Sunday.

**What I watch you do.** How a profile gets surveyed, whether a group
turns its folder round and reads its own criteria, whose hands are on the
municipal emergency plan. Some of that is invisible in the finished work:
a clean profile looks the same whether it was measured from one datum or
from three.

**What you say.** The checkpoint conference, the answer you give when a
figure is questioned, the sentence in which you explain why you would not
use one of two sources. If you can do something you have not yet managed
to get onto paper, say so at a conference — that conversation is
evidence, and it is usually the fastest way to show me something a draft
has not caught up with.

The last two are not marks for being keen. They are the same geography as
the product, gathered where the product cannot show it.

## What never moves the percentage

**Your judgement of your own work, or a classmate's.** Evaluation is my
job, and Ontario says so plainly. [[Judging Your Own Work]] and the peer
readings on the schedule change what you hand in; they never become part
of what it is worth.

**A shared group mark.** There is not one anywhere in this course. Every
paired and group task names the part that is yours: the two components
you sign for on [[The Preparedness Audit]], your half of the mechanism on
[[The Sphere Connections]], your own written position and your own answer
from the floor in [[The Land Use Question]], your named section and your
own rehearsal in [[The Local Hazard Assessment]].

**Homework.** Reading, drafting and practice are how you get ready, and
they are never the thing being marked. That is why the marked parts of
[[The Field and Map Portfolio]] are written in class. A "things to do
before our next class" list may remind you to bring an assignment in; the
reminder is not the assignment.

**Learning skills and work habits.** Responsibility, Organization,
Independent Work, Collaboration, Initiative and Self-regulation are
reported separately on your report card, as **E, G, S or N**. They do not
move the percentage in either direction. Ontario allows one narrow
exception, for a curriculum expectation that is itself about a habit, and
this course does not use it — read the criteria table on any task page
here and every row describes the work rather than the worker.

## Feedback before marks

Every task in Units 1 to 4 has a checkpoint on the schedule before it is
due, and after every checkpoint there is class time whose stated job is
acting on what it found. How much varies, and the day it falls on says
so: a whole period for [[The Stewardship Argument]], half a period for
[[The Process Study]] and [[The Preparedness Audit]], the second half of
the peer-challenge period for [[The Local Hazard Assessment]], the first
twenty minutes for [[The Disaster Case]] and for the revised position in
[[The Land Use Question]], and fifteen minutes for
[[The Sphere Connections]] and for the portfolio repair.

Bring something real to a checkpoint even if it is rough. A blank page
gets you nothing back, and the time afterwards then gets spent starting
rather than improving. The final evaluation is the one exception to all
of this: its preparation is the three review periods at the end of the
course.

## Late, missing, and honest

Talk to me before the due date and something reasonable happens. Talk to
me after, and something reasonable usually still happens, but with less
room to work with — the cost of being late here is lost feedback and lost
working time, not a deduction. Work that is not submitted at all cannot
be assessed, and an unassessed expectation is the only thing here that
genuinely cannot be fixed later.

Academic honesty is covered in [[Our Working Agreement]]; the short
version is that unattributed work is not yours and cannot be marked as
though it were.

If any of this is unclear, ask — [[Getting Help]].
