One minute, one current technology story, and the three questions you
already know: what is being *claimed*, what is actually *known*, and
who *benefits* if you believe it. This year a fourth joins them,
because you now know enough to ask it — **who has to maintain this,
and for how long?** Almost nothing in technology news is priced with
that included, and it is where most of the real cost lives.

## How to run it

1. One student brings the story: the headline and two sentences of
   summary, no more. The job rotates, so everyone takes a turn.
2. The room answers the four questions in order. Keep *claimed* and
   *known* strictly apart — that boundary is the whole skill.
3. Park anything worth a longer argument on the board. These
   headlines are where [[Should It Exist]] and [[When Code Hurts]]
   get their material.

## A worked round

A story reports that an organisation has automated a decision that
people used to make by hand — screening applications, flagging
accounts, routing requests. The pattern repeats every few weeks with
the nouns swapped. Sorting it takes about ninety seconds:

| Question | What a careful reader writes down |
| --- | --- |
| Claimed | It is faster, cheaper, and "removes human bias" |
| Known | It is faster. The rest is the vendor's wording, repeated |
| Who benefits | Whoever sold it, and whoever no longer has to justify a decision in person |
| Who maintains it | Unstated — and when the rules change, somebody has to find the code and edit it |

The last two rows are the ones that reward Grade 12. A decision that
used to be made by a person could be appealed to that person. A
decision made by a program can only be appealed to whoever still
understands the program, which is the question [[Who Maintains This]]
spends a whole period on.

## One variation

Same story, two sources: read an organisation's own announcement
beside a news report about it, and list what each one leaves out. Do
it again a month later, when the follow-up coverage arrives. The gap
between the launch story and the follow-up is the most honest
material this warm-up produces.

> [!tip] "We do not know yet" is a finding
> The strongest sentence said in this warm-up is often that the
> honest answer is not known — the evidence is thin, the study was
> small, the demo was staged, the benchmark was chosen by the people
> being benchmarked. Practise saying it plainly. It is the same
> standard [[Ethics, Security, and the Profession]] holds every claim
> to, including the ones your own team will be tempted to make about
> your project at the handover.
