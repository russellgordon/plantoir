## What you are making

A **build sheet**: the complete parts list for one workstation,
specced for a real client with real needs and a hard budget. Your
pair draws a client from the menu — a video editor on a budget, a
grandparent who video-calls family overseas, a small café that needs
a till, a student musician recording in a bedroom — and every line
names a part, its key numbers, and one sentence tying it to this
client.

Nothing gets assembled here — that is what [[Build a Workstation]] is
for. This task is the thinking a technician does before a single screw
turns, and reading the numbers honestly is the whole game:
[[Reading a Spec Sheet]] and [[Spec Sheet Practice]] are your allies.

## How to work

1. Draw your client. Write down three things the profile does not tell
   you, and ask them — another bench plays your client and answers in
   character. A part chosen before the questions are asked is a guess.
2. Set the budget envelope with your client, and write down what going
   over it would actually cost them.
3. Choose every part, line by line: processor, memory, storage,
   display, input, and the ports that connect them.
   [[The CPU and Memory]], [[Storage and Drives]], and
   [[Peripherals and Ports]] are the background reading.
4. Check the whole against its parts: does everything connect, and does
   the operating system you intend actually run on this hardware? Its
   published requirements are the test, not your optimism.
5. Find your sources yourself — manuals, spec sheets, support pages —
   and cite every number. [[Finding Answers Online]] shows the way.
6. Client meeting: another bench, acting as your client, reads the
   sheet and pushes back. Revise, and record what changed and why.

Every one of those periods is class time, and that is on purpose: work
that carries a mark gets done here, where I can watch it happen and you
can ask. In the third bench period, Day 15, I read every pair's draft
against the criteria table below and leave one written note; Day 16
opens with fifteen minutes for acting on it, before anything else.
Bring the note back with the sheet.

## Your own mark

The job is a pair job. The mark is not — a shared mark would tell
neither of you anything. Two things are yours alone:

- **The lines you researched.** Your initials go beside them, and at
  the client meeting you answer for those lines yourself. Where both
  sets of initials sit on a line, either of you may be asked.
- **Your own journal entry**, written at the bench in the last minutes
  of the period the sheet is finished in.

## Success criteria

| Quality | What it looks like on your sheet |
| --- | --- |
| Fit to client | Every justification names this client, not "a user" |
| Budget honesty | The total is shown; any overage is argued, not hidden |
| Precise terminology | Parts named by type, capacity, speed, connector |
| A coherent whole | Parts connect and meet the system requirements |
| Independent sources | Every number on the sheet has a stated source |
| A revised sheet | The client meeting visibly changed something |
| Lines you can answer for | Your initials beside the lines you researched, defended by you |

## Reflect

Your [[Tech Journal]] entry — written in class, at the bench, before
tools away — answers one question: which part fought hardest for its
place on the sheet, and what finally decided it? A sheet where every
choice was easy was not listening to its client.

> [!success]- If the choices feel overwhelming (click to expand)
> Ignore the parts and describe the client's Tuesday. What do they
> open, save, and wait for? Start from the one wait that annoys them
> most, and the first part chooses itself.

Triangulation — the evidence you will not have unless you go and get it.

This one is done in pairs, so the conversation is also where the
INDIVIDUAL evidence comes from. Ask each partner on their own, out of
the other's hearing, and write down two names, not one.

OBSERVE — Unit 1, Day 16, the compatibility-check period
  Watch for: what a pair does when a number they have just read
  contradicts a part they chose an hour ago. The handed-in sheet is
  coherent either way — either because a part was changed, or because
  the claim was quietly loosened until it fitted. Only the period shows
  you which.
  Going well: somebody says "then the memory is wrong" and goes back
  and changes a line that was already finished.
  Stuck: the new number gets called "close enough", the pair moves on,
  and nothing above it moves.
  Record: one column on the seating plan, a tick for each pair you see
  reopen a finished line.
  This corroborates B1.2. The sheet shows that requirements were met;
  the period shows whether they were understood well enough to overrule
  a decision the pair had already made.

TALK — Unit 1, Day 15, the per-pair round the agenda already schedules
  Ask, of each partner separately: "Point at any line and tell me where
  that number came from. If the maker's page and the shop's page
  disagreed, which one goes on the sheet?"
  Then: "Your client has just lost a fifth of the budget. Which line
  goes first, and what can they no longer do?"
  A strong answer names the manufacturer's own document and says why it
  outranks a retailer's summary — that is B4.3 heard aloud, and a cited
  sheet cannot tell you whether the student knows why the citation is
  the better one. On the second, a strong answer names a line and a
  consequence for this client; a weak one reaches for whatever is
  cheapest.
  Record: two ticks per STUDENT in the seating-plan margin — one for
  the source, one for the trade-off.

The product evidence is the sheet handed in on Day 17. That one arrives
on its own.
